*By Cal Newport*

**The big idea:** Deep work is focused, distraction-free work on hard problems. It's becoming rare just as it's becoming more valuable. People who can do it will thrive.

## Key ideas

**Deep versus shallow.** Shallow work is email, admin and errands: tasks you can do half-distracted. Deep work is strategy, writing and problem-solving that pushes your skills to the limit. Shallow work is easy to copy or automate. Deep work isn't.

**Busy isn't productive.** Many people act like human email routers, checking constantly. Big thinking, like a new strategy, gets broken into distracted bursts and the quality suffers.

**Pick a deep work rhythm.** Some people disappear for days (monastic). Some split weeks into deep and shallow days (bimodal). Most do best with a fixed daily block (rhythmic), like 8 to 10am every day.

**Make rituals.** Decide where you'll work, for how long and with what rules (phone in another room, no email). Rituals cut the willpower needed to start.

**Embrace boredom.** If you reach for your phone every time you're bored, your brain loses the ability to focus. Practise waiting.

**Schedule shallow work.** Batch email and admin into set times so they don't eat the day.

## Use it with clients

- Owners rarely get time to think about strategy because the day fills with shallow work. Help them protect one deep work block each week to work on the business.
- Look at how often the owner checks email and messages. Cutting it to set times is often an instant win.

## Try it yourself

Block 90 minutes tomorrow morning for deep work. Phone in another room, email closed. Pick one hard task and work only on that.

by: Inspired by Deep Work by Cal Newport

*Inspired by Deep Work by Cal Newport*

**The big idea:** High-quality work = time spent x intensity of focus. Raise your intensity and you can produce more in less time.

## Key ideas

**Attention residue.** Every time you switch tasks or glance at email, part of your attention stays stuck on what you left. That residue builds up during a half-distracted day and lowers your focus.

**Small checks cost a lot.** A quick look at your inbox every ten minutes feels harmless. It's not. Each check adds new residue and stretches the time it takes to do quality work.

**Design a focus space.** Intensity rises when distraction is impossible: phone on airplane mode, no internet if you can manage it, no TV, no snacks, no nearby chatter.

**Even ignored distractions cost focus.** A phone face-down on your desk still takes some attention. Remove it from the room.

**Short and intense beats long and scattered.** Two focused hours often beat a distracted day.

## Use it with clients

- Owners often work long, scattered days. Show them the formula and help them design one high-intensity block a day.
- An open-plan office or constant interruptions kill intensity. Help owners set "do not disturb" times for themselves and their team.

## Try it yourself

For your next important task, remove every distraction from the room and work for 60 minutes. Compare the output to a normal hour.

*By Nir Eyal*

**The big idea:** Distraction starts inside us. Boredom, stress and anxiety make us reach for relief. Learn to handle discomfort and you can handle distraction.

## Key ideas

**Discomfort drives distraction.** Boredom makes us check our phones. Social anxiety makes us say yes to meetings. Stress makes us overeat.

**Surf the urge.** Smokers' cravings dropped after a smoke break even if they didn't smoke. Urges rise, peak and fade on their own. Picture riding the wave until it passes.

**Make time for traction.** Traction is anything that moves you towards your goals. Distraction pulls you away. You can only call something a distraction if you know what you planned to do instead. Plan your day in time blocks.

**Hack back external triggers.** Turn off notifications, remove apps and set office hours for interruptions. Ask of each trigger: "Is this serving me, or am I serving it?"

**Use pacts.** Commit in advance with effort pacts (add friction, like website blockers), price pacts (pay a penalty) or identity pacts ("I'm indistractable").

## Use it with clients

- Owners often blame phones, staff or clients for distraction. Start with the inside: what feeling makes them reach for email?
- Help owners timebox their week, including time for family and rest. Then distractions are easy to spot.

## Try it yourself

Plan tomorrow in time blocks. When you feel the urge to check your phone, wait ten minutes and surf the urge.

*By Cal Newport*

**The big idea:** Use only the technology that supports what you value most. Cut the rest. Your attention is too precious to give away to apps built to grab it.

## Key ideas

**Treat tech like food.** Value-adding tools are whole foods. Mindless games and feeds are junk food. Check your digital diet.

**Concentration is a superpower.** Losing focus is like a top athlete losing their best skill. Constant tech clutter leaves part of your mind always elsewhere.

**Do a 30-day declutter.**
1. Take a break from optional tech for 30 days.
2. Fill the time with real activities: walks, reading, hobbies, conversations.
3. Bring back only the tools that clearly support your values, with rules for when and how you use them.

**Spend time alone with your thoughts.** Solitude helps you think clearly and solve problems. Take walks without headphones.

**Choose real connection.** A phone call or a coffee beats a like or a comment.

## Use it with clients

- Many owners are always "on" through phones, Slack and email. Help them set rules, like no email before 8am or after 7pm.
- Suggest a short tech declutter during a quieter month to reset habits.

## Try it yourself

Delete one app from your phone that doesn't serve your values. Take a 20-minute walk this week with no headphones.

*By David Rock*

**The big idea:** Your thinking brain is like a small stage that tires quickly. Know how it works and you can plan your day to think more clearly.

## Key ideas

**The stage is small.** Your working memory, controlled by the front of your brain, can only hold a few things at once. Each thought is an actor on the stage. Too many actors and the show falls apart.

**Five functions:** understanding, recalling, memorising, inhibiting (holding back distractions) and deciding. Each uses energy, and the energy runs out.

**Do hard thinking first.** Prioritising is one of the most draining tasks. Do it when your brain is fresh, usually early in the day.

**Get things off the stage.** Write tasks and ideas down so your brain doesn't have to hold them.

**Single-task.** Multitasking forces actors on and off the stage and drains energy fast.

**Use the director.** Noticing what your brain is doing, like "I'm getting distracted", gives you the power to change it.

**Feel safe.** Threats like unfairness or loss of status make the brain defensive and hurt thinking. Certainty, autonomy and fairness help it work well.

## Use it with clients

- Help owners plan their day around brain energy: decisions and priorities first, routine tasks later.
- When teams struggle, look for threats like unclear roles, unfair treatment or no say in decisions. These quietly hurt performance.

## Try it yourself

Tomorrow, set your priorities in the first 15 minutes of the day and write everything else down so it's off your stage.

*By Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi*

**The big idea:** Flow is the state of being so absorbed in a task that you lose track of time. It sits between boredom and anxiety, and it's where you perform and feel your best.

## Key ideas

**Four ingredients for flow at work:**

**1. Focus.** Flow needs full attention. Start each task with a short focus warm-up, like a minute of breathing or music, then aim that focus at the work.

**2. Feedback.** Know quickly whether you're doing well. Set up ways to see progress as you go, like word counts, completed items or responses.

**3. Fit.** The challenge must match your skill. Too easy and you're bored. Too hard and you're anxious. Stretch slightly beyond your current level.

**4. Freedom.** Remove interruptions and give yourself space to work without worrying about what others think.

**Flow makes life better.** People report their happiest moments not when relaxing but when stretched by a worthwhile challenge.

## Use it with clients

- Help owners spot when they're in flow. Often it's their highest-value work, and it should get more time.
- Help owners design team roles with the right level of challenge and clear feedback.

## Try it yourself

Pick a task for tomorrow that stretches you slightly. Set a clear goal, a way to see progress, and a 90-minute block with no interruptions.

*By Steven Kotler*

**The big idea:** Extreme sports athletes keep breaking limits because they've mastered flow. Anyone can learn to trigger flow and use it to perform and learn faster.

## Key ideas

**Flow is peak performance.** In flow, each action leads smoothly to the next. Everything you do, you do better in flow.

**Flow has triggers.** You can set the conditions:
- **Clear goals:** know exactly what you're doing next.
- **Immediate feedback:** see how you're doing as you go.
- **Challenge-skill balance:** stretch just past your comfort zone.
- **Deep focus:** remove distractions.
- **Novelty and risk:** something new or at stake sharpens attention.

**Flow follows a cycle.** Struggle (hard work loading information), release (step away and relax), flow, then recovery. Don't skip the struggle or the recovery.

**Flow speeds up learning.** People learn and improve far faster when they work in flow.

## Use it with clients

- Owners often stay in the struggle phase without stepping back. Encourage a walk or a break after hard thinking work. Breakthroughs often come next.
- Help owners set clear goals and fast feedback for key projects.

## Try it yourself

After your next stretch of hard work, take a 20-minute walk before deciding anything. Notice what ideas come up.

*Inspired by The Rise of Superman by Steven Kotler*

**The big idea:** Your attention is fully engaged when a task is just hard enough to stretch you but not snap you. Aim for about 4% beyond your current ability.

## Key ideas

**Too hard causes anxiety. Too easy causes boredom.** Flow sits in the channel between the two.

**Adventure athletes stay in the zone.** Big wave surfers can't take on a wave 50% bigger than they've ever ridden. It would crush them. So they push a little beyond their limit, again and again. That's why they improve so fast.

**Small stretches compound.** Each 4% stretch becomes your new normal. Keep stretching and your skill grows quickly.

**Adjust the challenge.** If you're bored, add a constraint, like a deadline or higher standard. If you're anxious, break the task into smaller pieces.

## Use it with clients

- Owners often set goals that are either far too big (anxiety) or too comfortable (boredom). Help them find the stretch that's just beyond their current level.
- Use the same idea for team development: stretch assignments slightly beyond someone's current ability.

## Try it yourself

Look at today's main task. If it feels boring, add a tighter deadline. If it feels overwhelming, cut it into smaller steps.

*By Steven Pressfield*

**The big idea:** An inner force called Resistance stops you doing your most important creative work. Beat it by turning pro: show up every day, no matter what.

## Key ideas

**Resistance is the enemy.** "Most of us have two lives: the life we live, and the unlived life within us. Between the two stands Resistance." It fills your head with doubt and pulls you towards comfort.

**Resistance looks like this:** "You have nothing to say." "You're not talented enough." "You've got too much to lose." An extra drink, a lie-in, a long lunch.

**The more important the work, the stronger the Resistance.** Use it as a compass. The task you fear most is often the one you most need to do.

**Turn pro.** Amateurs wait for inspiration. Professionals show up every day, stay on the job and keep going after setbacks. They treat their craft like a job.

**Inspiration follows action.** When you sit down to work every day, ideas start turning up.

## Use it with clients

- When an owner keeps putting off a big move, like a price rise, a new offer or a key hire, name it as Resistance. Naming it takes some of its power away.
- Help owners book recurring time for the important work they avoid, and treat it like a client appointment.

## Try it yourself

Name the task you're resisting most. Book 30 minutes a day for it this week and show up like a pro, inspired or not.

*By Seth Godin*

**The big idea:** Creative work is the generous act of making things better by trying something that might not work. Commit to the practice, not the outcome.

## Key ideas

**Choose your path.** Every day you can be creative or fall into one of three traps:
- **Hack:** copies what works without improving it.
- **Hustler:** chases short-term targets over value.
- **Cog:** follows instructions and never improves the system.

**Expect to feel like an imposter.** Doing something new means not knowing exactly what you're doing. That feeling is a sign you're doing real work.

**Trust the process.** You can't control the outcome. You can control showing up, shipping work and learning from it.

**Ship it.** Finish and share your work regularly. Feedback only comes once it's out in the world.

**Serve the smallest viable audience.** Make work for a specific group of people who care. You don't need everyone.

## Use it with clients

- Owners often wait until something is perfect before launching. Help them ship small versions and improve from feedback.
- When an owner feels like a fraud stepping into a new role, reframe it as a sign of growth.

## Try it yourself

Pick one piece of work you've been polishing. Ship it this week to a small group and ask for feedback.

*By Peter Hollins*

**The big idea:** Finishing needs four "follow-through muscles": focus, action, resisting distraction and persistence. When early excitement fades, those muscles do the work.

## Key ideas

**Inspiration fades.** At the start of a project you're full of energy. Later, the shine wears off and follow-through gets hard.

**Perfectionism is a roadblock.** Your ego raises the bar for "finished" so you can avoid the risk of failing. The project stalls.

**Finishing is learning.** You only learn certain lessons by seeing a project through to the end. Half-finished work teaches you little.

**Set a clear finish line.** Define what done looks like before you start.

**Make a plan, then follow it.** Write down the steps and deadlines. Use daily rules like "no email until I've done one hour on the project."

**Avoid the shiny new thing.** New ideas will tempt you mid-project. Write them down and come back later.

## Use it with clients

- Owners often start many initiatives and finish few. Agree a finish line and a short step plan for each one, and limit how many run at once.
- Keep a "parking lot" for new ideas so they don't derail current projects.

## Try it yourself

Pick one unfinished project. Write what done looks like and the next three steps. Finish one step today.

<!-- lesson
id: books-process-vs-product
title: Principle: Process vs Product
by: Inspired by A Mind for Numbers by Barbara Oakley
-->

*Inspired by A Mind for Numbers by Barbara Oakley*

**The big idea:** Focusing on the product (the finished result) feeds procrastination. Focusing on the process (time spent working) beats it.

## Key ideas

**Product focus triggers avoidance.** "Finish the report" feels big and painful, so your brain looks for escape.

**Process focus feels manageable.** "Work on the report for 25 minutes" is easy to start. You stop judging progress by results and judge it by showing up.

**Habits do the work.** Thinking about results uses the energy-hungry, rational part of your brain. Following a routine uses the habit part of your brain, which runs with little effort. Build routines and let them carry you.

**Use the Pomodoro technique.** Set a timer for 25 minutes, work on one thing, then take a five-minute break. Repeat.

**Reward the process.** Give yourself a small reward after each focused session, not just when the project is done.

## Use it with clients

- When an owner is stuck on a big task like a business plan, switch the goal from "finish it" to "two 25-minute sessions a day".
- Help teams measure activity (calls made, hours on key projects) alongside results.

## Try it yourself

Pick a task you're avoiding. Set a 25-minute timer and work only on that. Stop when the timer ends and reward yourself.

*Inspired by Smarter Faster Better by Charles Duhigg*

**The big idea:** Top performers constantly predict how things will unfold. Telling yourself stories about what will happen sharpens your focus and helps you perform.

## Key ideas

**Superstars tell stories.** MIT researchers studied a recruiting firm and found the top earners kept telling stories about what they'd seen. They threw out ideas in meetings and asked colleagues to help imagine how a conversation or pitch might go.

**Prediction builds focus.** Imagining what comes next makes you pay closer attention to what's happening. Your brain notices when reality differs from your prediction and learns from it.

**Stretch into the unfamiliar.** Top performers took on projects outside their usual area, where prediction was harder and they had to stay alert.

**Use it daily.** Before a meeting or call, picture how it will go. What will they ask? What might go wrong? How will you respond?

## Use it with clients

- Before a sales call or tough team conversation, ask the owner to walk you through how they expect it to go. It surfaces gaps and builds confidence.
- Encourage owners to share stories of recent wins and losses with their team. It helps everyone predict better.

## Try it yourself

Before your next important call, spend five minutes writing down how you expect it to go and what questions might come up. Afterwards, compare.

*By Hendrie Weisinger and JP Pawliw-Fry*

**The big idea:** Nobody performs better under pressure, not even "clutch" athletes. The best performers simply lose less. You can learn to reduce pressure's effect.

## Key ideas

**Stress and pressure are different.** Stress is too many demands. The goal is to reduce them. Pressure is a moment where the result matters. The goal is success. If you treat everything as a pressure moment, you're under pressure all the time.

**Pressure hurts everyone.** Your heart races, your thinking narrows and you make mistakes.

**Reduce the pressure.** Tell yourself "this is one of many opportunities" instead of "this is my only chance." Focus on the task, not the outcome.

**Prepare for it.** Practise under pressure-like conditions. Rehearse the moment in your head.

**Build your COTE of armour:**
- **Confidence:** remember past wins.
- **Optimism:** expect a good outcome.
- **Tenacity:** keep going after setbacks.
- **Enthusiasm:** enjoy the challenge.

## Use it with clients

- Owners often feel every pitch or month-end is make-or-break. Help them see it as one of many opportunities, which lowers the pressure and lifts performance.
- Rehearse big moments with owners: a key pitch, a bank meeting, a hard conversation.

## Try it yourself

Before your next big moment, tell yourself "This is one of many opportunities." Then focus on your first step, not the result.

*By Jim Afremow*

**The big idea:** Peak performance starts and ends with your state of mind. Sports psychologist Jim Afremow shows the mindsets Olympic champions use, and they work just as well at work.

## Key ideas

**Belief.** Champions go in expecting to win because they know they've trained harder than anyone. "Train like you're number two, but compete like you're number one."

**Focus on the present.** Champions focus on the next action, not the scoreboard. When your mind drifts to the result, bring it back to the process.

**Self-talk.** Champions coach themselves with short, positive cues like "smooth" or "attack". Swap "Don't mess up" for "Stay strong."

**Routines.** Pre-performance routines calm nerves and switch you into performance mode. The same warm-up, the same steps, every time.

**Bounce back.** Champions have a quick way to reset after a mistake, like a breath or a physical cue, so one error doesn't become three.

## Use it with clients

- Before big moments like pitches or key meetings, help owners build a simple routine they repeat each time.
- Help owners spot their self-talk under pressure and swap negative phrases for short, helpful cues.

## Try it yourself

Create a two-minute routine for before your next important call: a breath, a cue word and one thing to focus on. Use it every time.

*By George Mumford*

**The big idea:** "Pure performance" happens in a zone of relaxed concentration where effort seems to disappear. George Mumford taught Michael Jordan and Kobe Bryant to find it through mindfulness.

## Key ideas

**The zone feels effortless.** Shaun White described his best run as "completely focused, then slightly not caring." Bill Russell said the game seemed to slow down.

**Mindfulness opens the door.** Notice what's happening right now without judging it. A few minutes of daily practice trains your attention.

**Find the space between stimulus and response.** Mindfulness widens the gap between what happens and how you react, so you choose calmly.

**Concentration plus relaxation.** Too tense and you tighten up. Too relaxed and you lose focus. Aim for relaxed focus.

**Let go of the outcome.** When you stop worrying about results, you play more freely and perform better.

## Use it with clients

- Owners who tense up in big moments benefit from a few minutes of daily mindfulness. It helps them stay calm and respond rather than react.
- Teach the idea of relaxed focus for pitches and negotiations.

## Try it yourself

Before your next important task, spend two minutes noticing your breath. Then work with relaxed focus, letting go of the result.

*By Jason Selk*

**The big idea:** A ten-minute daily mental workout, used by elite athletes and executives, builds confidence and focus. Try it for two weeks and judge for yourself.

## Key ideas

**1. Centering breath.** Breathe in for six seconds, hold for two, out for seven. Use it before and during pressure moments to control your heart rate and think clearly.

**2. Identity statement.** A short line about who you are and how you perform: "I'm the calmest person in the room and I close." Repeat it daily.

**3. Personal highlight reel.** Picture yourself performing at your best in an upcoming situation, like a mental movie, for about a minute.

**4. Repeat the identity statement and breath.** Close the workout by locking in the feeling.

**Focus on process goals.** Set goals for actions you control, like "make 20 calls", not just results like "win 3 clients".

**Do a daily success log.** Each night, write three things you did well, one thing to improve and how you'll improve it.

## Use it with clients

- Teach owners the centering breath before sales calls and tough meetings.
- The nightly success log is a great habit for owners who only notice what went wrong.

## Try it yourself

Do the full ten-minute workout every morning for two weeks. Keep the success log each night.
