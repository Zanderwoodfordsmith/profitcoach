*By Michael Gerber*

**The big idea:** "If your business depends on you, you don't own a business, you have a job. And it's the worst job in the world." Build a business that works without you by turning how you do things into systems.

## Key ideas

**The entrepreneurial myth.** Most businesses are started by technicians, skilled people who think "I'm great at the work, so I'll run my own business." They end up doing the work, plus everything else, and burn out.

**Three roles in every owner.** The Entrepreneur dreams and looks ahead. The Manager plans and organises. The Technician does the work. Most owners spend nearly all their time as the Technician.

**Work on the business, not in it.** Step back and design how the business should run.

**Build it like a franchise.** Imagine your business is the prototype for 5,000 more just like it. Every task needs a system that ordinary people can follow to get great results. A bakery owner shouldn't aim to bake the best cakes in town. They should build a system that bakes the best cakes in town.

**Write your organisation chart now.** Map every role the business will need, even if your name is in most boxes today. Then replace yourself one box at a time.

**Systemise everything.** Sales, delivery and admin all get documented, tested and improved.

## Use it with clients

- This is the core problem for most owner-led businesses: everything routes through the owner. Use the three roles to show where their time goes.
- Draw the future organisation chart with the owner, then pick the first box to hand over.
- Turn their best way of doing one key task into a written system this month.

## Try it yourself

Draw your business's organisation chart with every role it needs. Count how many boxes have your name in them, and pick one to hand over next.

*By Mike Michalowicz*

**The big idea:** Flip the formula. Instead of Sales - Expenses = Profit, use Sales - Profit = Expenses. Take your profit first and run the business on what's left.

## Key ideas

**Use smaller plates.** Smaller plates mean smaller portions. Open separate bank accounts for income, profit, owner's pay, tax and operating expenses. A smaller expenses account forces you to spend less.

**Serve in order.** Every time money comes in, move a set percentage to profit, owner's pay and tax first. Expenses get what's left.

**Remove temptation.** Keep the profit and tax accounts at a different bank, out of easy reach.

**Set a rhythm.** Move money on set dates, for example the 10th and 25th of each month. Each quarter, take part of the profit as a reward.

**Start small.** If 10% feels impossible, start with 1% and raise it each quarter. The habit matters more than the number.

**Constraint breeds creativity.** When expenses have a fixed budget, you find smarter ways to run the business.

## Use it with clients

- Many owners have healthy revenue but no cash. Profit First is a simple, practical fix you can set up in one session.
- Review the owner's accounts and start with a small profit percentage they can stick to.
- Use the quarterly profit distribution as a motivating milestone.

## Try it yourself

Open a separate profit account. Next time money comes in, move 1 to 5% into it before paying any bills.

*By Jim Collins*

**The big idea:** Jim Collins studied companies that went from average to beating the market by more than three times for 15 years. Greatness came from a handful of disciplined choices.

## Key ideas

**Level 5 leadership.** Great leaders combine personal humility with fierce resolve. They credit others for success and take the blame for failure.

**First who, then what.** Get the right people on the bus, the wrong people off, and the right people in the right seats. Then decide where to drive.

**Confront the brutal facts.** Face reality, however hard, while keeping faith that you'll win in the end.

**The Hedgehog Concept.** Find where three circles overlap: what you're deeply passionate about, what you can be the best in the world at, and what drives your economic engine. Then focus there.

**A culture of discipline.** Disciplined people, thought and action. Less bureaucracy needed.

**The flywheel.** Greatness doesn't come from one big moment. It comes from pushing a heavy flywheel in one direction, turn after turn, until momentum builds.

## Use it with clients

- Use the Hedgehog Concept in strategy sessions to sharpen what the business focuses on.
- "First who, then what" is a powerful frame for owners who try to grow before getting the right team in place.
- Help owners face the brutal facts in their numbers.

## Try it yourself

Draw the three Hedgehog circles for your business. Write what goes in each and where they overlap.

*By Josh Kaufman*

**The big idea:** You don't need an expensive MBA. Every business is built on five simple parts, and you can learn them yourself.

## Key ideas

**Business school is overrated.** A Stanford study following MBA graduates for decades found little link between the degree and career success.

**Every business has five parts:**
1. **Value creation:** find what people want and create it.
2. **Marketing:** get attention from the right people.
3. **Sales:** turn interested people into paying customers.
4. **Value delivery:** give customers what you promised, and more.
5. **Finance:** bring in enough money to keep going and make it worthwhile.

**If any part is missing, the business fails.** A great product with no marketing, or great sales with poor delivery, won't last.

**Learn the mental models.** Understanding a few core ideas, like pricing, leverage and systems, gives you most of the value.

## Use it with clients

- Use the five parts as a quick health check with a new owner. Which part is weakest right now?
- Many owners are strong at value creation and delivery but weak at marketing and sales. Use this frame to show where to focus.

## Try it yourself

Score your own business from 1 to 10 on each of the five parts. Pick the lowest one to work on this quarter.

*By Jason Fried and David Heinemeier Hansson*

**The big idea:** Running a business doesn't need to be complicated. The founders of Basecamp share a simpler way: solve your own problem, with less, and stand for something.

## Key ideas

**Solve your own problem.** Nike's founder poured rubber into a waffle iron because he couldn't find good running shoes. If you need it, others probably do too.

**Do it with less.** Constraints are an advantage. Start small, keep costs low and cut features until only the essentials remain.

**Pick a fight.** Stand against something in your industry. A clear enemy gives people a reason to choose you.

**Planning is guessing.** Long-term plans give a false sense of control. Decide what to do this week.

**Meetings are toxic.** They break up the day and waste many people's time at once. Keep them rare, short and focused.

**Out-teach the competition.** Share what you know. Teaching builds trust and an audience.

**Build an audience.** Speak, write and share so people come to you.

## Use it with clients

- Owners often believe growth needs more people, more products and more plans. Use Rework to explore doing less, better.
- Help owners decide what they stand against in their industry. It makes their marketing far sharper.
- Cut unnecessary meetings from the owner's week.

## Try it yourself

Write one sentence on what your business stands against in your industry. Use it in your next piece of content.

*By W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne*

**The big idea:** "The only way to beat the competition is to stop trying to beat the competition." Instead of fighting in a crowded red ocean, create a blue ocean where competition doesn't matter.

## Key ideas

**Red oceans versus blue oceans.** Red oceans are crowded markets where businesses fight over the same customers, often on price. Blue oceans are new spaces where you create fresh demand.

**Blue oceans pay.** In the authors' study of 108 business launches, the few that created new market space produced far more of the profit than those that fought for share.

**Value innovation.** Offer more value to customers while lowering your costs at the same time.

**Use the four actions:**
- **Eliminate:** what does the industry take for granted that you could drop?
- **Reduce:** what could you offer well below the industry standard?
- **Raise:** what could you offer well above the standard?
- **Create:** what could you offer that the industry never has?

**Cirque du Soleil did this.** It dropped star performers and animals, raised the artistry, and created a new kind of show between circus and theatre.

## Use it with clients

- Run the four actions with an owner competing on price. It often reveals a way to stand apart.
- Look at what the owner's competitors all do the same way. That's where a blue ocean might hide.

## Try it yourself

Fill in the four actions grid for your own offer: one thing to eliminate, reduce, raise and create.

*By Clayton Christensen*

**The big idea:** Customers don't buy products. They "hire" them to do a job in their lives. Understand the job and innovation stops being a game of luck.

## Key ideas

**Find the job to be done.** A fast-food chain wanted to sell more milkshakes. Asking about flavours didn't help. Watching buyers did. Many bought shakes early in the morning for a long, boring commute. They hired the shake to keep them full and give them something to do with one hand. Thicker shakes that lasted longer sold more.

**Jobs have three sides.** Functional (what needs doing), emotional (how they want to feel) and social (how they want to be seen).

**Look for the struggle.** Jobs show up when people struggle with their current options or use workarounds.

**Most innovation fails because it's guesswork.** A McKinsey survey found 94% of executives were unhappy with their company's innovation. Understanding the job reduces the guessing.

**Compete with non-consumption.** Sometimes the biggest competitor is customers doing nothing.

## Use it with clients

- Ask owners: what job do customers hire you for? The answer often reshapes their marketing and offer.
- Interview a few recent customers about the moment they decided to buy and what they used before.

## Try it yourself

Ask three recent clients what was happening in their business when they decided to work with you. Look for the job behind their answers.

*By Peter Thiel*

**The big idea:** Copying what works takes the world from 1 to n. Creating something new takes it from 0 to 1. The biggest wins come from doing something nobody else is doing.

## Key ideas

**Monopoly beats competition.** Businesses in fierce competition fight over thin margins. A business with something unique can charge more and plan for the long term.

**Be 10 times better.** To stand out, aim to be 10 times better on something that matters. PayPal made paying on eBay at least 10 times faster than posting a cheque.

**Start small and dominate.** Win a small market first, then expand.

**Find secrets.** Ask "What important truth do very few people agree with you on?" Hidden truths lead to new businesses.

**Answer seven questions before launching:** Can you build something much better? Is now the right time? Can you start with a big share of a small market? Do you have the right team? Can you deliver it? Will your position last? Have you spotted something others haven't?

## Use it with clients

- Owners often copy what competitors do. Ask what they could do 10 times better than anyone in their market.
- Encourage owners to own a small niche completely before spreading wider.

## Try it yourself

Answer Thiel's question: what important truth do you believe about your market that few others agree with? Build your message around it.

*By Eric Ries*

**The big idea:** Don't spend months building something nobody wants. Build a small version, test it with real customers, learn and adjust. Repeat fast.

## Key ideas

**Build, measure, learn.** Build a minimum viable product (MVP), the simplest version that tests your idea. Measure how real customers respond. Learn what to change.

**Validated learning.** Progress isn't building on time and on budget. It's learning what customers actually want, proven by what they do, not what they say.

**Test your riskiest assumptions first.** Will people want it? Will they pay? Test those before anything else.

**Pivot or persevere.** If the data shows the idea isn't working, change direction. If it's working, keep going.

**Measure what matters.** Avoid "vanity metrics" that look good but don't guide decisions. Track things like conversion and repeat purchases.

**Small batches.** Work in small pieces so you find problems early.

## Use it with clients

- Before an owner invests in a new service, product or location, help them design a cheap test first.
- Ask "What's the riskiest assumption here, and how can we test it this month?"

## Try it yourself

Pick a new idea for your business. Design a test you could run in two weeks for under £100 that would prove whether people want it.

title: Sprint
by: Jake Knapp, John Zeratsky and Braden Kowitz

length: 9m

*By Jake Knapp, John Zeratsky and Braden Kowitz*

**The big idea:** Google Ventures solves big problems in five days. A sprint takes you from problem to tested prototype in one week.

## Key ideas

**Monday: map the problem.** Map the steps a customer takes to reach the result you want. Ask experts to check it. Pick the one step that matters most.

**Turn problems into "How might we...?" questions.** "Site crashes under traffic" becomes "How might we keep the site up when traffic spikes?" It makes problems feel like challenges.

**Tuesday: sketch solutions.** Each person sketches ideas on their own. Working alone first beats group brainstorming.

**Wednesday: decide.** Vote on the best ideas. One decider makes the final call.

**Thursday: prototype.** Build a realistic fake, just enough to test.

**Friday: test.** Show it to five real customers. Five interviews reveal most of the big problems.

**Clear the diary.** The team works only on the sprint for the week.

## Use it with clients

- When an owner faces a big decision, like a new offer, website or process, a short sprint gets answers fast instead of months of debate.
- Use "How might we...?" questions in your sessions to turn complaints into challenges.

## Try it yourself

Take one problem in your business and write five "How might we...?" questions about it. Pick the best one to work on this week.

*By Peter Sims*

**The big idea:** Breakthroughs rarely come from one big leap. They come from lots of small, low-risk experiments that show you what works.

## Key ideas

**Make little bets.** Chris Rock tests new material in small clubs before a big tour. Most jokes fail. The few that work make the show.

**Affordable loss.** Only risk what you can afford to lose. Small bets let you learn without betting the business.

**Fail fast to learn fast.** Each failed bet teaches you something. Pixar's films go through many rough versions before they work.

**Healthy versus unhealthy perfectionism.** Healthy perfectionism comes from your own standards. Unhealthy perfectionism comes from fear of judgement and blocks experiments.

**Prototype early.** Make rough versions quickly and get feedback.

**Get out and see.** Spend time with customers to discover problems worth solving.

## Use it with clients

- Owners often freeze on big moves. Break the decision into small bets: test a new price with three clients, trial a role part-time, run one ad campaign.
- Celebrate experiments in the team, not just successes.

## Try it yourself

List three small bets you could make this month to test a bigger idea. Make the first one this week.

*By Drew Boyd and Jacob Goldenberg*

**The big idea:** Innovation doesn't come from thinking outside the box. It comes from working inside it, using simple patterns to change what you already have.

## Key ideas

**Subtraction.** Remove an essential part and see what's left. Contact lenses removed the frames from glasses.

**Division.** Split a product or service into parts and rearrange them. Move a part to a different place or time.

**Multiplication.** Copy a part and change it. A razor with several blades, each doing a slightly different job.

**Task unification.** Give an existing part a new job. Suitcase wheels that also act as a stand.

**Attribute dependency.** Link two things that weren't linked. Prices that change with demand, or lights that dim with daylight.

**Start with what you have.** Constraints spark creativity.

## Use it with clients

- Use the patterns in a session to rethink an owner's service. What could you subtract? What could be split into stages?
- Subtraction is great for simplifying packages and offers.

## Try it yourself

Take your main service and apply subtraction: remove one part people assume is essential. What new offer could that create?

**The big idea:** Original thinkers feel the same fear and doubt as everyone else. The difference is they act anyway. Originality can be learned.

## Use it with clients

- When owners need to sell a change to their team or partners, help them lead with the weaknesses.

*By Jeff Bezos*

**The big idea:** Jeff Bezos's letters and speeches show the mindsets that made Amazon: stay in "Day 1", obsess over customers and think long term.

## Key ideas

**It's always Day 1.** Day 2 is stasis, then decline. Keep the energy of a start-up: experiment, move fast and avoid getting comfortable.

**Obsess over customers, not competitors.** Customers are always a little unhappy, which keeps you inventing on their behalf.

**Think long term.** Make decisions for where you want to be in years, not quarters. Short-term pain is often worth long-term gain.

**One-way and two-way doors.** Most decisions are two-way doors: you can reverse them, so decide fast. Save slow, careful thinking for one-way doors.

**Disagree and commit.** You can disagree with a decision and still commit fully to making it work.

**Minimise regret.** Bezos left a safe job to start Amazon by imagining himself at 80 and asking which choice he'd regret.

**Write it down.** Amazon replaced slide decks with six-page memos. Writing forces clear thinking.

## Use it with clients

- Owners often treat every decision as a one-way door and stall. Help them sort decisions into two-way doors to decide faster.
- Use the regret question for big personal decisions, like selling, stepping back or expanding.

## Try it yourself

List the decisions you're putting off. Mark each as a one-way or two-way door, and make every two-way decision this week.

**The big idea:** The best way to build a lasting brand is culture. Great culture leads to happy staff, great service, loyal customers and more sales. Zappos grew to $1 billion in sales this way.

*By Nir Eyal*

**The big idea:** The most successful products create habits. They use a four-step "Hook" cycle. Understand it to build better products, and to see why your phone has such a hold on you.

*By Morgan Housel*

**The big idea:** Doing well with money has little to do with how smart you are and a lot to do with how you behave. The goal is independence: to do what you want, when you want, with whom you want.

## Key ideas

**Compounding is powerful.** A penny doubled every day for a month becomes millions, and most of the growth comes at the end. Starting a week later costs you most of it. Time matters more than the amount.

**Wealth is what you don't see.** Wealth is the money not spent: the car not bought, the upgrade skipped. Spending to look rich makes you less rich.

**Know what "enough" is.** Without a sense of enough, the goalposts keep moving and you take risks you don't need to.

**Leave room for error.** Plans fail. Keep a cash buffer so bad luck doesn't force bad decisions.

**Save without a reason.** Savings give you options and freedom when the unexpected happens.

**Be reasonable, not perfectly rational.** Pick a money plan you can stick with.

## Use it with clients

- Owners often have most of their wealth tied up in the business. Discuss "enough" and building wealth outside it.
- Encourage owners to keep a cash reserve. It turns a crisis into an opportunity.

## Try it yourself

Write down what "enough" means for you in numbers. Then check how many months of costs your cash reserve covers.
