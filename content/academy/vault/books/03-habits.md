*By James Clear*

**The big idea:** A habit sticks when it is obvious, easy, attractive and satisfying. Miss any one of those four and the old routine wins.

## Key ideas

**The four laws of behaviour change.** No obvious cue and you forget. Too hard and you do the easy, familiar thing instead. Not attractive and you resist it. No reward and you lose interest.

**Stack it.** Tie the new habit to one you already do. "After I pour my coffee, I'll write my top three tasks." The old habit becomes the cue.

**Start with two minutes.** Shrink the habit until it takes less than two minutes. "Read before bed" becomes "read one page." "Go for a run" becomes "put on my trainers." Show up first, then grow it.

**Pair it with something you enjoy.** Only allow yourself a favourite podcast during a walk, or a favourite coffee while you do admin. The habit becomes something you look forward to.

**Score it.** Track the habit on a calendar or app. Each tick is a small reward, and a streak becomes something you want to protect.

**Vote for your identity.** Each repetition is a vote for the person you want to be. "I'm someone who reviews the numbers every Monday" lasts longer than "I'm trying to check the numbers."

## Use it with clients

- When an owner says "I keep meaning to...", ask which law is missing. Usually the habit is too big or has no cue.
- Turn weekly priorities into two-minute starters. "Review cash flow" becomes "open the bank dashboard at 8am Monday."
- Track key habits in your sessions. Ask for the streak, not the excuse.

## Try it yourself

Pick one habit for your practice, such as daily outreach. Stack it on an existing habit, shrink it to two minutes and track it for 14 days.

*By BJ Fogg*

**The big idea:** A behaviour happens when motivation, ability and a prompt meet at the same moment (B = MAP). Make the habit tiny and you don't need much motivation at all.

## Key ideas

**Motivation goes up and down.** Hard tasks need high motivation. Easy tasks need very little. You brush your teeth even when exhausted because it's easy. Design habits that are easy enough to do on your worst day.

**Shrink the behaviour.** Floss one tooth. Do two press-ups. Write one sentence. Tiny versions get done, and they often grow on their own.

**Find an anchor.** Use something you already do as the prompt. "After I put the kettle on, I'll do two press-ups." The anchor reminds you every day.

**Celebrate straight away.** Right after the habit, give yourself a small celebration, like a fist pump or "Nailed it!" The good feeling wires the habit into your brain. Emotions create habits, not repetition alone.

**Recipe:** "After I [anchor], I will [tiny behaviour]. Then I'll celebrate."

## Use it with clients

- Owners often build big plans that collapse in week two. Help them write tiny habit recipes for the actions that matter, like one follow-up call after their morning coffee.
- When a team habit isn't sticking, check whether it has a clear anchor and whether it's easy enough.

## Try it yourself

Write one tiny habit recipe for your business. Do it every day this week and celebrate each time, even if it feels silly.

*By Stephen Guise*

**The big idea:** Big habits need big motivation, and motivation runs out. Mini habits are "stupidly small" actions you can do on any day, no matter how tired or busy you are.

## Key ideas

**Make it a joke.** Shrink the habit until you think "This is so small it's ridiculous." One press-up. One sentence. One page. No excuse can beat it.

**Motion creates motivation.** Once you start, you usually keep going. One press-up often becomes 20. You get the momentum without needing to feel motivated first.

**Small wins build belief.** Hitting your target every day, even a tiny one, builds confidence. Missing a big target every day drains it.

**Never raise the minimum.** Do more whenever you like, but keep the requirement tiny. That keeps the habit safe on bad days.

**Run a few at once.** Because they're so small, you can build two or three mini habits at the same time.

## Use it with clients

- When an owner struggles with consistent marketing or sales, set a mini habit: one LinkedIn comment a day, or one follow-up email.
- Owners love big plans. Help them keep the daily minimum tiny so the habit survives the busy weeks.

## Try it yourself

Set one mini habit so small it's a joke. Do it every day for 30 days and track your streak.

*By Charles Duhigg*

**The big idea:** Every habit is a loop of cue, routine and reward. To change a bad habit, keep the cue and the reward, and swap in a new routine.

## Key ideas

**The habit loop.** A cue triggers a routine, which leads to a reward. Over time your brain craves the reward as soon as it sees the cue.

**Find the real reward.** Fewer than 10% of smokers quit with nicotine patches, so nicotine isn't the whole reward. Some smokers crave the social break, others the escape from boredom. Test different routines to find what you really want.

**Name the cue.** Most cues fall into five types: location, time, emotional state, other people or the action just before. Note these each time the urge hits.

**Plan a new routine.** "When I feel bored at 3pm (cue), I'll walk to chat with a colleague (new routine) to get a break (reward)."

**Keystone habits.** Some habits trigger many others. Exercise often leads to better eating and sleep. In companies, a focus on one thing, like safety, can lift everything else.

**Belief makes change stick.** Groups help people believe change is possible.

## Use it with clients

- Owners often have bad habits like checking email constantly or saying yes to every request. Help them map the cue and the real reward, then design a new routine.
- Look for keystone habits in the business. A weekly numbers meeting often improves many other things.

## Try it yourself

Pick one bad habit. For three days, write down the cue each time it happens. Then design a new routine for the same reward.

*By Robert Maurer*

**The big idea:** Kaizen is the Japanese practice of continuous, small improvement. Tiny steps slip past the brain's fear response, so you keep going where big changes stall.

## Key ideas

**Big goals trigger fear.** The bigger the change, the more your brain senses danger. Fear triggers fight or flight, and you reach for comfort instead of progress.

**Small steps tiptoe past fear.** A step so small it feels silly, like one minute of exercise or one sentence, doesn't alarm your brain. You act calmly and keep going.

**Ask small questions.** "What's one small thing I could do to get a little closer to my goal?" Small questions spark ideas without pressure.

**Think small thoughts.** Rehearse the action in your head for a few seconds each day. Mental practice builds confidence before you act.

**Solve small problems.** Fix small issues before they grow. Small irritations are early warnings.

**Reward small wins.** Small rewards keep you motivated without making the work about the prize.

**Improve 1% a day.** Small daily gains add up fast.

## Use it with clients

- When an owner freezes in front of a big change, like hiring a manager or raising prices, ask the small question: what's one small step this week?
- Kaizen works well for teams. Ask staff for one small improvement each week.

## Try it yourself

Pick a goal you keep avoiding. Ask "What's one step so small it's almost silly?" Do it today.

*By Darren Hardy*

**The big idea:** Small, smart choices made consistently create huge results over time. Each step feels unimportant, which is why most people quit before the payoff.

## Key ideas

**Choices make you.** Every small decision changes the direction of your life. Most of us sleepwalk through our choices, doing what others want instead of what serves our goals.

**Track to build awareness.** Write down every choice in one area, like spending or food, for a week. Tracking shows you the gap between what you think you do and what you actually do.

**Consistency beats intensity.** One healthy meal does nothing. A healthy meal every day for a year changes your life. The same is true for sales calls, saving and learning.

**Momentum is hard to start and easy to keep.** Like pushing a car, the first few weeks take the most effort. Once moving, it rolls with less work.

**Watch your influences.** Inputs (what you read and watch), associations (who you spend time with) and environment all shape your choices.

## Use it with clients

- Owners often want the big win: the one deal or campaign that changes everything. Show them that steady weekly activity compounds into bigger results.
- Ask owners to track one area for a week, like where their time goes or what they spend. The data often surprises them.

## Try it yourself

Choose one small action that would change your business if you did it every day for a year. Track it daily for the next 30 days.

*By Kelly McGonigal*

**The big idea:** Willpower is like a muscle. Every act of self-control draws from the same tank, and modern life drains it fast. You can build a bigger tank and use it wisely.

## Key ideas

**Willpower runs low.** Controlling emotions made people spend more. Resisting sweets made them procrastinate. Each act of self-control leaves less for the next.

**Slow your breathing.** Breathing at about five breaths a minute calms your body and boosts self-control in the moment.

**Forgive yourself.** Guilt after a slip often leads to more slips. Self-forgiveness helps you get back on track.

**Build the tank daily:**
- **Sleep well.** Tired brains have less self-control.
- **Exercise.** Even short workouts strengthen willpower.
- **Meditate.** A few minutes a day trains attention.
- **Eat steadily.** Stable blood sugar supports self-control.

**Do hard things early.** Willpower is strongest in the morning. Put your most important task first.

## Use it with clients

- Owners who make poor decisions late in the day may simply be out of willpower. Move key decisions and hard conversations to the morning.
- Reduce the number of small decisions owners make each day with simple rules and routines.

## Try it yourself

Move your hardest task to the first hour of your day for a week. Notice how much easier it feels.

*By Mel Robbins*

**The big idea:** When you feel the urge to act on a goal, count 5-4-3-2-1 and move before your brain talks you out of it.

## Key ideas

**Hesitation kills action.** You have about five seconds between an impulse and your brain's excuses. Act inside that window.

**The countdown is a launch.** Counting backwards breaks the habit of hesitating and focuses your mind. When you hit "one", you move.

**Action builds control.** Psychologists call it locus of control. The more you feel in control of your life, the happier and more successful you are. A bias towards action builds that feeling.

**Use it to replace bad habits.** Reaching for the wine at night? Count 5-4-3-2-1 and put the bottle back. Feel anger rising? Count down and take a breath. The countdown is the cue, the action is the new routine and the feeling of control is the reward.

**It works for courage too.** Speak up in a meeting, make the call, send the email.

## Use it with clients

- Owners often know what to do but hesitate: the call to a lost client, the conversation with an underperformer. Teach the countdown as a way to start.
- Suggest the rule for avoided sales tasks, like picking up the phone to a prospect.

## Try it yourself

Next time you hesitate over something you know you should do, count 5-4-3-2-1 and act before you reach one.

*By Brendon Burchard*

**The big idea:** High performers aren't just talented. They practise a few habits that keep them clear, energised and focused over the long term.

## Key ideas

**Release tension, set intention.** At each change in your day, from work to home or one task to the next, close your eyes, relax your face, neck and shoulders, and ask "What feeling do I want to bring to this next part?"

**Create necessity.** Bob Marley said "You never know how strong you are until being strong is your only choice." High performers make their goals part of who they are and tell others, so performing well feels necessary.

**Seek clarity.** High performers know who they want to be, how they want to interact and what skills they need. They ask these questions often.

**Generate energy.** Sleep, exercise and transitions keep energy high.

**Raise productivity.** Focus on the few outputs that matter most in your field.

**Develop influence.** Teach people how to think, challenge them to grow and be a role model.

**Show courage.** Speak up and take on hard things, especially when it's uncomfortable.

## Use it with clients

- Owners rush from meeting to meeting carrying stress from one to the next. The release and intention habit takes 30 seconds and changes how they show up.
- Ask owners to say their goals out loud to their team. Public commitment creates necessity.

## Try it yourself

Before each meeting today, take 30 seconds to release tension and choose the feeling you want to bring in.

*By Chip Heath and Dan Heath*

**The big idea:** Everyone has a rational side (the Rider) and an emotional side (the Elephant). To change behaviour, direct the Rider, motivate the Elephant and shape the Path.

## Key ideas

**Direct the Rider.** The rational side needs clear direction. Find what already works and do more of it. Script the first moves. Vague goals like "be healthier" fail. "Buy 1% milk" works.

**Motivate the Elephant.** Logic alone won't move people. Make them feel something. Show progress early: a car wash loyalty card that started with two free stamps out of ten got nearly twice as many people to finish as one needing eight with none, even though both needed eight washes. Shrink the change so it feels doable.

**Shape the Path.** Change the environment to make the right behaviour easy. Remove friction, build habits and use the crowd, because people copy what others do.

**What looks like resistance is often lack of clarity.** What looks like laziness is often exhaustion.

## Use it with clients

- When an owner wants the team to adopt a new system, plan all three: clear first steps (Rider), a reason people care about (Elephant) and an easy setup (Path).
- Show early progress. Tell the team they're already partway there.

## Try it yourself

Think of a change you want your clients or team to make. Write one action for each part: direct the Rider, motivate the Elephant, shape the Path.

*By Daniel Pink*

**The big idea:** Rewards like bonuses work for simple, routine tasks. For creative, thinking work, they backfire. People do their best work when they have autonomy, mastery and purpose.

## Key ideas

**Carrots and sticks fail for complex work.** "If you do this, you get that" narrows focus and harms creativity and long-term motivation.

**Pay people fairly first.** Money matters until it's fair. Then take it off the table so people can focus on the work.

**Autonomy.** Give people control over what they work on, when, how and with whom. Software company Atlassian gave programmers a day to work on anything they liked. They came up with new products and dozens of fixes.

**Mastery.** People love getting better at something that matters. Give them work that stretches them, with clear feedback.

**Purpose.** People work harder when they know why their work matters and who it helps.

## Use it with clients

- Many owners use bonuses to fix motivation problems in creative or customer-facing roles. Explore autonomy, growth and purpose first.
- Help owners give team members more ownership over how they hit their targets.
- Share client wins with the team so people see the purpose of their work.

## Try it yourself

Think of one person you work with. Ask how you could give them more autonomy, a chance to grow, or a clearer sense of purpose this month.

*Talk by Tali Sharot, TEDxCambridge*

**The big idea:** Neuroscientist Tali Sharot shows that fear and warnings rarely change behaviour. What works is giving people immediate rewards, social proof and a sense of progress.

## Key ideas

**Warnings don't work well.** Graphic warnings on cigarettes and talks about future health risks have little effect. Threats make people freeze or switch off, and the future feels far away.

**Use immediate rewards.** In one hospital, staff washed their hands far more when an electronic board gave instant positive feedback, rising from about 10% to about 90%. People respond to rewards now, not risks later.

**Use social proof.** We're wired to copy others. Telling people what others like them already do works better than explaining why they should.

**Track progress.** Showing people their improvement keeps them going. A visible sense of progress feeds motivation.

## Use it with clients

- When an owner wants their team to change a habit, swap warnings for quick wins: celebrate the behaviour as soon as it happens and make progress visible.
- In your own sales, show prospects what similar owners have done rather than only explaining risks.

## Try it yourself

Pick one behaviour you want from your team or a client. Design an immediate reward and a way to show progress, then try it this week.
