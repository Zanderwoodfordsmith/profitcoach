*By Dale Carnegie*

**The big idea:** Written in 1936 and still true. People are drawn to those who are genuinely interested in them and make them feel important.

## Key ideas

**Be genuinely interested in others.** "You can make more friends in two months by becoming interested in other people than in two years by trying to get people interested in you." Find what fascinates the other person and get curious about it too.

**Talk about their interests.** People will listen for hours when you talk about them.

**Remember names.** A person's name is the sweetest sound to them. Use it.

**Make people feel important, and mean it.** Give honest, specific appreciation. Avoid empty flattery.

**Don't criticise, condemn or complain.** Criticism makes people defensive. Try to understand why they did what they did.

**Let them save face.** Admit your own mistakes quickly. Show respect for their opinions, even when you disagree.

**Begin with friendliness.** Start conversations in a warm, positive way and find points of agreement.

## Use it with clients

- In sales conversations, spend more time asking about the owner's business, goals and frustrations than talking about your services.
- Owners do most of their company's selling. These principles are a simple training plan for them and their team.
- Coach owners to give specific appreciation to staff, not just correction.

## Try it yourself

In your next three conversations, ask questions until you find what the other person is most excited about. Talk about that.

*By Daniel Pink*

**The big idea:** We're all in sales now. Any time you persuade someone to act, you're selling. Modern selling is about serving, not pushing.

## Key ideas

**We all sell.** Persuading a colleague, a client or your children is selling. Your success depends on moving people.

**Attunement.** See things from the other person's view. Ask more questions, mirror their style slightly and imagine their situation. Being slightly less powerful in your own mind helps you tune in.

**Buoyancy.** Stay afloat in a sea of rejection. Before a call, ask yourself "Can I do this?" instead of telling yourself "I can do this." The question makes you think through how. After a no, explain it as temporary and specific, not permanent and personal.

**Clarity.** Help people see their problem in a new way. Finding the right problem matters more than solving the obvious one.

**Pitch in new ways.** Try a one-word pitch, a question pitch or a rhyming pitch to make your idea easy to grasp.

**Serve.** Make it personal and make it purposeful. Ask "If they agree, will their life improve?"

## Use it with clients

- Many owners say "I'm not a salesperson." Reframe selling as helping people solve problems.
- Teach buoyancy to owners and sales staff: how to explain rejection in a way that keeps them going.

## Try it yourself

Before your next sales call, ask yourself "Can I do this?" and list three reasons why you can.

*By Robert Cialdini*

**The big idea:** Six principles explain why people say yes. Use them honestly and your requests become far more persuasive.

## Key ideas

**Reciprocity.** People feel obliged to return favours. Give first: useful advice, a free insight or a helpful introduction.

**Commitment and consistency.** Once people commit to something small, they want to stay consistent. Start with small agreements.

**Social proof.** People follow others like them. Share case studies, testimonials and numbers of clients served.

**Liking.** We say yes to people we like. Find common ground and give genuine compliments.

**Authority.** People trust experts. Show credentials, results and know-how.

**Scarcity.** People want more of what's limited. Be honest about real limits, like a set number of client places. Framing what someone stands to lose is often stronger than what they stand to gain.

**Use it ethically.** These principles work because people trust them. Misusing them destroys trust.

## Use it with clients

- Review an owner's sales process and marketing for each principle. Most are missing social proof and reciprocity.
- In your own sales, give a genuine insight before asking for anything, and share results from similar clients.

## Try it yourself

Look at your website or sales page. Add one example of social proof and one way to give value up front.

*By Phil Jones*

**The big idea:** Certain phrases, which Phil Jones calls magic words, speak to the part of the brain that decides quickly. Small changes in wording can lead to big changes in results.

## Key ideas

**"What do you know about...?"** Opens curiosity. When someone realises they don't know much, they want to learn more. "What do you know about how other owners free up ten hours a week?"

**"How open-minded are you...?"** People like to see themselves as open-minded, so they lean in. "How open-minded are you about trying a different approach to hiring?"

**"I'm not sure if it's for you, but..."** Takes the pressure off. People relax and become curious.

**"Just imagine..."** Helps people picture the result. "Just imagine having a team that runs the day-to-day without you."

**"What happens if...?"** Gets people to think about the cost of doing nothing. "What happens if nothing changes in the next year?"

**"You have three options."** Gives people a simple choice, often including doing nothing, and helps them decide.

**"What's your timescale?"** Moves a conversation towards action.

## Use it with clients

- Owners and their teams can drop these phrases into sales calls, emails and follow-ups straight away.
- Use "What happens if...?" with owners who keep putting off important changes.

## Try it yourself

Pick two phrases and use them in your next sales conversation or outreach message. Note how people respond.

*By Chris Voss*

**The big idea:** Former FBI hostage negotiator Chris Voss says compromise is often the worst outcome, like wearing one black shoe and one brown. Use tactical empathy to get what you want while the other side feels heard.

## Key ideas

**Tactical empathy.** Understand the other side's feelings and situation, and say it out loud. People who feel understood are more willing to work with you.

**Mirroring.** Repeat the last few words they said, as a question. "Too expensive?" People keep talking and reveal more.

**Labelling.** Name their emotion: "It seems like you're worried about the timing." Labels calm negative feelings and build trust.

**Get to "That's right."** Summarise their view so well they say "That's right." That's the moment they feel understood.

**Use calibrated questions.** "How" and "what" questions make the other side solve your problem. "How am I supposed to do that?" is a polite way to say no.

**Embrace "no".** "No" makes people feel safe and in control. Ask questions where "no" moves things forward: "Is now a bad time to talk?"

**Use a late-night radio voice.** Calm, slow and low builds trust under pressure.

## Use it with clients

- Teach owners mirroring and labelling for tough conversations with clients, suppliers and staff.
- On sales calls, label the prospect's concerns instead of arguing with them: "It sounds like you've been burned by consultants before."

## Try it yourself

In your next negotiation or sales call, label one emotion and mirror at least three times. See what you learn.

*By Roger Fisher and William Ury*

**The big idea:** Standard haggling leaves people tired and resentful. Principled negotiation gets better deals and keeps relationships intact.

## Key ideas

**Separate the people from the problem.** Try on their view: imagine how they got to their position and the pressure they're under. Attack the problem together, not each other.

**Focus on interests, not positions.** A position is what someone says they want. An interest is why they want it. Two people fighting over an orange may want different things: one the juice, one the peel for baking.

**Invent options for mutual gain.** Brainstorm several solutions before deciding. Look for ways both sides win.

**Use objective criteria.** Base the agreement on fair standards, like market rates or expert opinion, not on who pushes harder.

**Know your BATNA.** Your Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement is what you'll do if there's no deal. A strong BATNA gives you confidence to walk away.

## Use it with clients

- Before a big negotiation with a supplier, landlord or key client, help the owner list the other side's interests and their own BATNA.
- When partners or managers clash, use interests versus positions to find solutions that work for both.

## Try it yourself

Before your next negotiation, write down your BATNA and three interests the other side probably has.

*By Simon Sinek*

**The big idea:** People don't buy what you do. They buy why you do it. Great businesses and leaders start with why.

## Key ideas

**The Golden Circle.** Most businesses talk from the outside in: what they do, how they do it, then maybe why. Inspiring businesses talk from the inside out: why first, then how, then what.

**Why attracts the right people.** When Nike ran an ad with Colin Kaepernick, its share price dipped, but online sales jumped 25% in the following week. People who shared Nike's belief bought.

**Leaders start with why.** Martin Luther King said "I have a dream", not "I have a plan." People follow a cause they believe in.

**Find your why.** Look back at your life for key stories: moments of joy, pride and challenge. Look for the common thread. Write it as: "To [contribution] so that [impact]."

**Live it.** A why only matters if decisions, hiring and marketing reflect it.

## Use it with clients

- Help owners find the why behind their business. It sharpens marketing, attracts better staff and helps decisions.
- Rewrite the owner's website or pitch to lead with why before what.

## Try it yourself

Write your why statement: "To [contribution] so that [impact]." Test it on someone who knows you well.

*By Donald Miller*

**The big idea:** Customers don't care about your story. They care about their own. Make the customer the hero and your business the guide, and your marketing becomes clear.

## Key ideas

**The customer is the hero.** Talk about their problem and their goals, not your history and awards. You're Yoda, they're Luke Skywalker.

**Answer the story questions:**
- **Who is the hero and what do they want?** Name one clear desire.
- **What's their problem?** Name the external problem, how it makes them feel and why it's unfair.
- **Who is the guide?** Show empathy ("we understand") and authority (results, testimonials).
- **What's the plan?** Give three simple steps to work with you.
- **What's the call to action?** Tell them clearly what to do next.
- **What's at stake?** Show what failure looks like if they do nothing.
- **What does success look like?** Paint the picture of life after.

**Clarity beats clever.** If customers have to work to understand you, they'll leave.

## Use it with clients

- Use the story questions to rewrite an owner's website homepage or sales deck. Most talk too much about themselves.
- Apply it to your own marketing: the owner is the hero, you're the guide.

## Try it yourself

Write a one-paragraph brand script for your business using the story questions. Put the customer first.

*By Chip Heath and Dan Heath*

**The big idea:** Some ideas stick and others vanish. Sticky ideas share six traits: they're simple, unexpected, concrete, credible, emotional and told as stories (SUCCESs).

## Key ideas

**The curse of knowledge.** Once you know something, it's hard to imagine not knowing it. Experts explain things in ways that confuse others.

**Simple.** Find the core. Clinton's campaign kept on track with one line: "It's the economy, stupid."

**Unexpected.** Break a pattern to grab attention, then hold it with curiosity.

**Concrete.** Use specific, physical examples instead of abstract ideas.

**Credible.** Use details, stats people can picture, or let people test it themselves.

**Emotional.** Make people care. One person's story moves people more than big numbers.

**Stories.** Stories show people how to act and make ideas memorable.

## Use it with clients

- Help owners make their sales message stick with one simple core idea and one concrete client story.
- Use it when owners share their vision with the team. A story beats a slide of goals.

## Try it yourself

Take your main sales message. Cut it to one simple sentence and add one concrete client story.

*By Leil Lowndes*

**The big idea:** Small talk feels awkward, but it's a skill. Think of conversation like table tennis: serve something easy to hit, then keep the rally going.

## Key ideas

**Serve with W.I.T.:**
- **We:** start with a shared comment. "We're in for a treat, I hear she's a great speaker." "We" makes strangers feel like friends.
- **It:** ask about something around you. "What did you think of that session?"
- **They:** ask about the other person. "What brings you here?"

**Keep the rally going.** Listen for keywords in their answer and ask about those.

**Use a slow, flooding smile.** Look at the person, pause, then let a big smile spread. It feels personal.

**Let your eyes stick.** Hold eye contact a moment longer than usual to show interest.

**Avoid the "What do you do?" opener.** Ask "How do you spend your time?" or something about their interests instead.

**Remember details.** Bring up something they told you last time. It shows you listened.

## Use it with clients

- Networking is a big source of clients for owners and advisors. Give owners a few W.I.T. openers before events.
- Help owners train client-facing staff in simple rapport skills.

## Try it yourself

At your next event or meeting, open with a "we" comment and follow up on at least two keywords the other person uses.

*By Olivia Fox Cabane*

**The big idea:** Charisma isn't a gift you're born with. It's a set of behaviours anyone can learn: presence, power and warmth.

## Key ideas

**Steve Jobs learned it.** In early talks Jobs was awkward and shy. He became charismatic over years of practice.

**Presence.** Be fully in the moment. People can tell when your mind wanders. Bring your focus back to the person in front of you, for example by noticing your breath or your toes.

**Power.** Show that you can affect the world, through confidence, body language and calm. Take up space and speak slowly.

**Warmth.** Show goodwill. People want to know you like them and want to help. Picture something you're grateful for to bring warmth into your face.

**Manage your inner state.** Your body language follows your mind. Calm anxiety before key moments and visualise success.

**Different styles.** Focus charisma (Elon Musk), visionary charisma (Steve Jobs), kindness charisma (the Dalai Lama) and authority charisma (Colin Powell). Choose the one that fits the moment.

## Use it with clients

- Owners often need more presence and warmth on sales calls and with their teams. Practise these behaviours with them.
- Before a big pitch, help the owner calm nerves and focus on warmth as well as authority.

## Try it yourself

In your next conversation, put your phone away and focus fully on the other person. Notice how they respond.

*By Amy Cuddy*

**The big idea:** Your body shapes your mind. Open, expansive body language helps you feel more confident, and others respond to that confidence.

## Key ideas

**Body affects feelings.** Open, wide postures help you feel creative, decisive, focused and confident. Closed, slouched postures leave you anxious, distracted and self-absorbed.

**People respond in kind.** When your body language is confident and open, people treat you that way, which boosts your confidence further.

**Boost before big moments.** Before a pitch, interview or tough meeting:
- Stretch your chest and shoulders: hands on your lower back, push your chest up.
- Stand tall with your arms wide or hands on hips for a couple of minutes.
- Avoid hunching over your phone right before you go in.

**Presence comes from being yourself.** Feel connected to your true values and abilities, and you'll show up with quiet confidence instead of nerves.

**Fake it till you become it.** Small acts of confidence, repeated, change how you see yourself.

## Use it with clients

- Owners often hunch over laptops before calls. Suggest two minutes of open posture before important meetings.
- Help owners notice their body language in team meetings. Closed postures signal stress to the team.

## Try it yourself

Before your next important call, stand tall in an open posture for two minutes. Notice how you feel when you start.
