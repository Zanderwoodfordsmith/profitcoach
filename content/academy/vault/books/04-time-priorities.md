*By Gary Keller and Jay Papasan*

**The big idea:** Extraordinary results come from doing fewer things, better. Keep asking one question: what's the ONE thing I can do such that by doing it, everything else becomes easier or unnecessary?

## Key ideas

**Ask the focusing question.** "What's the ONE thing I can do such that by doing it, everything else will be easier or unnecessary?" Ask it for your year, your week and your day.

**Three commitments (M.A.P.):**
- **Mastery:** treat your ONE thing as a path you keep improving on, not a box to tick.
- **Accountability:** own your results. No excuses, no blame.
- **Passion:** chase a result big enough that "good enough" won't do.

**Say no to protect your yes.** "One yes must be defended over time by 1,000 nos." Saying yes too quickly spreads you thin.

**Not everything matters equally.** A long to-do list makes everything feel urgent. It isn't. Accept a little chaos in other areas while you focus on the thing that matters most.

**Time-block it.** Book four hours a day for your ONE thing, ideally in the morning, and protect it like your most important meeting.

## Use it with clients

- Ask owners the focusing question at the start of every quarter. One clear priority beats ten half-done projects.
- Help owners block time for their ONE thing in the diary before anything else goes in.
- When an owner's list is long, ask which single item would make the rest easier.

## Try it yourself

Ask the focusing question about your business this quarter. Block two hours in your diary this week for that one thing.

*By Greg McKeown*

**The big idea:** Less, but better. A non-essentialist thinks almost everything is essential. An essentialist thinks almost everything is not, so they can give their best to the few things that matter.

## Key ideas

**Most opportunities aren't essential.** Warren Buffett owes most of his wealth to a handful of investments. He says no to almost everything else.

**Accept trade-offs.** Every yes is a no to something else. Southwest Airlines became a leader by choosing what not to do: no meals, no seat assignments, no first class. Make trade-offs on purpose.

**Set boundaries.** Boundaries aren't weakness. They protect your time for what matters. Decide in advance what you won't do.

**Use extreme criteria.** If an opportunity isn't a clear yes, it's a no. Score options out of 100. Anything under 90 becomes a zero.

**Build in buffers.** Things take longer than planned. Add 50% to your time estimates.

**Make it routine.** Turn essential work into routines so it happens without willpower.

## Use it with clients

- Owners often say yes to every client, product and project. Help them spot the essential few and drop or delegate the rest.
- Use the 90-point rule for new opportunities like partnerships, new services or speaking requests.
- Add buffers to project timelines so the team stops missing deadlines.

## Try it yourself

List everything you're committed to. Score each one out of 100. Drop, delegate or delay anything under 90.

*By Derek Sivers*

**The big idea:** If you feel anything less than "Wow! That would be amazing! Hell yeah!", say no. Refuse almost everything and do the few things you choose all the way.

## Key ideas

**Use the hell yeah test.** When you're over-committed and scattered, only say yes to things that excite you. Everything else is a no. That frees space for what's worth doing.

**Ask what you hate not doing.** "What do I love?" gives a long, vague list. "What do I hate not doing?" is sharper. Think about what you crave after a few days without it. That's what's worth doing.

**Look for what you're drawn to.** What do you do in your spare time without being asked? Clues to your best work hide there.

**Do it all the way.** Once you choose something, commit fully. Half-hearted effort wastes time.

## Use it with clients

- Owners often take on clients and projects that drain them. Ask them to run the hell yeah test on their client list and offers.
- Help owners notice what work they hate not doing. Their best role in the business often hides there.

## Try it yourself

For one week, run every new request through the test. If it isn't a hell yeah, politely say no.

*By Damon Zahariades*

**The big idea:** The single best way to improve your productivity is to say no more often. Each respectful no creates time for the work and people that matter.

## Key ideas

**Why it's hard.** We're trained to say yes. As kids, "no" got us in trouble. At work, yes seemed the way to get ahead. We assume other people's goals matter more than ours, and that saying no is selfish.

**Three truths that give you strength:**
1. Every yes is a no to something else, often your own priorities.
2. Your time and goals matter as much as anyone else's.
3. Most people respect a clear, polite no more than a reluctant yes.

**How to say no well:**
- Be direct and brief. Don't over-explain.
- Be kind. Thank them for thinking of you.
- Don't leave false hope. "Maybe later" usually means more requests.
- Offer an alternative if you want to help: a resource, someone else, or a smaller task.

**Expect pushback.** Some people test your no. Hold firm and stay polite.

## Use it with clients

- Owners often say yes to every customer request, meeting and favour. Role-play a few polite no scripts with them.
- Help owners give their team permission to say no to low-value work.

## Try it yourself

Write one polite no script you can reuse. Use it at least once this week.

*Inspired by Essentialism by Greg McKeown and the work of William Ury*

**The big idea:** We say yes when we want to say no because we forget our purpose and fear awkwardness. A positive no turns every no into a yes to what matters most.

## Key ideas

**Why we say yes.** When we're unclear on our goals, we drift into social games. We're also wired to fit in with the group, so saying no feels risky.

**Every no is an inner yes.** Saying no to a low-value meeting is saying yes to your priorities. Each one strengthens your commitment to what you stand for.

**A no can protect the relationship.** A clear, honest no is kinder than a resentful yes or a late cancellation.

**Use yes, no, yes.**
1. **Yes:** state what you're protecting. "I'm committed to finishing the client proposals this week."
2. **No:** say no clearly. "So I can't join the planning meeting."
3. **Yes:** offer something positive. "I can send you my notes, or meet next Tuesday."

## Use it with clients

- Give owners the yes-no-yes script for requests from staff, partners and clients.
- Help owners link each no to their quarterly priorities, so saying no feels like strength, not rudeness.

## Try it yourself

Write a yes-no-yes reply to one request you've been putting off answering. Send it today.

*By Evan Carmichael*

**The big idea:** One word sums up what you stand for. It keeps you going through hard times, makes decisions easier and helps people remember you.

## Key ideas

**Your One Word is your core value.** It's the reason you do what you do. Evan Carmichael's word is "entrepreneurs". Everything he creates serves them.

**Find it by looking back.** Look at three areas: your proudest moments, the things that make you angry, and what people come to you for. What word connects them?

**Test it.** Remind yourself of the word during the day. Does it push you to act? Does it make decisions easier? If not, keep looking.

**It pulls you through the dip.** When results slow and encouragement dries up, your word reminds you why you started.

**It makes you memorable.** In a noisy world, people remember one clear idea. Brands that stand for one word are easy to talk about.

## Use it with clients

- Help owners find the one word their business stands for. It sharpens their marketing and helps staff make decisions without asking.
- When an owner is weighing up an opportunity, ask whether it fits their word.

## Try it yourself

Write down your three proudest moments, three things that frustrate you and three things people ask you for. Find the word that connects them.

*By Brian Tracy*

**The big idea:** Your "frog" is your biggest, most important task, the one you're most likely to put off. Do it first and the rest of the day gets easier.

## Key ideas

**Find your frog.** Know your key result areas: the few things you're paid to deliver. Your frog is the task that moves one of those forward most.

**Think about consequences.** Which task would have the biggest impact if you did it, or the biggest cost if you didn't? That's your frog.

**Use the ABCDE method.** Mark each task:
- **A:** must do, serious consequences.
- **B:** should do, mild consequences.
- **C:** nice to do, no consequences.
- **D:** delegate.
- **E:** eliminate.

Never do a B while an A is left undone.

**Eat the ugliest frog first.** If you have two frogs, start with the bigger one.

**Don't stare at it too long.** Start before you feel ready. Momentum builds once you begin.

## Use it with clients

- Owners often start the day with email and easy tasks, then the frog waits till tomorrow. Help them name the frog the night before.
- Use ABCDE on an owner's to-do list in a session. The D and E items are often the quickest wins.

## Try it yourself

Tonight, write tomorrow's frog on a sticky note. Do it before you open email in the morning.

*By Brian Moran and Michael Lennington*

**The big idea:** Annual goals feel far away, so we put them off until the last quarter. Treat every 12 weeks as a full year and you get a month's worth of progress every week.

## Key ideas

**Urgency all year round.** In a 12-month year, December feels miles away in March. In a 12-week year, the deadline is always close, so you act now.

**Own the result.** Commit fully. One way is to bet on yourself: tell a friend "In 12 weeks I'll hit this goal, or I'll pay you £100."

**Plan weekly actions.** Break the 12-week goal into a short list of actions to do each week. Keep the plan simple and focused on what drives the result.

**Keep score of execution.** Each week, score the percentage of planned actions you completed. Aim for 85% or more. Track what you do, not just the outcome, because actions lead results.

**Hold a weekly review.** Spend 15 minutes each week checking your score and planning the next week. Better still, do it with a small group who hold each other to account.

**Take a week off.** After 12 weeks, rest and reflect before starting the next.

## Use it with clients

- This fits a 90-day planning rhythm perfectly. Set the owner's 12-week goals, then the weekly actions behind them.
- Score execution in every session: what percentage of planned actions got done? It shifts the conversation from excuses to behaviour.

## Try it yourself

Set one 12-week goal. List three weekly actions that drive it, then score yourself every Friday.

*By Tim Ferriss*

**The big idea:** About 20% of your work produces 80% of your results. Cut, delegate or automate the rest and you can do more in far less time.

## Key ideas

**Use the 80/20 rule.** Find the 20% of activities and customers that create most of your results. Find the 20% that cause most of your headaches. Do more of the first and less of the second.

**Ask "Is this the best use of my time?"** Does it make money? Must I do it? Do I enjoy it? If not, it's an 80% task.

**Let it go.** Run small "ignorance experiments": stop doing tasks with small, reversible consequences and see what happens. Many tasks nobody misses.

**Parkinson's law.** Work expands to fill the time available. Set short deadlines and the work shrinks to fit.

**Delegate and automate.** Hand off repeatable tasks with clear instructions. Automate what software can do.

**Batch and limit checking.** Check email at set times rather than constantly.

## Use it with clients

- Do an 80/20 review of the owner's customers. Which few bring most of the profit? Which few cause most of the problems?
- Help owners list tasks they do that someone else could do for less. Start delegating the easiest one.
- Set tighter deadlines on internal projects.

## Try it yourself

List your activities from last week. Mark the 20% that drove most of your results and pick one 80% task to stop, delegate or automate.

*By Jake Knapp and John Zeratsky*

**The big idea:** Two forces eat your time: the Busy Bandwagon (endless tasks) and Infinity Pools (endless distraction). A simple daily system helps you make time for what matters.

## Key ideas

**The Busy Bandwagon.** We wear "busy" like a badge, rushing from email to meeting to to-do list.

**Infinity Pools.** If you can pull to refresh or it streams, it's an Infinity Pool. Apps are designed to be easy and addictive. The average person spends four-plus hours a day on their phone.

**The daily system:**
1. **Highlight.** Choose one thing each day you want to make time for. Pick something urgent, satisfying or joyful.
2. **Laser.** Beat distraction. Log out of apps, remove email from your phone and put friction between you and Infinity Pools.
3. **Energise.** Move, eat real food, get daylight and sleep well. Energy makes focus possible.
4. **Reflect.** Each evening, note what worked and adjust tomorrow.

**Small experiments.** Try different tactics and keep what works for you.

## Use it with clients

- Ask owners for their daily highlight. One meaningful thing each day adds up to a big quarter.
- Help owners add friction to their main distractions: email notifications, news apps and social feeds.

## Try it yourself

Write tomorrow's highlight tonight and block 60 to 90 minutes for it. Delete one Infinity Pool app from your phone for a week.

*By Daniel Pink*

**The big idea:** Timing matters. Your attention rises and falls in a daily rhythm of peak, trough and rebound. Match the task to the time.

## Key ideas

**Beware the afternoon trough.** Between about 2pm and 4pm, doctors make more errors, students score lower and executives say silly things on earnings calls. The early afternoon is a danger zone.

**Peak (morning for most people).** Do analytical work: planning, writing, problem-solving, important decisions and sales calls.

**Trough (early afternoon).** Do admin and routine tasks: email, filing, expenses.

**Rebound (late afternoon).** Mood lifts and the mind loosens. Do creative work and brainstorming.

**Night owls flip it.** About one in five people peak later in the day. Know your type.

**Take real breaks.** Short breaks, especially outside, moving or with other people, restore focus. A short nap of 10 to 20 minutes can help too.

**Start strong and finish well.** Beginnings and endings shape how we feel about a project. Use fresh starts like a new month or quarter to reset.

## Use it with clients

- Help owners move sales calls and big decisions to their peak and push admin into the trough.
- Avoid scheduling important client meetings in the early afternoon slump.

## Try it yourself

Plan tomorrow by energy: hardest thinking work in your peak, admin in your trough, creative work in the rebound.

*By David Allen*

**The big idea:** Your mind is for having ideas, not holding them. Build a trusted system outside your head and you get a clear mind and full focus on the task in front of you.

## Key ideas

**Capture everything.** Every task, idea and commitment goes into an inbox you trust: an app, notebook or tray. "There's no reason to ever have the same thought twice."

**Clarify.** Process the inbox regularly. For each item, ask "Is it actionable?" If not, bin it, file it or park it in "someday/maybe". If yes, what's the very next physical action?

**The two-minute rule.** If the next action takes less than two minutes, do it now.

**Organise.** Put actions on lists by context (calls, computer, errands), projects on a projects list, and dated items in the calendar.

**Reflect weekly.** Once a week, review every list, clear your inboxes and decide what matters next. The weekly review keeps the system trustworthy.

**Engage.** Pick what to do based on context, time, energy and priority.

## Use it with clients

- Owners carry hundreds of open loops in their heads. Help them do a full brain dump and set up one simple capture tool.
- A weekly review is one of the most valuable habits an owner can build. Make it part of their rhythm.

## Try it yourself

Spend 30 minutes writing down every open loop in your head. Then process each one: do it, bin it, delegate it or schedule it.

*Inspired by Getting Things Done by David Allen*

**The big idea:** One huge to-do list breeds anxiety and procrastination. Sort tasks into "batch buckets" by context and empty each bucket when you're in the right place and mood.

## Key ideas

**Long lists create stress.** Your mind thinks everything on the list needs doing now. The longer the list, the more you avoid it.

**Sort by context and mode.** Create separate lists for different situations:
- **Calls:** make them on the commute or between meetings.
- **Deep work:** writing and planning, in a quiet focused block.
- **Errands:** do them all in one trip.
- **Admin:** email and expenses in a low-energy slot.

**Batch similar tasks.** Switching between different kinds of work costs time and energy. Doing similar tasks together keeps you in one mode.

**As many buckets as you need, as few as you can manage.**

## Use it with clients

- Owners often jump between sales, admin, people issues and delivery all day. Help them batch similar work into blocks.
- Suggest a calls bucket emptied at a set time each day.

## Try it yourself

Split your to-do list into three or four buckets by context. Empty one bucket in a single sitting today.

*By Ryder Carroll*

**The big idea:** A simple notebook can become your planner, to-do list and journal in one. Writing by hand slows you down enough to think about what really matters.

## Key ideas

**The index.** The first pages list where everything is, so you can find it later.

**The future log.** A few pages for tasks and events in coming months.

**The monthly log.** At the start of each month, list the dates on one page and tasks on the next. Move over anything still relevant from last month.

**The daily log.** Each day, jot quick bullets: tasks (•), events (○) and notes (–). Mark done tasks with an X.

**Migration.** At the end of each month, look at every open task. If it still matters, rewrite it in the new month. If not, cross it out. Rewriting by hand makes you question whether a task is worth it.

**Reflect.** Use the journal to look back at what you did and how you felt.

## Use it with clients

- Some owners hate apps. A bullet journal gives them a simple, analogue system that sticks.
- Monthly migration is a great habit for any owner: if a task isn't worth rewriting, it probably isn't worth doing.

## Try it yourself

Set up a monthly log in a notebook for next month. At month end, migrate only the tasks that still matter.

by: Greg McKeown

*By Greg McKeown*

**The big idea:** Important work doesn't have to be hard. Make essential work easier and you get more done with energy left over.

## Key ideas

Use the acronym D.D.O.G.G.:

**Done: define what done looks like.** A clear finish line relaxes your mind. "Done" might be hitting send on an email or uploading a video. Picture the moment of completion for 60 seconds.

**Delete: cut unneeded steps.** Ask "What steps can I remove?" Most tasks have parts that add little value.

**Obvious: make the first step obvious.** Name the very first physical action. Vague tasks feel heavy. Clear first steps feel light.

**Gradual: go slow to go fast.** Set a lower and upper limit for daily progress, like at least 10 minutes and no more than 60. Steady progress beats bursts and burnout.

**Grateful: pair the task with appreciation.** Gratitude lowers stress and makes work feel lighter.

**Ask "What if this could be easy?"** Effort isn't a measure of value.

## Use it with clients

- When an owner is putting off a big project, like a new website or a pricing review, define "done" and the first obvious step together.
- Encourage owners to ask "What if this could be easy?" when they design systems for the team.

## Try it yourself

Pick a project you've been avoiding. Write what done looks like, delete one step and name the first obvious action. Do that action today.

*By Marie Kondo and Scott Sonenshein*

**The big idea:** Clutter, physical and digital, splits your attention and raises stress. Tidy your workspace, files, time and meetings so what's left sparks joy and helps you work.

## Key ideas

**Clutter drains focus.** When your brain is busy noticing everything around you, it can't focus on the work in front of you.

**Tidy by category, all in one go.** Don't tidy a little each day. Do one category at a time, quickly and completely: papers, books, desk items, then digital files.

**Keep what sparks joy or serves a purpose.** Hold each item and ask "Does this spark joy, or does it help me work?" If not, thank it and let it go.

**Tidy your digital space.** Clear your desktop, organise folders simply and unsubscribe from emails you never read.

**Tidy your time.** List your activities and meetings. Keep what's essential or joyful. Cut or shorten the rest.

**Tidy your meetings.** Only attend meetings where you add value. Keep them short with a clear purpose.

## Use it with clients

- An owner's diary is often the most cluttered space they have. Help them tidy their recurring meetings.
- A cluttered shared drive slows everyone down. Encourage a one-off tidy with a simple folder structure.

## Try it yourself

Tidy one category this week, such as your computer desktop or your recurring meetings. Keep only what sparks joy or serves a purpose.

*By Caroline Webb*

**The big idea:** Behavioural science can make your everyday working life better. A morning intention, a few checks during the day and a nightly recap add up to more good days.

## Key ideas

**Morning intention.** Look at the day ahead and ask:
- "What matters most today?" Pick one or two key outcomes.
- "What does that mean for my attitude, attention and actions?"
- "What could get in the way, and how will I handle it?"

**Block focus time.** Schedule uninterrupted blocks with notifications off for your key outcomes.

**Check in during the day.**
- **Feeling defensive?** Breathe deeply for 90 seconds.
- **Resisting a task?** Ask "What bigger goal does this serve?"
- **Annoyed by someone?** Ask "Could they just be tired, hungry or under pressure?"

**Nightly recap.** Write three things that went well, and what you learned. Your brain starts to notice progress.

**Sleep and breaks matter.** A tired brain sees threats everywhere.

## Use it with clients

- Teach owners the three morning questions as a two-minute start to the day.
- When an owner is frustrated with a team member, try the "tired, hungry or under pressure" question before reacting.

## Try it yourself

Tomorrow, set your morning intention in two minutes and do the nightly recap before bed.
