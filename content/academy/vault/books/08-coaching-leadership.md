*By Michael Bungay Stanier*

**The big idea:** Say less, ask more. Your advice isn't as good as you think. A handful of great questions help people find their own answers, and they own those answers.

## Key ideas

**Tame your advice monster.** When someone shares a problem, the urge to jump in with a solution is strong. Hold back and stay curious a little longer.

**Seven questions:**
1. **"What's on your mind?"** Skips the small talk and goes straight to what matters.
2. **"And what else?"** The first answer is rarely the only one, or the best one. Ask it two or three times.
3. **"What's the real challenge here for you?"** Focuses on the true problem, and on the person.
4. **"What do you want?"** Clarifies what they're actually after.
5. **"How can I help?"** Makes them state the request clearly.
6. **"If you're saying yes to this, what are you saying no to?"** Forces real choices.
7. **"What was most useful for you?"** Locks in the learning at the end.

**Ask one question at a time** and then be quiet.

## Use it with clients

- Use these questions in your own sessions. "And what else?" alone often uncovers the real issue.
- Teach owners to coach their team with the same questions instead of solving every problem themselves. It's one of the fastest ways to free up an owner's time.

## Try it yourself

In your next three conversations, ask "And what else?" at least twice before offering any advice.

*By Ken Blanchard and Spencer Johnson*

**The big idea:** Don't be a dictator or a hands-off manager. Three simple habits get results and build strong relationships.

## Key ideas

**One-minute goals.** Each week, agree a few clear goals with each person:
1. Ask what they hope to complete this week.
2. Ask which one goal would have the biggest impact.
3. Get them to describe it clearly.
4. Have them write it down in a short note they can reread in a minute.

**One-minute praisings.** Catch people doing something right. Praise them straight away, say exactly what they did well and how it helps, pause, then encourage them to keep going.

**One-minute redirects.** When something goes wrong, address it quickly. Say what went wrong and how you feel about it. Pause. Then remind them they're better than this mistake and you have confidence in them.

**People who feel good about themselves produce good results.**

## Use it with clients

- Many owners give feedback only when something goes wrong. Introduce one-minute praisings to shift the culture.
- Help owners run weekly one-minute goal-setting with key staff, so priorities are clear without micromanaging.

## Try it yourself

Give three one-minute praisings this week: be specific, say why it matters, and encourage more of it.

*By Jocko Willink and Leif Babin*

**The big idea:** Two Navy SEAL commanders say there are no bad teams, only bad leaders. Effective leaders take full ownership of everything in their world, including others' mistakes.

## Key ideas

**Own everything.** When things go wrong, great leaders don't blame. They ask "What could I have done better?" Ownership spreads through the team.

**Believe in the mission.** If you don't understand or believe in the "why", you can't lead others to it. Ask questions until you do.

**Keep it simple.** Complex plans fail under pressure. Make sure everyone understands the plan in plain words.

**Prioritise and execute.** When overwhelmed, pick the most important problem, solve it, then move to the next.

**Decentralise command.** Give small teams clear intent, the "why" behind the plan, so they can make decisions without waiting for orders. If snipers find the planned rooftop has no cover, they can choose a better spot because they understand the goal.

**Check your ego.** Ego blocks ownership and learning.

## Use it with clients

- When an owner blames the team, gently ask what they could have done differently: clearer instructions, better training, better hiring.
- Help owners share the "why" behind decisions so staff can act without asking every time.

## Try it yourself

Think of something that went wrong recently. Write down three things you could have done to prevent it, without blaming anyone else.

*By Patrick Lencioni*

**The big idea:** Teams fail for five linked reasons, each building on the one below. Fix them from the bottom up, starting with trust.

## Key ideas

**1. Absence of trust.** People hide weaknesses and mistakes. Build trust by being open about your own. Try a simple exercise: each person shares something about their background, like their first job or biggest challenge growing up.

**2. Fear of conflict.** Without trust, people avoid honest debate. Meetings stay polite and dull. Encourage healthy disagreement about ideas.

**3. Lack of commitment.** If people haven't voiced their views, they don't buy in. End meetings by confirming exactly what was decided.

**4. Avoiding accountability.** Without commitment, people won't call each other out. Make goals and standards public so peers hold each other to account.

**5. Inattention to results.** People focus on their own status or department. Keep the team's shared results visible.

**The leader goes first.** Owners must show vulnerability, invite debate and hold people accountable.

## Use it with clients

- When an owner says "My team won't take responsibility", walk down the pyramid. The real issue is often trust or commitment.
- Help owners end meetings with a clear summary of decisions and owners.

## Try it yourself

At your next team or client meeting, finish by asking "What exactly have we agreed, and who owns each part?"

*By Kerry Patterson, Joseph Grenny, Ron McMillan and Al Switzler*

**The big idea:** A crucial conversation has high stakes, strong emotions and different views. Keep the dialogue going and you can work through almost anything.

## Key ideas

**Spot when it's crucial.** Chasing an unpaid invoice, raising a missed promotion or confronting a colleague who isn't pulling their weight.

**People go silent or violent.** Under pressure, we either withdraw or attack. Both kill dialogue.

**Start with facts, not conclusions.** Use "When... I...": "When the report arrived two days late, I worried we'd miss the client deadline." Then invite their side: "What happened from your view?"

**Make it safe.** People open up when they feel respected and believe you care about their goals. If safety breaks, step out, restore respect and come back.

**Find a shared goal.** "We both want this client to stay happy."

**Agree what happens next.** Who does what by when, and how you'll follow up.

## Use it with clients

- Owners often avoid crucial conversations with underperforming staff, partners or late-paying clients. Role-play using "When... I..." before the real conversation.
- Help owners find the shared goal before a tough conversation.

## Try it yourself

Think of a conversation you've been avoiding. Write your opening using "When... I..." and one question that invites their side.

*By Adam Grant*

**The big idea:** People are takers, matchers or givers. Givers end up at both the bottom and the top. The difference is how they give.

## Key ideas

**Three styles.**
- **Takers** give only when they expect more back.
- **Matchers** give as much as they get.
- **Givers** give more than they expect to receive.

**Givers win and lose.** In a study of engineers, the least productive were mostly givers. So were the most productive. Takers and matchers landed in the middle.

**Why successful givers win.** They build strong networks, earn trust and help others perform. Their reputation opens doors.

**Why some givers lose.** They give to everyone, all the time, and burn out. Successful givers protect their time, give in focused bursts and watch out for takers.

**Spot takers.** Takers are often charming upwards and dismissive downwards. Watch how people treat those with less power.

## Use it with clients

- Generous owners often say yes to everything and burn out. Help them give in ways that also fit their goals and protect their time.
- Use the taker test in hiring: ask how candidates treated junior colleagues, and check with the receptionist.

## Try it yourself

Do one act of generosity this week that plays to your strengths, like an introduction, advice or a resource, without expecting anything back.

by: Inspired by Give and Take by Adam Grant

*Inspired by Give and Take by Adam Grant*

**The big idea:** "You should be willing to do something that takes five minutes or less for anybody." Adam Rifkin was once named the world's best networker by Fortune. His secret is the five-minute favour.

## Key ideas

**Small favours, big network.** Rifkin, a shy entrepreneur, built a huge network by doing quick favours for anyone, with no strings attached.

**Five-minute favours include:** an introduction, feedback on an idea, sharing a useful article, writing a short recommendation or answering a question.

**Ask people to pay it forward.** Rifkin only asks that people help someone else in return. Generosity spreads.

**Reputation pays dividends.** People trust givers. Opportunities, referrals and support come back, often from unexpected places.

**Keep it small.** Five minutes keeps generosity sustainable and protects your time.

## Use it with clients

- For coaches and advisors, five-minute favours are one of the best ways to build referrals. Introduce owners to useful contacts.
- Encourage owners to build the same habit with suppliers, partners and peers.

## Try it yourself

Do one five-minute favour every day this week: an introduction, a recommendation or a useful link sent to someone in your network.

*By Peter Drucker*

**The big idea:** Effectiveness can be learned. Focus on your contribution: know your strengths and know where your time goes.

## Key ideas

**Know your time.** Most people don't know where their time goes. Track it for a few weeks. Then cut what doesn't need doing, delegate what others can do, and protect large blocks for important work.

**Know your strengths.** "We are all incompetent at most things." Ask what you do uncommonly well and build on it. Use a feedback analysis: write down what you expect from a project, then compare it with the results 6 to 12 months later.

**Focus on contribution.** Ask "What can I contribute that will make a real difference?" instead of "How busy am I?"

**Do first things first, one at a time.** Concentrate on a few priorities.

**Make effective decisions.** Understand the real problem, define what success looks like, and turn the decision into action with clear owners.

## Use it with clients

- Have owners track their time for two weeks. The results almost always reveal hours to cut or delegate.
- Help owners build their role around their strengths and hire for their weaknesses.

## Try it yourself

Track your time in 30-minute blocks for one week. Mark each block as "contribution", "delegate" or "cut".

*By John Doerr*

**The big idea:** OKRs (Objectives and Key Results) helped Google, Intel and others grow fast. Set an inspiring objective and three to five measurable results that show you're getting there.

## Key ideas

**Objective = what.** An inspiring goal. Make it ambitious but believable. The Gates Foundation first aimed to end malaria by 2015, realised it was impossible and moved the date to keep the team motivated.

**Key results = how you'll know.** Three to five measurable results with numbers and deadlines. If you hit them all, you've met the objective.

**Focus on a few.** Pick three to five objectives at most. Focus is the point.

**Make them visible.** Share OKRs across the team so everyone sees how their work connects to the bigger goals.

**Track and adjust.** Check progress weekly. Change key results if the situation changes.

**Stretch.** Hitting about 70% of a stretch goal often means you aimed well.

## Use it with clients

- OKRs fit 90-day planning well. Help owners turn their quarterly priorities into one objective with three measurable key results.
- Make the team's OKRs visible on a simple scoreboard.

## Try it yourself

Write one objective for the next quarter with three key results that have numbers and dates.

*By Chris McChesney, Sean Covey and Jim Huling*

**The big idea:** The enemy of execution is the "whirlwind", the urgent day-to-day work that eats all your time. Four disciplines help teams hit important goals despite it.

## Key ideas

**1. Focus on the wildly important.** Pick one or two "wildly important goals" (WIGs). Write them as "from X to Y by when": "From £80k to £100k monthly revenue by December."

**2. Act on lead measures.** Lag measures (like revenue) tell you if you've hit the goal, but too late to change it. Lead measures (like sales calls made) predict the result and you can control them. Track and push the lead measures.

**3. Keep a compelling scoreboard.** A simple, visible scoreboard shows the team whether they're winning. People play differently when they keep score.

**4. Create a rhythm of accountability.** Hold a short weekly meeting where each person reports on last week's commitments, reviews the scoreboard and makes one or two new commitments.

## Use it with clients

- This fits owner-led businesses well. Help the owner pick one WIG, find two lead measures and set up a weekly 20-minute WIG meeting.
- When an owner says the team is too busy for new goals, name the whirlwind and protect a small amount of time each week for the WIG.

## Try it yourself

Write one wildly important goal as "from X to Y by when". Identify two lead measures you can track weekly.

*By Jeff Sutherland*

**The big idea:** Scrum is a way to run projects in short cycles called sprints. Plan, build, check and improve, again and again, instead of following one long plan.

## Key ideas

**Make a backlog.** List everything the project could deliver, ordered by value. Write each item from the user's view: "As a [who], I want [what], so that [why]."

**Work in sprints.** Pick the top items you can finish in one to four weeks. Commit to them as a team.

**Hold a daily stand-up.** Fifteen minutes each day: What did I do yesterday? What will I do today? What's in my way?

**Show the work.** At the end of the sprint, demo what you've finished to the people who will use it and get feedback.

**Look back.** Ask what went well, what didn't and what to change next sprint.

**Remove blockers fast.** The team's biggest job is clearing what slows them down.

**Small teams win.** Teams of three to nine work best.

## Use it with clients

- When an owner runs big projects like a new website, system or product, suggest two-week sprints with a demo at the end of each one.
- A daily 15-minute stand-up can transform a disorganised team.

## Try it yourself

Pick a project. Write its backlog, choose what you can finish in two weeks, and review progress at the end of the fortnight.

*By Atul Gawande*

**The big idea:** What we need to know has outgrown our ability to remember it. Simple checklists help even experts avoid avoidable mistakes.

## Key ideas

**Experts still make simple errors.** Surgeon Atul Gawande found avoidable mistakes, like skipped hand-washing, happen often in operating theatres. The fix came from aviation: the checklist.

**Checklists work.** A short surgical checklist led to big drops in complications and deaths across hospitals that tried it.

**Two types:**
- **Do-confirm:** do the work from memory, then pause and check you didn't miss anything.
- **Read-do:** read each step and do it, like a recipe.

**Keep them short.** Five to nine items. Only the critical steps people tend to miss. Simple, clear wording.

**Checklists build teamwork.** Include steps that make people talk, like introducing everyone and agreeing the plan before starting.

**Test and improve.** Try the checklist in real situations and refine it.

## Use it with clients

- Most owner-led businesses run on the owner's memory. Help them turn key processes, like onboarding a client or quoting, into short checklists their team can follow.
- Checklists are one of the quickest ways for an owner to delegate with confidence.

## Try it yourself

Pick a process you repeat often. Write a five to nine-step checklist for the steps most likely to be missed and use it next time.

*Talk by Dan Pink, TEDGlobal*

**The big idea:** There's a gap between what science knows and what business does. Bonuses and incentives work for simple tasks but hurt performance on creative work. Autonomy, mastery and purpose work better.

## Key ideas

**The candle problem.** In a classic experiment, people offered money to solve a creative puzzle took longer than those offered nothing. Rewards narrow focus, which helps with simple tasks and hurts with tasks that need creative thinking.

**Incentives backfire on thinking work.** Study after study shows that for tasks needing even basic thinking, bigger rewards often lead to worse performance.

**Autonomy works.** Companies that give people control over their work, like Atlassian's "FedEx days" (deliver something new overnight) and results-only work environments, see more creativity and output.

**Mastery and purpose.** People want to get better at things that matter and to be part of something bigger than themselves.

## Use it with clients

- Watch this with owners before they design a new bonus scheme for creative or client-facing roles.
- Help owners try a small autonomy experiment, like a day for staff to fix a problem they choose.

## Try it yourself

Ask one team member, or yourself, where more autonomy would improve the work. Try it for a month.
