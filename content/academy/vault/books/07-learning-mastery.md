*By Josh Kaufman*

**The big idea:** You don't need 10,000 hours to get good at something. About 20 hours of smart practice gets you from knowing nothing to performing noticeably well.

## Key ideas

**Decide what "good enough" looks like.** Be specific about what you want to be able to do. It tells you what to practise and what to skip.

**Break the skill into parts.** Split it into the smallest sub-skills. Find the few that matter most for a beginner and practise those first.

**Learn enough to self-correct.** Read just enough to spot your own mistakes, then start practising. Don't hide in research.

**Remove barriers to practice.** Clear distractions and set up everything you need in advance, so starting is easy.

**Practise for 20 hours.** The first few hours feel awkward. Commit to 20 hours upfront so you push through the frustration barrier.

## Use it with clients

- Owners often avoid skills like reading a P&L, public speaking or using a new CRM because they assume it'll take forever. Show them 20 focused hours will get them competent.
- Help owners break a team member's new skill into sub-skills and train the critical few first.

## Try it yourself

Pick one skill your business needs from you. Write what "good enough" looks like, list three critical sub-skills and book your first practice hour.

*By Scott Young*

**The big idea:** Ultralearning is self-directed, intense learning. Scott Young learned MIT's four-year computer science course in a year. Anyone can use the same strategies.

## Key ideas

**Make a learning map (metalearning).** Spend the first 10% of your time working out what to learn. What concepts do you need to understand, what facts to memorise and what procedures to practise? Be specific about the end goal.

**Focus.** Work in focused sessions. Start before you feel ready and stay with it through the discomfort.

**Learn directly.** Practise in the real situation as soon as possible. To learn to sell, sell. To learn a language, speak it.

**Drill weak spots.** Find the part that holds you back most and practise it on its own.

**Test yourself.** Recalling information builds memory far better than rereading.

**Get feedback fast.** Seek honest, specific feedback, even when it stings.

**Experiment.** Once you have the basics, try new approaches to find your own style.

## Use it with clients

- When an owner needs a new skill fast, like pricing, recruiting or finance, help them build a short ultralearning plan with a clear end goal.
- Encourage learning by doing. Running real sales calls teaches more than another course.

## Try it yourself

Pick a skill to learn this month. Spend one hour mapping what you need to learn, then practise it directly in a real situation this week.

*By Anders Ericsson and Robert Pool*

**The big idea:** Experts aren't born. They're built through deliberate practice: focused, structured practice just beyond your current ability, with fast feedback.

## Key ideas

**Practice beats talent.** Ericsson trained a student, Steve Faloon, to remember 82 digits. Most people can hold about seven. Steve did it by practising the right way.

**Deliberate practice has key ingredients:**
- **Specific targets.** Not "get better" but "get from 39 digits to 40".
- **Intense focus.** Full attention for the whole session.
- **Immediate feedback.** Know right away what worked and what didn't.
- **Just beyond comfort.** Stretch slightly past your current level.
- **Mental models.** Build a clear picture of what good looks like.

**Naive practice doesn't work.** Repeating what you already do, without feedback or stretch, leads to a plateau. Years of experience don't guarantee skill.

**Find a teacher.** A good coach designs practice, spots errors and pushes you just beyond your limits.

## Use it with clients

- Owners and their teams often have years of experience but stopped improving long ago. Introduce deliberate practice for key skills like sales calls: specific targets, recordings and feedback.
- Help owners see that "we've always done it this way" is naive practice.

## Try it yourself

Record your next sales call or presentation. Pick one specific thing to improve and practise only that before the next one.

*By Peter Brown, Henry Roediger and Mark McDaniel*

**The big idea:** Rereading and cramming feel productive but don't work. Learning that feels harder lasts longer.

## Key ideas

**Easy learning fades.** Rereading makes material feel familiar, so you think you know it. Learning that's easy is like writing in sand.

**Quiz yourself.** Every 15 minutes of reading or 30 minutes of audio, pause and ask: What were the key ideas? Which were new? How can I use them? Pulling information out of your memory strengthens it.

**Space it out.** Review material over days and weeks, not all at once. A little forgetting between reviews makes the memory stronger.

**Mix it up.** Practise different skills or problem types in one session instead of blocks of the same thing. It feels slower but builds deeper skill.

**Explain it.** Teaching an idea in your own words shows what you really understand.

**Connect it.** Link new ideas to what you already know.

## Use it with clients

- When owners train their team, swap long one-off sessions for short quizzes and spaced reviews.
- Encourage owners to explain new ideas back to you in their own words. If they can't, they don't own it yet.

## Try it yourself

After this lesson, close it and write the three key ideas from memory. Review them again in three days.

*By Barbara Oakley*

**The big idea:** Barbara Oakley failed maths at school and later became an engineering professor. Your brain has two modes of thinking, and great learners switch between them.

## Key ideas

**Focused mode.** Concentrated attention on a problem. Good for working through familiar steps.

**Diffuse mode.** A relaxed, wandering state, like a walk, a shower or sleep. Your brain makes new connections in the background. Many breakthroughs come here.

**Switch between the two.** Work hard on a problem, then step away. When you come back, the answer often appears.

**Chunk it.** Group information into meaningful chunks through focus, understanding and practice. Chunks free up working memory for harder ideas.

**Beat procrastination with process.** Focus on time spent, not the finished product. Use 25-minute focused sessions with short breaks.

**Test yourself and space it out.** Recall beats rereading. Spread practice over several days.

**Sleep consolidates learning.** Learn before sleep and review the next day.

## Use it with clients

- Owners trying to solve big strategic problems often grind at their desk. Encourage focused work followed by a walk or a night's sleep before deciding.
- Use 25-minute sessions for tasks owners avoid, like updating financial forecasts.

## Try it yourself

Work on a hard problem for 25 minutes, then go for a walk without your phone. Write down any ideas that come.

*By Kevin Horsley*

**The big idea:** Memory isn't something that happens to you. You create it. Kevin Horsley thought he had a bad memory, then learned simple techniques and memorised 10,000 digits of pi.

## Key ideas

**Bring information to life.** Turn dull facts into vivid pictures. To remember the name Horsley, picture a horse.

**Use your senses.** Imagine touching, hearing and smelling the image. The more senses, the stronger the memory.

**Exaggerate.** Make the image huge, colourful, funny or strange. A giant pink horse sticks better than a normal one.

**Use a journey (memory palace).** Place images along a route you know well, like rooms in your house. To recall them, walk the route in your mind.

**Remember names.** Focus on the name, turn it into a picture, and link that picture to a feature of the person's face.

**Pay attention.** Most "bad memory" is really poor attention. Decide to remember.

## Use it with clients

- Remembering names and details builds trust. Teach owners the name technique for networking events and client meetings.
- Use a memory palace to remember the key points of a talk without notes.

## Try it yourself

At your next meeting or event, use the name technique for every new person you meet. Test yourself afterwards.

*By Jim Kwik*

**The big idea:** If you struggle to learn, it's down to a limiting mindset, low motivation or poor methods. Fix all three and your brain can do far more than you think.

## Key ideas

**Mindset: drop limiting beliefs.** "When you fight for your limitations, you get to keep them." Act as though your abilities can grow.

**Motivation: find your why.** Clear purpose, enough energy and small, simple steps keep you going.

**Methods: learn how to learn.** Better techniques beat more effort.

**Read faster with the 4-3-2-1 drill.** Read with your finger under the words for four minutes and mark where you stop. Then read the same section in three minutes, then two, then one, pushing to reach the same spot. Finish by reading new material for four minutes at a comfortable pace. Most people read noticeably faster afterwards.

**Use a guide.** Moving a finger or pen under the line keeps your eyes moving and stops you rereading.

**Fuel your brain.** Sleep, exercise, good food and clear surroundings all improve learning.

## Use it with clients

- Owners often say they're "not a reader" or "bad with numbers". Challenge the limiting belief and give them a simple method to start.
- Suggest the 4-3-2-1 drill to owners who want to get through more reading.

## Try it yourself

Do the 4-3-2-1 reading drill once today and note your reading speed before and after.

*By Josh Waitzkin*

**The big idea:** Josh Waitzkin became a chess champion and then a Tai Chi world champion. He found the same learning principles work for any skill.

## Key ideas

**Feel the fundamentals.** Josh learned chess with just a king and pawns, then added one piece at a time until each felt natural. Deep basics free your mind to see the bigger picture.

**Make smaller circles.** Practise a small technique again and again until you understand it deeply. Then expand. Depth beats breadth.

**Learn from losses.** Waitzkin studied his defeats closely. A growth mindset treats losses as lessons.

**Use triggers to reach your best state.** Build a routine that puts you into a calm, focused state on demand, then shorten it until you can switch in seconds.

**Stay present under pressure.** Accept distractions and use them, instead of needing perfect conditions.

## Use it with clients

- Help owners master the basics of their business, like cash flow, pipeline and delivery, before chasing advanced strategies.
- Review losses properly. A lost deal or unhappy client is a lesson, not just a bad day.

## Try it yourself

Pick one basic skill in your work and practise a small part of it deeply this week, like your first two minutes on a sales call.

*By Tim Ferriss*

**The big idea:** Using cooking as the example, Tim Ferriss shares a recipe for learning anything fast: DiSSS (Deconstruction, Selection, Sequencing, Stakes).

## Key ideas

**Deconstruction.** Break the skill into small parts. Read normal and unusual guides. Ask experts: "If I had 20% of the ideal training time, what should I focus on?" and "What do beginners waste time on?"

**Selection.** Find the 20% of material that gives 80% of the results. Focus only on that.

**Sequencing.** Learn in the right order. Sometimes the usual order isn't the best one.

**Stakes.** Create real consequences to keep you on track. Bet money with a friend or make a public commitment.

**Compress it.** Summarise the key ideas on one page so you can review them fast.

## Use it with clients

- When an owner needs to get up to speed fast, like hiring their first manager or learning digital marketing, help them run DiSSS: interview experts, pick the vital 20%, and set stakes.
- Encourage owners to put the essentials of key processes on one page.

## Try it yourself

Pick a skill. Ask one expert the two Ferriss questions this week and write their answers on a one-page cheat sheet.

*By Daniel Coyle*

**The big idea:** Talent isn't born. It's grown through deep practice, ignition and good coaching. Daniel Coyle visited "talent hotbeds" that produce stars and found the same patterns.

## Key ideas

**Deep practice.** People in talent hotbeds work at the edge of their ability, make mistakes, fix them and repeat. Struggle is how skill grows.

**Myelin builds skill.** Each time you practise correctly, your brain wraps the circuit in a substance called myelin, which makes it faster and more accurate.

**Ignition.** Motivation comes from a spark, often a moment where someone sees "I could be like them". A small Moscow tennis club produced more top-ranked women players than the whole US for a time, partly because players saw stars just ahead of them.

**Master coaching.** Great coaches know the skill deeply, watch closely and give short, specific corrections.

**Slow it down.** Practising slowly helps you notice and fix errors.

## Use it with clients

- Help owners create ignition for their team by sharing success stories from people just a step ahead.
- Encourage owners to give short, specific feedback rather than long lectures.

## Try it yourself

Pick a skill and practise it slowly for 15 minutes today, fixing each mistake as you go.

*By Ron Friedman*

**The big idea:** There's a third path to greatness beyond talent and practice: reverse engineering. Study great work, decode the blueprint and use it to leap ahead.

## Key ideas

**Collect great examples.** Masters collect other masterpieces. David Bowie collected records. Van Gogh owned over 1,000 Japanese prints. Exposure to great work helps you spot patterns without trying.

**Decode the blueprint.** Take great work apart. Outline a great article. Map the structure of a great sales page. Look for patterns across several examples.

**Copy, then change.** Imitate the structure first. Then adapt it with your own ideas and style.

**Use a scorecard.** Measure what matters and track your progress against the masters.

**Take smart risks.** Try ideas in small, safe ways before committing.

## Use it with clients

- When an owner needs a better website, proposal or onboarding process, collect five great examples and decode what they have in common.
- Help owners study the best businesses in their industry and adapt what works.

## Try it yourself

Collect three great examples of something you need to create, like a proposal or LinkedIn post. Outline the structure they share and use it for your next one.

*By Robert Greene*

**The big idea:** In a world of outsourcing and automation, mastery makes you hard to replace. Robert Greene studied masters through history and found a path anyone can follow.

## Key ideas

**Follow your primal curiosity.** When Einstein was five, a compass mesmerised him. That early curiosity fuelled his life's work. Notice what has always fascinated you.

**Go through an ideal apprenticeship.** Masters spend years learning deeply, often under a mentor. Value learning over money or status early on.

**Find a mentor.** Learn from someone ahead of you, absorb what they know, then move beyond them.

**Develop social intelligence.** See people as they are, not as you wish they were.

**Become creative.** Once you've mastered the basics, you can experiment and combine ideas in new ways.

**Mastery is intuition.** After years of deep practice, masters see patterns others miss.

## Use it with clients

- Help owners identify which skill they want to master and how it connects to their business's strengths.
- Encourage owners to mentor their key people, and to find mentors of their own.

## Try it yourself

Write down what has fascinated you since childhood. Ask how it connects to the work you do now.

*By David Epstein*

**The big idea:** In a complex world, generalists often beat specialists. Steve Nash, Tom Brady and Roger Federer all tried many sports before focusing on one.

## Key ideas

**Late specialisers win.** Many top performers have a "sampling period", trying lots of activities before narrowing down. They learn what suits them and build broad skills.

**Kind versus wicked environments.** In "kind" fields like chess, rules are clear and early specialisation works. In "wicked" fields like business, rules change and feedback is unclear. Broad experience helps there.

**Analogies spark solutions.** People with range borrow ideas from other fields to solve problems.

**Try things to find fit.** You don't find your path by thinking. You find it by testing. Short-term plans and experiments beat a fixed long-term plan.

**Struggle helps learning.** Hard, varied practice builds flexible skills.

## Use it with clients

- Owners often worry they lack a narrow specialism. Show them their broad experience is an advantage in complex business problems.
- Encourage owners to borrow ideas from other industries.

## Try it yourself

Pick a problem in your business. Ask how a different industry would solve it, such as hospitality, tech or sport.

*By Cal Newport*

**The big idea:** "Follow your passion" is bad advice. People who love their work build rare, valuable skills first. Passion follows mastery.

## Key ideas

**Passion mindset versus craftsman mindset.** The passion mindset asks "What can the world offer me?" and makes you notice what you dislike. The craftsman mindset asks "What can I offer the world?" and focuses on getting better.

**Build career capital.** Rare and valuable skills are your currency. Use them to "buy" the things people love about great jobs: autonomy, impact and creativity.

**Use deliberate practice.** Stretch yourself, get feedback and keep improving.

**Control comes with skill.** Ask for more autonomy once you've built capital, not before.

**Find a mission.** Big missions come once you're at the edge of your field and can see what's possible.

## Use it with clients

- Owners who've lost passion for their business often stopped building new skills. Help them find what to master next.
- Use the craftsman mindset with teams. Help staff build skills that earn them more autonomy.

## Try it yourself

Name one rare and valuable skill you could build this year. Plan one deliberate practice session for it this week.

*By Chris Guillebeau*

**The big idea:** Some people find work they seem born to do. They've won the career lottery: the right mix of joy, money and flow.

## Key ideas

**Joy, money and flow.** The best work gives you all three: work you enjoy, that pays well, and that uses your skills so time flies.

**Start a side hustle.** Test new ideas alongside your main work without big risk.

**Build an escape plan.** Save money, build skills and grow a network so you have options.

**Hack your job.** Make your current role better by asking for projects that suit you or changing how you work.

**Try lots of things.** Run small experiments to discover what fits.

## Use it with clients

- Owners who feel stuck often have the money but lost the joy or flow. Help them reshape their role around what energises them and delegate the rest.
- Encourage owners to test new business ideas as side projects before betting big.

## Try it yourself

Score your current work out of 10 for joy, money and flow. Pick one change that would raise your lowest score.
