*By Carol Dweck*

**The big idea:** People with a fixed mindset think ability is set at birth, so every challenge feels like a test they might fail. People with a growth mindset see a challenge as a chance to get better. The good news is that you can move from one to the other.

## Key ideas

**Your brain changes with use.** London taxi drivers who spent years learning the city's streets grew a bigger memory centre in the brain. Hard, focused practice builds faster brain circuits. Ability grows with effort.

**A fixed mindset avoids anything hard.** If you believe talent is fixed, struggling at something "proves" you lack the gift. So you avoid it, protect your image and stop learning.

**A growth mindset gets curious.** When something is hard, the growth mindset asks "What can I learn here?" and keeps going. Effort becomes the path to skill, not a sign of weakness.

**Praise the process.** Praising talent ("You're so clever") builds a fixed mindset. Praising effort and strategy ("You worked that out step by step") builds a growth one.

**Add the word "yet".** "I can't sell" becomes "I can't sell yet." One word turns a verdict into a starting point.

## Use it with clients

- Listen for fixed language from owners: "I'm not a numbers person" or "I'm no good at delegating." Add "yet" and ask what they would need to learn.
- When an owner reviews their team, ask whether they praise results or praise the way people got there.
- Treat a missed target as data. Ask "What did we learn?" before "Who got it wrong?"

## Try it yourself

Write down one skill you've told yourself you're "not good at". Add the word "yet", then pick one small step to practise it this week.

*Inspired by Little Bets by Peter Sims*

**The big idea:** How you read a failure decides what you do next. A fixed mindset hears "I am who I am." A growth mindset hears "I need to improve." Only the second one keeps you learning.

## Key ideas

**Fixed mindsets come from praise.** When people tie their identity to praise like "you're so smart", every setback threatens who they are. That creates constant stress and a need to keep proving themselves.

**Fixed mindsets pick the easy task.** Carol Dweck gave students a choice between an easy and a hard task. Around 90% of the growth-mindset students chose the hard one. Most fixed-mindset students chose the easy one.

**Fear of error blocks progress.** When you work hard to avoid every mistake, you also avoid the experiments that lead to new ideas.

**Two steps to switch.**
1. **Re-label.** When you think "I'm bad at this", rename the thought: "That's my fixed mindset talking."
2. **Recall.** Remember a time you got good at something through practice. Your ability grew then, and it can grow now.

## Use it with clients

- Owners who fear failure often avoid testing new offers, prices or hires. Frame each one as a small experiment with a clear lesson, not a verdict on them.
- When a test fails, help the owner write down what it taught them before deciding what to try next.

## Try it yourself

Next time you feel the sting of a setback, say "That's my fixed mindset" out loud. Then name one thing you can learn from it.

*By Ryan Holiday*

**The big idea:** Ego is the need to be better than, more than and recognised for, far past what is useful. It gets in your way when you start out, when you succeed and when you fail.

## Key ideas

**When you aspire, ego prefers talking to doing.** It is easy to post about plans, share big goals and enjoy the buzz. Ego swaps hype for action. Talk less and do the work.

**When you succeed, ego makes you stop learning.** Success tells you that you have it figured out. You stop listening and lose the habits that got you there. Stay a student, stay organised and keep your purpose in view.

**When you fail, ego blames others.** A setback becomes "unfair" or "sabotage". Ego protects your image instead of fixing the problem. Own the result, learn from it and move on.

**Keep a student mindset.** Always find someone ahead of you to learn from, someone level with you to push you, and someone behind you to teach.

## Use it with clients

- Owners who built a business on their own talent often struggle to hand over control. Gently ask whether a decision is about the business or about being needed.
- After a strong year, check for complacency. What habits, reviews or sales activity have slipped since things got easier?
- After a bad month, steer the conversation from blame to "What can we change?"

## Try it yourself

Pick one goal you've been talking about more than working on. Stop mentioning it for two weeks and spend that energy doing it.

*By Ryan Holiday*

**The big idea:** Every obstacle hides an opportunity. Control your reaction, look for the upside and act on it. The thing in your way can become the way forward.

## Key ideas

**Say "Good."** Terrible boss? Good, a chance to try new skills. Lost your work? Good, now you can make it better. This is a full mental flip, not forced positivity.

**Control your emotions first.** You cannot see the opportunity while you're panicking. Steady yourself, then look at the situation as it really is.

**Love your fate (amor fati).** Accept what happened, feel it, then decide to make the most of it. Wishing it were different wastes energy.

**Run a premortem.** Before a big project, imagine it has failed. List what went wrong. Plan for those problems now, so they don't knock you off course later.

## Use it with clients

- When an owner brings a crisis (a lost client, a key person leaving, a cash squeeze), give them space, then ask "What does this make possible?"
- A lost key employee is a chance to document the role and stop relying on one person.
- Use a premortem before big decisions like a new hire, a price rise or a launch. Ask "It's 12 months from now and this failed. Why?"

## Try it yourself

Write down your biggest current problem. Under it, write three ways it could make your business stronger.

*By Jane McGonigal*

**The big idea:** When we play a game, we choose the challenge, so we feel in control instead of threatened. Bring that "gameful" mindset to work and hard goals feel like quests instead of burdens.

## Key ideas

**Build self-efficacy with quests.** Self-efficacy is belief in your ability to solve a specific problem. Build it with small daily quests that move you towards a bigger goal. Each quest you finish adds confidence for the next.

**Keep score.** Tracking progress helps you see how far you've come, understand what works and enjoy the game. What gets scored gets attention.

**Use power-ups.** Power-ups are quick things that lift your energy or mood, such as a short walk, a glass of water or a call with a friend. Collect a few and use them when energy dips.

**Spot the bad guys.** Name what blocks you, such as distractions, self-doubt or tiredness. Once you name a "bad guy", you can plan how to beat it.

**Recruit allies.** Tell a few people about your quest and ask for help. Games are more fun with a team.

## Use it with clients

- Break an owner's big 90-day goal into weekly quests. Small wins build the confidence to tackle bigger ones.
- Turn their key numbers into a scoreboard they enjoy checking, not a report they dread.
- Ask "What is the bad guy this week?" to surface the real blocker quickly.

## Try it yourself

Pick one big goal. Set yourself three small quests to finish this week and give yourself a point for each one.

*By Amy Morin*

**The big idea:** Psychotherapist Amy Morin lost her mother and her husband within three years. She held on by remembering the habits that keep people stuck. Fix three of them and you fix most of the rest.

## Key ideas

**Drop the idea that the world owes you.** "I worked so hard, I don't deserve this" only adds anger. Mentally strong people shift their focus to helping others. When you're busy doing good, you stop keeping score.

**Focus on what you can control.** Draw a line across a page. Above it, write what you can't control. Below it, write what you can. Tear off the top half and throw it away. You can't control the past, other people or the future. You can control your focus, what things mean and how you act.

**Accept that change takes time.** Mentally strong people expect results to take longer than they want. They keep going instead of giving up when the payoff is slow.

**The other habits follow.** Wasting time on self-pity, giving away your power, fearing risk, dwelling on the past, repeating mistakes, resenting others' success, giving up after failure, fearing time alone and needing to please everyone all shrink once you master the first three.

## Use it with clients

- When an owner is stuck on something outside their control, like the economy or a competitor, do the line exercise with them. Then plan around what sits below the line.
- Owners often expect fast results from new systems. Agree a realistic timeline up front so they don't quit early.

## Try it yourself

Do the line exercise for your biggest worry. Tear off the top half and act on one item from the bottom half today.

*By Jordan Peterson*

**The big idea:** Life drifts into chaos when you lose your footing. Two rules bring order back fast: compare yourself to who you were yesterday, and tell the truth.

## Key ideas

**Compare yourself to yesterday, not to others.** Online you can always find someone more skilled and more successful. Constant comparison drains confidence and stops you acting. Instead, ask "Was I a better version of myself today than yesterday?" Rate yourself from 1 to 10. Small daily gains keep you moving.

**Tell the truth, or at least don't lie.** Lying to yourself about your work, your relationships or your results keeps you stuck. You can only improve on yesterday if you're honest about what yesterday was.

**Aim at something.** Pick a goal worth working towards. Even a small step towards a good aim gives life direction and meaning.

**Take on responsibility.** Meaning comes from carrying a load you choose. The more responsibility you take for your life, the more purpose you feel.

## Use it with clients

- Owners often compare themselves with bigger competitors and feel behind. Bring the focus back to their own progress: this quarter against last quarter.
- Create space for honest numbers. An owner who hides from the real margin or pipeline can't fix it.

## Try it yourself

At the end of each day this week, rate yourself out of 10 against yesterday. Write one thing you'll do better tomorrow.

*By Don Miguel Ruiz*

**The big idea:** Most of us carry an inner judge that punishes us for every mistake. Four simple agreements quiet that judge and free up the energy it wastes.

## Key ideas

**1. Be impeccable with your word.** Stop using words that harm you or others. "I'm an idiot" doesn't fix anything. At the end of the day, ask whether your words lifted people up or pulled them down.

**2. Don't take anything personally.** What people say and do reflects their own pain, not your worth. Think "poor them" instead of "why me?" You can still set boundaries and seek fairness.

**3. Don't make assumptions.** We fill gaps with stories, then react to the stories. Ask questions instead. Say what you want clearly.

**4. Always do your best.** Your best changes day to day. Tired-you has a different best from rested-you. Do your best for that moment and you'll have nothing to judge yourself for.

## Use it with clients

- Owners often take staff mistakes or client complaints personally. Agreement 2 helps them respond calmly and fix the system instead.
- Many team problems come from assumptions. Encourage owners to ask, check and spell out what "done" looks like.
- Use agreement 4 when owners beat themselves up after a hard week.

## Try it yourself

Pick one agreement and practise it for seven days. Note each time you catch yourself breaking it.

*By Tony Robbins*

**The big idea:** Take control of your life by deciding what you really want and breaking the limiting beliefs that hold you back.

## Key ideas

**Decide what you want.** Set inspiring goals in four areas: career or business, personal growth, adventure and contribution. For each, ask "What would I go for if I knew I could not fail?" Write until you feel excited. People aren't lazy. Their goals just don't inspire them.

**Find your limiting beliefs.** Most limiting beliefs are generalisations from one painful moment. One bad project becomes "I'm no good at big projects." Look for beliefs that start "I'm too...", "I'll never..." or "I always...".

**Question them.** Ask where each belief came from. Is that person an expert? Is the belief always true? Find counter-examples. Once you doubt a belief, it loses its grip.

**Replace them.** Write a new belief that pushes you forward and collect evidence that supports it.

**Change your state.** How you move, breathe and speak changes how you feel. Stand tall and move with energy and your mood follows.

## Use it with clients

- When an owner sets goals that feel flat, ask the "could not fail" question. Big, inspiring goals create energy.
- Listen for "we've tried that before" or "our industry doesn't work like that". Question the source and look for counter-examples together.

## Try it yourself

Write three limiting beliefs about your business. For each, list one counter-example and a new belief to replace it.

*By Viktor Frankl*

**The big idea:** Viktor Frankl survived four Nazi concentration camps by finding meaning in each moment. Life is a search for meaning, and meaning gives you the strength to endure almost anything.

## Key ideas

Frankl found three "wells of meaning" you can draw on when times are hard.

**1. A life task.** Frankl kept himself alive by vowing to rewrite the manuscript the guards took from him. People who knew a task was waiting for them were most likely to survive. When you have work only you can do, life feels meaningful.

**2. Love.** For Frankl, love means seeing the potential in others and helping them reach it. Mentoring, parenting and leading all count.

**3. Courage in suffering.** When you can't change a situation, you can still choose your attitude to it. "Everything can be taken from a man but one thing: the last of the human freedoms, to choose one's attitude."

**Between stimulus and response there is a space.** In that space lies your freedom to choose how you respond.

## Use it with clients

- Owners who feel flat after years of running the business often need a fresh "life task". Ask what they want the business to make possible for them, their team and their family.
- Help owners see that developing their people is a source of meaning, not just a management chore.
- In hard periods, focus the owner on the response they can choose.

## Try it yourself

Write one sentence on the work only you can do. Then name one person whose potential you could help grow this month.

*By Maxwell Maltz*

**The big idea:** Your self-image sets the limits of what you achieve. Upgrade how you see yourself, and your mind starts working towards your goals on autopilot.

## Key ideas

**You have a built-in goal-seeking system.** Like a self-driving car, your subconscious works out the route once you set the destination. Charles Darwin got stuck on his book, stopped forcing it, and the answer came to him days later.

**Self-image is the hidden blueprint.** Everyone carries a picture of "the sort of person I am", built from past wins, failures and what others said. If you see yourself as a failure type, you'll find a way to fail even with good intentions.

**Worry jams the system.** When you overthink and second-guess, you grab the steering wheel from your own autopilot. Trust it more.

**Upgrade your self-image with imagination.** Spend a few minutes each day relaxing and picturing yourself acting as the person who has already reached your goal. Your nervous system can't fully tell a vivid image from a real experience, so practice in your head builds real confidence.

## Use it with clients

- Owners often see themselves as "the technician" or "the person who does the sales". Help them picture themselves as the owner of a business that runs without them.
- Before a big pitch or tough conversation, ask the owner to rehearse it in their head, seeing it go well.

## Try it yourself

Spend five minutes each morning this week picturing yourself as the advisor you want to be in a year. See how you work, talk and decide.

*By Seth Godin*

**The big idea:** Every worthwhile project starts fun, then hits a long slog called the Dip. The people who push through the Dip become the best in their world. The secret is knowing when to push and when to quit.

## Key ideas

**Being number one pays.** The top choice gets far more reward than the tenth. People short on time go straight to the best.

**"Best in the world" means best in their world.** Your world can be small. A niche, a region or a type of client. The more specialised you are, the easier it is to be the best.

**The Dip is where most people quit.** Early progress is quick and exciting. Then results slow down and the work gets hard. Quitting here wastes everything you've put in.

**Decide in advance when you'll quit.** Quitting in a panic is costly. Before you start, set your limits: how much time and money you'll invest and what result would tell you to stop.

**Quit the dead ends.** Some paths never get better. Quit those fast so you have the energy to push through the Dips that matter.

## Use it with clients

- Owners often spread themselves across too many services or markets. Help them pick where they can be the best and drop the rest.
- When a new system or campaign stalls after a few weeks, check whether it's a Dip or a dead end before the owner gives up.

## Try it yourself

List your current projects. Mark each one "Dip" (worth pushing through) or "dead end" (quit now). Quit one dead end this week.

*By Angela Duckworth*

**The big idea:** Grit is passion and perseverance for long-term goals. It predicts success better than talent does. Gritty people stay fixed on the big goal but flexible on how they get there.

## Key ideas

**Firm on the goal, flexible on the steps.** Gritty people don't stubbornly chase every item on a to-do list. They know the "why" behind what they do and change tactics when something isn't working.

**Deliberate practice.** Grit means more than long hours. Gritty people fill their hours with focused practice on their weak spots and seek feedback all the time.

**Grit grows.** You can build grit four ways. Develop real interest in your work. Practise every day. Connect your work to a purpose beyond yourself. Hold on to hope that effort pays off.

## Use it with clients

- When an owner's big goal feels far away, connect daily work back to the "why". Purpose fuels perseverance.
- Help owners stay flexible. If one lead source isn't working, change the tactic, not the goal.
- Teams copy the owner. A gritty owner who keeps going after setbacks builds a gritty team.

Write your top-level goal for the next three years. Underneath, list the daily practice that will get you there and do it every day this week.

*By David Goggins*

**The big idea:** David Goggins finished Navy SEAL hell week on broken legs and set a world record with 4,030 pull-ups. His principles show how much more you can give than you think.

## Key ideas

**Callous your mind.** Pull-ups gave Goggins thick calluses that blunted the pain. Your mind works the same way. Do something uncomfortable every day, even something small. Each time, you build a tougher mind.

**The 40% rule.** When you feel you've hit your limit, you've usually given about 40% of what you've got. Your brain sets off the alarm early to protect you. Knowing that lets you push further.

**Use an accountability mirror.** Goggins stuck notes on his mirror with honest goals and truths about himself. Facing the truth every day stops you hiding from it.

**Fill your cookie jar.** Keep a list of hard things you've already overcome. When you want to quit, reach into the jar and remind yourself what you're capable of.

## Use it with clients

- When an owner says "We can't do any more", ask whether they've really hit the limit or just the first wall.
- Help owners build their own cookie jar: past crises they've survived and wins they've earned. It's powerful in a tough quarter.
- Encourage honest scorecards. A weekly "accountability mirror" for key numbers stops problems hiding.

## Try it yourself

Write your own cookie jar: five hard things you've overcome. Read it the next time you want to give up.

*By Nassim Taleb*

**The big idea:** Resilient things survive shocks and stay the same. Antifragile things get stronger from shocks. You can build a life and a business that gain from chaos.

## Key ideas

**Cap your downside.** Each morning, imagine losing your savings, your job or your business. Once you feel you could cope, the rest of the day is a bonus. Taleb did this as a trader, so bad news stopped knocking him over.

**Seek good stress.** The right amount of stress for the right time builds strength. A short workout after a long flight makes you feel better. Look for small, regular stresses that make you grow.

**Take lots of small bets.** Make many small moves with limited downside and big upside. Most will fail cheaply. A few will pay off well.

**Avoid big fragile bets.** Don't stake everything on one client, one product or one forecast. Things that can't survive one shock eventually break.

## Use it with clients

- Check how fragile the business is. Does one client make up 30% of revenue? Does one person hold all the knowledge? Spread the risk.
- Encourage owners to test new offers and channels cheaply instead of making one big expensive bet.
- Build cash reserves so a bad month becomes a chance to buy, hire or win share.

## Try it yourself

List the three biggest single points of failure in your business. Take one step this month to reduce one of them.

*By Mark Manson*

**The big idea:** Problems never go away, they just get better. Warren Buffett has money problems, just better ones. The good life comes from choosing good problems and caring about fewer, better things.

*By Dave Evans and Bill Burnett*

**The big idea:** Two Stanford designers apply design thinking to careers. You don't know what you want until you try it, so sketch several possible lives and test them before you commit.

## Key ideas

**Sketch three lives.** Everyone has several good lives in them. Write three five-year plans:

2. **Your plan B**, if your current path disappeared.

**Test before you commit.** The biggest risk is assuming you'll enjoy the day-to-day of a new life. Test that cheaply first.

**Have prototype conversations.** Find people already living the life you're considering. Ask for 15 minutes to hear their story, the good and the bad.

**Try prototype experiences.** Shadow someone for a day, take a short course or run a small side project. Small experiments beat big leaps.

**You can't connect the dots looking forward.** Build your path by trying things and seeing what fits.

## Use it with clients

- Owners planning their next chapter, such as stepping back, selling or launching something new, can sketch three versions and test them before committing.
- Before a big change like opening a new service line, encourage prototype conversations with people already doing it.

## Try it yourself

*By Vishen Lakhiani*

**The big idea:** Have big goals, but don't tie your happiness to them. Be happy now, and you'll chase big goals with less fear.

## Key ideas

**Question the rules you were handed.** Much of what we believe about work, success and life comes from culture, not truth. Lakhiani calls these "brules", short for "bullshit rules". Ask of each rule: is it true, and does it help me?

**Bend reality.** Hold a big vision for the future and be happy in the present at the same time. Happiness now gives you the energy and calm to chase the vision.

**Ask the three most important questions.** What experiences do I want? How do I want to grow? How do I want to contribute?

## Use it with clients

- Many owners say "I'll be happy when we hit £5M." Help them find satisfaction in the work now, so the goal pulls instead of pressures.
- Challenge industry "brules" like "clients won't pay more" or "we can't grow without me". Test whether they're true.

*By Gabriele Oettingen*

**The big idea:** Dreaming about success on its own makes you less likely to get it. Your brain feels like you've already arrived, so you lose energy. Pair the dream with the obstacle and you get a powerful motivation tool called WOOP.

## Key ideas

**Positive fantasies drain energy.** Students who pictured getting good grades scored lower. Graduates who pictured great jobs got fewer offers. The fantasy relaxes the body and kills the drive to act.

**Dreams still help you choose.** Imagining several futures quickly shows you which one is worth fighting for. Keep dreaming, then add a reality check.

**Use WOOP:**
- **Wish:** What do I want?
- **Outcome:** What's the best result if I get it? Picture it.
- **Obstacle:** What inside me will get in the way? Picture that too.
- **Plan:** "If [obstacle], then I will [action]."

**If-then plans work.** Deciding in advance what you'll do when the obstacle shows up makes you far more likely to follow through.

## Use it with clients

- Use WOOP in planning sessions. Once an owner names a goal, ask "What in you is most likely to get in the way?"
- Turn the answers into if-then plans: "If I start checking email at 8am, then I'll close it and make two sales calls first."

## Try it yourself

Run WOOP on one goal for this month. Write your if-then plan on a sticky note where you'll see it every day.

*By Scott Adams*

**The big idea:** Dilbert creator Scott Adams says you raise your odds of success by getting good, not amazing, at skills that work well together. And by using systems instead of goals.

## Key ideas

**Systems beat goals.** A goal is a target you hit once. A system is something you do every day that raises your odds. "Lose 10kg" is a goal. "Eat well every day" is a system. Goal-chasers feel like failures until they arrive. System-followers win every day.

**Stack your skills.** Being in the top 25% at two or three skills that combine well is rarer and more valuable than being the best at one. Adams was a decent artist with a good sense of humour and business experience. Together they made Dilbert.

**Learn clear writing.** Cut unneeded words. Use "actor, action, object": "The boy hit the ball", not "The ball was hit by the boy."

**Learn conversation.** Smile, use open body language and ask questions to find common interests. Simple, and enough to put you in the top 10% of conversationalists.

**Manage your energy.** Do your hardest work when your energy is highest. Sleep, food and exercise drive it.

## Use it with clients

- Owners often set big annual goals and then drift. Help them turn each goal into a weekly system they can tick off.
- Point out their skill stack. What rare mix of skills makes their business different? Build the marketing around it.

## Try it yourself

Take your biggest goal and write the daily or weekly system that would make it likely. Start the system tomorrow.

*Talk by Tony Robbins, TED*

**The big idea:** Tony Robbins explores the invisible forces that drive what people do. Decisions shape destiny, and emotion and human needs shape decisions.

## Key ideas

**Resourcefulness beats resources.** People say they failed because they lacked money, time or contacts. Robbins argues the real issue is resourcefulness: the emotion and drive to find a way. Creativity, determination and passion make up for missing resources.

**Decisions shape destiny.** Three decisions drive your life every moment: what you focus on, what it means to you and what you'll do about it.

**Six human needs drive behaviour.**
1. **Certainty:** feeling safe and in control.
2. **Variety:** change and challenge.
3. **Significance:** feeling important.
4. **Connection and love.**
5. **Growth.**
6. **Contribution:** giving beyond yourself.

Everyone values all six, but in a different order. That order explains why people do what they do.

**Growth and contribution bring fulfilment.** The first four needs can be met in healthy or unhealthy ways. Lasting fulfilment comes from the last two.

## Use it with clients

- When an owner resists a change, ask which need it threatens. Delegating can threaten certainty or significance.
- Use the six needs to understand staff behaviour. A team member who craves significance needs recognition. One who craves certainty needs clear processes.

## Try it yourself

Rank the six needs in the order that drives you most. Ask whether your business is meeting your top two in a healthy way.
