*By Daniel Kahneman*

**The big idea:** Two systems drive your thinking. System 1 is fast, automatic and emotional. System 2 is slow, careful and logical. System 1 runs more of your life than you think, and it makes predictable mistakes.

## Key ideas

**System 1 is the secret author.** It creates quick impressions and feelings, which System 2 often accepts without checking. That's fine for everyday choices, but costly for big ones like hiring, partnerships or investments.

**Familiarity feels like truth.** Hear something often enough and it starts to feel true. Repetition isn't evidence.

**Anchors pull you.** The first number you hear shapes your estimate, even if it's random. The first price in a negotiation anchors the whole deal.

**What you see is all there is.** System 1 builds a confident story from whatever information is in front of it and ignores what's missing.

**Losses hurt more than gains.** Losing £100 feels about twice as bad as winning £100 feels good. This makes us too cautious and keeps us in bad situations to avoid "losing".

**Slow down for big decisions.** Ask: what am I missing? What would change my mind? What does the data say?

## Use it with clients

- Owners often make big decisions on gut feel: a hire who "felt right" or a supplier they've always used. Help them slow down and check with System 2.
- Use anchoring in pricing: show the premium option first.
- Spot loss aversion when an owner won't drop an unprofitable client or product.

## Try it yourself

Before your next big decision, write down three pieces of information you don't have. Get at least one before you decide.

*By Chip Heath and Dan Heath*

**The big idea:** Our gut is full of questionable advice. Four steps, called WRAP, help you make better decisions at work and in life.

## Key ideas

**Three traps.** We rarely consider more than two options. We get blinded by short-term emotion. And we're overconfident: doctors who felt "completely certain" were wrong 40% of the time.

**W - Widen your options.** Avoid "whether or not" decisions. In one study, 69% of big organisational decisions had only one option, and those failed more often. Ask "What else could we do?" Imagine your current options vanished.

**R - Reality-test your assumptions.** Look for evidence that you're wrong. Ask people who disagree. Run a small test before committing.

**A - Attain distance.** Short-term emotion clouds judgement. Ask "How will I feel about this in 10 minutes, 10 months and 10 years?" Or "What would I tell my best friend to do?"

**P - Prepare to be wrong.** Plan for the worst and the best. Set a tripwire: a point at which you'll review the decision.

## Use it with clients

- When an owner says "Should we do X or not?", ask for three more options before deciding.
- Before a big hire or investment, suggest a small test first: a trial project, a pilot or a short contract.
- Use 10/10/10 for emotional decisions like letting someone go.

## Try it yourself

Take one decision you're facing. Run it through WRAP and write down at least three options before choosing.

*By Annie Duke*

**The big idea:** Good decision-makers know when to decide fast and when to slow down. Most decisions don't deserve the time we give them.

## Key ideas

**Decide fast if a decision passes any of these tests:**

**The happiness test.** Will this decision affect your happiness a week from now? If not, decide quickly. Most choices about food, clothes and small purchases fall here.

**The only-option test.** Once you have two or more options that meet your standards, just pick one. Agonising over 90% versus 91% wastes time.

**The freeroll test.** If the downside is tiny and reversible, go fast. You can always change course.

**Slow down when the stakes are high and the choice is hard to undo.** Then gather information, consider other views and think about the range of outcomes.

**Judge decisions, not just outcomes.** A good decision can lead to a bad result through bad luck. Don't learn the wrong lesson from one outcome.

**Get outside views.** Ask others for their opinion before sharing yours, so you don't sway them.

## Use it with clients

- Owners often get stuck on small decisions and rush big ones. Use the three tests to sort which is which.
- When asking the team for input, get their views before the owner shares theirs.

## Try it yourself

Look at the decisions on your plate. Use the three tests to make every small one today, and book time for the one big one.

*By Annie Duke*

**The big idea:** Two things shape how life turns out: the quality of your decisions and luck. Professional poker player Annie Duke shows how to tell them apart and decide better.

## Key ideas

**Treat every decision as a bet.** Imagine someone says "Wanna bet?" on your belief. You'd pause and check what you might be missing. Thinking in bets makes you see a range of possible futures, not one.

**Don't judge by results alone.** Poker players call it "resulting": judging a decision only by how it turned out. A good decision can lose. A bad one can win. Look at the reasoning, not just the outcome.

**Say how sure you are.** Instead of "This will work," say "I'm 70% sure this will work." Putting a number on it makes you more honest and open to new information.

**Build a truth-seeking group.** Find people who'll challenge your thinking and reward accuracy over agreement.

**Time travel.** Ask how your future self would feel about this decision. Picture regret before you act.

## Use it with clients

- After a lost deal or a failed hire, help owners separate luck from decision quality before drawing lessons.
- Ask owners to put a percentage on their confidence in forecasts and plans. It sparks better conversations.

## Try it yourself

For your next three decisions, write down how confident you are as a percentage. Check back in a month to see how well calibrated you were.

*By Shane Parrish*

**The big idea:** In life and business, the person with the fewest blind spots wins. Mental models are thinking tools that reveal blind spots and spark better solutions.

## Key ideas

**Inversion.** Solve the opposite problem. Instead of "How do I win more clients?", ask "How would I lose every client?" Then avoid those things.

**First principles.** Break a problem down to its basic truths and rebuild from there, instead of copying what everyone else does.

**Second-order thinking.** Ask "And then what?" Every decision has knock-on effects. A price cut wins sales now but may attract the wrong clients and squeeze margins later.

**The map is not the territory.** Reports, plans and numbers are maps. They simplify reality. Check them against what's actually happening.

**Circle of competence.** Know what you know well and where your knowledge ends. Get help outside your circle.

**Probabilistic thinking.** Think in odds, not certainties.

## Use it with clients

- Use inversion in planning sessions: "What would guarantee this year goes badly?" The answers become the risks to manage.
- Ask "And then what?" before an owner makes a big change, like cutting prices or adding a new service.

## Try it yourself

Take a goal for this quarter and invert it. List five ways to guarantee failure, then make sure you're not doing any of them.

*By Adam Grant*

**The big idea:** The ability to rethink and unlearn matters as much as the ability to think. People who change their minds after careful thought rise faster.

## Key ideas

**Rethinkers win.** Top forecasters change their minds twice as often as others. Students who revise exam answers score higher. The best-rated presidents revised more of their own policies.

**The overconfidence cycle.** Form an opinion, look for support, feel validated, then defend it proudly. Each loop makes you more certain and less willing to learn.

**Three traps:**
- **Preaching:** defending your beliefs as sacred.
- **Prosecuting:** attacking others' views to win.
- **Politicking:** chasing approval.

**Think like a scientist.** Treat your views as hypotheses to test. Look for evidence that you're wrong.

**Ask more, tell less.** To change someone's mind, ask questions that help them rethink instead of lecturing them.

## Use it with clients

- Owners often hold strong beliefs about their market, team or pricing. Help them treat those beliefs as hypotheses and test them.
- When an owner wants to change a team member's view, coach them to ask questions instead of lecturing.

## Try it yourself

Write down one strong opinion you hold about your business. List what evidence would change your mind, then go and look for it.

*By Ray Dalio*

**The big idea:** Ray Dalio built the world's largest hedge fund on one formula: Pain + Reflection = Progress. Turn every mistake into a principle so you handle the next one better.

## Key ideas

**Pain is a signal.** When something hurts, it shows a gap in your knowledge. Instead of running from it, reflect on it and learn.

**Write principles.** After each mistake or hard decision, write a short rule for handling similar situations. Over time you build a playbook.

**Be radically open-minded.** Accept that you have blind spots. Seek out smart people who disagree with you and ask why.

**Practise radical transparency.** Share honest feedback openly. Dalio's firm records meetings and encourages people to challenge anyone, including him.

**Use a five-step process:**
1. Set clear goals.
2. Spot problems that block them.
3. Find the root causes.
4. Design a plan to fix them.
5. Do it.

**Weigh views by believability.** Give more weight to people who have a track record in the area.

## Use it with clients

- After a painful event, like losing a client or a bad hire, help the owner write a principle so it doesn't happen again.
- Encourage owners to build a culture where staff can challenge ideas, including the owner's.
- Use the five steps as a simple problem-solving agenda for sessions.

## Try it yourself

Think of a recent mistake. Reflect on what caused it and write one principle you'll follow next time.

*By Brian Christian and Tom Griffiths*

**The big idea:** Computer science has tested answers to everyday problems. Use them to make better calls on hiring, scheduling and when to explore versus stick.

## Key ideas

**The 37% rule (when to stop looking).** When choosing from a series of options, like candidates or properties, look at the first 37% without choosing. Then pick the next one better than all of those. It gives you the best odds of finding a great option without endless searching.

**Explore versus exploit.** Early on, try new things (explore). As time runs out, stick with what you know works (exploit). A new hire should explore more early on. A business near a deadline should exploit.

**Sorting and scheduling.** To finish the most tasks, do the shortest first. To keep the most people happy, do the most important first. Pick a rule that fits your goal.

**Overfitting.** Too much detail can make plans worse. Simple rules often beat complex models when the future is uncertain.

**Caching.** Keep what you use most within easy reach, and let go of what you rarely use.

## Use it with clients

- Use the 37% rule in hiring: interview a set of candidates to set the bar, then hire the next one who beats it.
- When an owner can't decide whether to keep testing new marketing or double down, use explore versus exploit based on their timeline.

## Try it yourself

For your next search, whether candidates, suppliers or tools, decide in advance how many you'll review before choosing.

*By Edward Burger and Michael Starbird*

**The big idea:** Extraordinary people are ordinary people who think differently. Five simple thinking habits, named after the elements, help anyone solve hard problems.

## Key ideas

**Earth: understand deeply.** Go back to the basics. Break a problem into its root causes. Procrastination might really be distraction, low motivation and overwhelm.

**Fire: fail to succeed.** Make deliberate mistakes and learn from them. A quick, rough attempt shows you what to fix.

**Air: ask questions.** Ask perspective-changing questions like "What's the real question here?" or "What if I tried the opposite?"

**Water: see the flow of ideas.** Every idea builds on earlier ones. Ask "Where did this come from and where could it go next?"

**Quintessence: keep changing.** Make these habits part of who you are. The real goal is to keep growing.

## Use it with clients

- When an owner is stuck, use Earth: break the problem into root causes before jumping to solutions.
- Encourage owners to make a quick, rough first version of a new process (Fire) instead of waiting for the perfect one.

## Try it yourself

Pick a problem you're stuck on. Break it into three root causes, then try a quick, rough fix for one of them.

*By Charles Duhigg*

**The big idea:** Productivity comes from how you think, not just how hard you work. Motivation, focus and better decisions all come from making the right choices.

## Key ideas

**Motivation comes from control and meaning.** Choose where to work, how long to focus and what to do while working. Then link the task to how it helps others, builds your skill or leads to something you'll be proud of.

**Predict your day.** Before the day starts, picture how it will unfold. Your brain loves checking its predictions, so it pays more attention to what it didn't expect.

**Think in probabilities.** Picture several possible futures and how likely each is. It leads to better decisions.

**Psychological safety builds great teams.** Google found its best teams weren't the smartest. They were the ones where everyone spoke up roughly equally and felt safe to take risks.

**Use stretch goals and SMART goals together.** A big stretch goal sets direction. SMART goals break it into concrete steps.

## Use it with clients

- When an owner's team lacks drive, look at control and meaning. Do people have choices in how they work? Do they know why it matters?
- Help owners make team meetings safe to speak up in. Ask quiet members first.
- Pair a big stretch goal with SMART steps in quarterly planning.

## Try it yourself

Tomorrow morning, spend five minutes picturing how the day will go. Note what surprised you by the evening.

*By Eric Barker*

**The big idea:** Much of what we believe about success is wrong or incomplete. Measuring success by money alone is a mistake. Success has four parts.

## Key ideas

**Measure four things:**
- **Happiness:** do you enjoy your life?
- **Achievement:** do you feel you're winning? Set and hit small, meaningful goals each day.
- **Significance:** do the people you care about value you?
- **Legacy:** are you helping others succeed?

**Balance all four.** People who chase one, usually achievement, often feel empty. Check each one regularly.

**Know your strengths.** Success comes from finding the right "pond", a role and environment that suit who you are. Even odd traits can become strengths in the right setting.

**Givers can win, with boundaries.** Generous people often rise to the top, as long as they protect their time.

**Persistence needs a story.** People keep going when they see their struggle as part of a meaningful story. Frame setbacks as part of the journey.

## Use it with clients

- Owners often measure success only by revenue. Ask them to score happiness, achievement, significance and legacy. The gaps often explain why they feel flat.
- Help owners put team members in the right pond, matching roles to strengths.

## Try it yourself

Score yourself out of 10 on happiness, achievement, significance and legacy. Pick one small action for your lowest score.

*By Tim Ferriss*

**The big idea:** Tim Ferriss asked more than 100 high achievers the same questions. One answer stood out: when overwhelmed and unfocused, the best people use a few simple daily practices to reset.

## Key ideas

**Ask clear questions.** "Life punishes the vague wish and rewards the specific ask." Better questions lead to better answers.

**Move.** Many mentors walk when they feel overwhelmed. A 15 to 30-minute walk without devices clears the head.

**Meditate.** A few minutes of stillness calms the mind and restores focus.

**Write it down.** Getting thoughts on paper shrinks them. Many mentors journal or make lists to get clarity.

**Ask "What would this look like if it were easy?"** Simple questions cut through complexity.

**Say no more often.** Many mentors said learning to say no was one of their best investments.

## Use it with clients

- Give owners a simple reset routine for overwhelming days: walk, breathe, write.
- Use Ferriss's questions in sessions. "What would this look like if it were easy?" often unlocks stuck problems.

## Try it yourself

Next time you feel overwhelmed, take a 20-minute walk without your phone. Then write down the one thing that matters most.

*Talk by Antonio Rangel, TEDxCaltech*

**The big idea:** Caltech neuroscientist Antonio Rangel shows how the brain makes everyday choices. It puts a value on each option and compares them, and what you pay attention to shapes the result.

## Key ideas

**The brain runs a value calculation.** Every simple choice, like what to eat, involves your brain weighing up each option's value and picking the higher one.

**Attention tips the scales.** The option you look at longer tends to win. Where your attention goes shapes what you value in the moment.

**Self-control means weighing the long term.** People with good self-control bring long-term factors, like health, into the calculation. People who struggle weigh mostly short-term factors, like taste.

**You can steer it.** Directing attention to long-term benefits changes the value your brain puts on each option, and so the choice you make.

## Use it with clients

- Owners face constant short-term pulls: firefighting, easy tasks, quick wins. Help them put long-term goals in front of them, like a visible 90-day plan, so those goals carry more weight in daily choices.
- In sales, what a prospect focuses on shapes their decision. Keep attention on the value of the outcome, not just the price.

## Try it yourself

Put your top long-term goal somewhere you'll see it before making daily decisions, like your desktop or desk. Notice whether your choices shift.
