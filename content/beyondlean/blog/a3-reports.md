## Why reports matter

A report is one of the most important ways we communicate. Information has to be passed on accurately (quality), cost effectively (cost), promptly (timing) and in the right amount (quantity). A report, good or bad, also shows the level of the person who wrote it in their organisation.

Reports have two main purposes. The first is to keep the organisation working correctly and moving towards its objectives. The second is to help communication between managers and workers.

When a manager receives a report, they can confirm that their message has reached the team properly, and they can see the team's opinions and how far the work has progressed. If they see a problem, they can deal with it straight away. To make this happen, a report is essential.

With day-to-day pressures it may seem too much trouble to write a good report. But getting used to writing good, proper reports improves each person's ability and builds good communication across the organisation.

Throughout this guide, "5W and 1H" means who, what, when, where, why and how.

## Types of report and what each one needs

### Schedules

For example: an annual plan, a kaizen plan, a new activity or an equipment installation.

**Purpose:** to control the progress of a particular event, showing the activities still to be carried out, using 5W and 1H.

**Requirements:**

1. A clear target and policy.
2. Improvements on past experience.
3. Clear timing for progress status reports.

### Progress status reports

**Purpose:**

1. To reflect on actions taken so far, understand the current status and prepare for the future.
2. To report the current status to the manager or the people concerned, so they understand it better and can offer suggestions, help and instruction.

**Requirements:**

1. Reflect actions taken so far against the plan, to understand the current status for future benefit.
2. Show the items already done, and make clear the items still to be done, using 5W and 1H.
3. Show how future progress is expected to compare with the target.

### Activity review reports

**Purpose:** to review whether the plan was followed or the targets achieved, for future benefit. In other words, to keep good activities as a standard system, and to identify and improve the activities that went wrong.

**Requirements:**

1. Compare results with the policy and target, using clear evaluation results.
2. Evaluate each item to show how much it contributed towards the target. The next plan should include sound improvement (kaizen) suggestions, using 5W and 1H.

### Technical reports

For example: test results or investigation results.

**Purpose:** to tell the relevant departments the technical facts found through investigation, evaluation, measurement or testing, where the causes and the size of a problem are unclear and need technical handling. This includes interim reports.

**Requirement:** the results must be clear, so the relevant departments can decide what to do next.

### Countermeasure or kaizen requests

**Purpose:** to promote countermeasures and follow up on progress.

**Requirement:** make clear the current status, concerns, the countermeasures requested (suggestions), the expected effect and the expected timing.

### Recurrence prevention reports

**Purpose:**

1. To stop the same or similar problems from happening again.
2. To improve weak areas of control and strengthen the organisation.

**Requirements:**

1. Identify the causes under the different categories of the 5Ms: man, material, machine, method and environment.
2. Take suitable countermeasures and preventive actions in terms of quality, quantity, cost, timing and safety.

### Meeting minutes and results

**Purpose:**

1. To make clear the items decided in the meeting, so the people who attended understand them the same way.
2. To tell the people who did not attend, and other relevant people, about the decisions made.

**Requirement:** describe the decisions clearly using 5W and 1H.

### Bulletins, and daily, weekly and monthly reports

**Purpose:** to report conditions up to a cut-off time, without delay, to the right people, so they take the right action.

**Requirements:**

1. Reports should be on time.
2. Include past performance (the trend) and the target.
3. Include the current status, temporary countermeasures, requests, permanent countermeasures and team members' concerns.

## What makes a good A3 report?

If an A3 report covers its purpose and requirements (why, who, what, when, where and how) in a way that is simple, clear and easy to look at, and it is cost effective and timely, it can be called a good report. In other words, 5W and 1H, and a good sense of quality, quantity, cost and timing, are very important.

### Why?

Reports that don't make clear why they were written, or what they are trying to say, are rarely taken notice of. They are far from a good report. Every effort must be made to make sure the report fulfils the purpose it was written for, and that purpose should be decided before you start writing.

Examples of purposes:

1. To help understanding
2. To share ideas
3. To request countermeasures
4. To get guidance, help or suggestions
5. To get instructions or approval
6. To review and prepare for the future
7. To take the next step forward or make a decision
8. To communicate
9. To persuade the relevant people to act

### Who?

Change your writing style to suit the reader, without changing the basic idea. The style hardly matters to the writer, who already understands the subject fully, but it makes a considerable difference to the reader. Amazingly, this simple logic is uncommon.

A report to a managing director is different in style from a report to other levels. In senior management meetings especially, where company progress is updated, summarise the important points logically and give them as much information as they need to decide what action to take.

### What?

See the types of report above.

### How long?

Aim to give readers the information they need in a short time, measured in minutes or even seconds. It is human nature to report as much as possible, or to believe that more information gives a better understanding. It doesn't. Unnecessary or too much detail gets in the way of understanding.

### Where and how?

How and where you report affects how well people understand it.

![Understanding rises from issuing a report, to explaining it at a table, to reporting at the actual workplace](/beyondlean/blog/a3-reports/where-and-how.jpg)

Understanding is lowest when you only issue the report. It is higher when you sit down and explain it, and highest when you report at the actual workplace: "go and see".

## How to write it

### Paper size and number of pages

A3 and A4 sizes are recommended. The more pages you have, the harder the report is to understand, and that leads to waste.

### Title

The title should give a clear and precise idea of the contents, because:

1. the title helps people understand the contents
2. each reader can judge from the title whether the contents need close examination
3. the title helps decide the priority.

### The 20:80 rule: conclusion first

On a one-sheet report, use the first 20% of the space for 80% of the content, and use the rest of the space for the details. This makes the report fast and accurate to understand. The reader gets the picture from the title, then reads the conclusion, and you have grabbed their attention.

For reports of more than two pages, use the first page for 80% of the content and the remaining pages for the rest. Try not to put the conclusion or your requests on the second or later pages. However, the conclusion doesn't have to come first if the following items are described concisely, with clear headings, on a one-sheet report:

1. Purpose, background and basic approach
2. Current status, facts and performance
3. Main concerns and countermeasures
4. Future direction, opinion and prediction

### Keep it simple

Avoid reports that are hard to understand because of complicated wording. A report is not a novel or a letter: the fewer descriptive words, the better. Don't use ten words when one will do. If you don't think about efficiency, your report won't be read.

A simple report has:

1. simple, clear and preferably short sentences
2. no unnecessary or unimportant content
3. charts, graphs and drawings to show the status of a project or problem, and examples if needed
4. as many drawings as possible, so readers don't misunderstand.

### Put the important part first

If you want to show three conclusions, show the most important one first, unless they need a special order, such as by time. Apply the same principle to problems and to schedules for future actions. The reason is efficiency. Just imagine if the most important conclusion came last.

### Make clear requests

One of the most important jobs of a report is to get people, or an organisation, to do something. Always communicate the exact request using 5W and 1H.

### Give your opinion

Unfortunately, some people turn up, show their reports, talk about themselves and go. What have we learned? What do we need to do? You always need a policy for reporting. Your report needs a logical flow, such as: "This is what I think. This is what I want to do. Please give me your opinion and cooperation."

### Make the status clear

This matters most for status reports, such as daily, weekly and monthly reports. It is hard to see the status at a glance in text. Use charts, graphs and symbols, like O and X, to compare results with the target, and your status can be understood in a second.

![Simple charts marked O or X show status against target at a glance](/beyondlean/blog/a3-reports/status-at-a-glance.jpg)

### Show why, and how good or bad

Show clearly why the status or result is OK, on plan or not, and how good or bad it is. This helps others understand your report and speeds up future actions.

### Make the countermeasure and progress clear

If the status or result is X, make the countermeasure clear using 5W and 1H. It is your responsibility to turn that X into an O within a set time.

### Predict the future

It is very important in our jobs to improve future conditions. Analysing the past and present makes it easier to predict the future, and that tells us what to do. Report not only the current status but also past progress, so the report achieves its purpose. Reviewing the present makes it easy to explain what to do next.

### Make priorities clear

Making priorities clear helps readers understand each item and grasp the overall picture. Emphasise important points by underlining, **bold type** or colour.

### Use drawings and charts

Charts and drawings are a universal way of communicating. Even if you don't understand a language, you can tell what is happening from pictures.

Reports are often mostly text, with pictures as an extra. Aim the other way round: explain the content with pictures, and use text to support them (a single word is better), so readers understand quickly and correctly. Apply the same principle to your layout.

### Neat text and drawings

It is human nature to prefer looking at nice things, such as paintings, flowers or scenery, and to dislike looking at untidy, unsightly ones. Neat, tidy text and drawings are very important for a good report.

### Size of text

We rarely see a report with text that is too large. What we tend to see is text that is too small, because we try to cram as much as we can into as little space as possible. This needs correcting. Text that is too small doesn't stimulate the brain. It only strains the eyes.

### Distribution

The basic rule is to send reports to positions in the company, not to named people (except senior management). Send copies to the right positions, in the right number. For reports to senior management, adjust the report so it doesn't give too much detailed data, unless they ask for it.

### Be cost effective

Make every effort to let readers get the information they need from your report in the least time. Also try to reduce the number of copies, the paper size and so on.

### Be timely

Reports raised too late are worse than not reporting at all. Raise reports in good time, allowing for how long approvals and distribution take.

### Check it yourself

Some people send reports without fully checking that they do what they are meant to do. Always check your own report to make sure it is easy to understand. Remember that you probably know more about the subject than the reader does. Avoid acronyms and slang.

The main points to check:

1. There is a logical link between the purpose of the report and its conclusion.
2. Readers can follow the story from "purpose and reason" through to "conclusion and suggestions for the future".

## Why handwrite A3 reports?

When A3 reports are handwritten:

- they can't be emailed, so they force face-to-face communication
- you have to understand the detail before you write
- because they take more time, you only produce valuable reports
- you have to get a result to make the time worthwhile.

When reports are produced electronically:

- not enough time is spent understanding the subject
- we don't communicate effectively
- it is too easy to make and email a report
- a report can be used to dodge blame or ownership.

## Nemawashi: lining up your ducks

**Purpose:** to get agreement on the report and gain sign-off. Before you submit it, spend some time getting people to buy in to your objectives and to understand the content.

**Requirement:** take the time to walk the report through with each person who needs to sign it. Sit down and explain the content, the actions and any surprises. This speeds up sign-off and helps everyone feel at ease with the actions that come out of the report, which gives them ownership of those actions.

**Result:** a fully signed-off report, with a clear understanding and ownership of the actions.

## Checklist: what to include

Which items you include, and in what order, depends on the type of report you are writing. Use this list to help you raise a good report:

1. Summary
2. Conclusion
3. Background or motive
4. Preface
5. Purpose
6. Objective
7. Reason for reporting
8. Process
9. Special features
10. Current status
11. Policy
12. Past review
13. Interpretation
14. Schedule
15. Focus points of the activity
16. Important points
17. Process outline
18. Structure
19. Problem
20. Priority
21. Countermeasure responsibility
22. Target
23. Conditions
24. Plan
25. Cause and effect analysis
26. Future actions
27. Request
28. Timing
29. Evaluation and test results
30. Effectiveness of the countermeasure, or expected effectiveness
31. New problems
32. Actions and results
33. Cause investigation
34. Influence on others
35. Review
36. The writer's impression or opinion
37. Recurrence prevention
38. Future plan and future actions
39. Remaining problems and actions
