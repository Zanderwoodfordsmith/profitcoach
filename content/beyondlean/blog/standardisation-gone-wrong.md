We all know that standardisation is the foundation stone for any improvement activity. It's the building block and key enabler for so many of the other improvement tools we use, so how could it go wrong?

## A dozen doughnuts

I recently went into a well-known bakery chain to get some treats for a "Sprint" workshop I was running. The context isn't really important, but based on some of the questions I've been asked recently, I'll give it to you anyway.

The workshop was run to design some new wording for the automated phone menu, based on behavioural economics (Nudge Theory). The aim was to show customers that it would be much quicker and easier for them to use the newly improved self-serve options online, rather than call the contact centre. (Not relevant in any way, but someone would have asked!)

Anyway, back to the story. I finally got to the front of the queue in the bakery (rhymes with "dregs"), with my box of 12 iced ring doughnuts and 3 bags of oversized triple choc, white choc and milk choc cookies. I was greeted by a really efficient and pleasant server who asked, "Is there anything else I can get for you today?" I said there wasn't.

It was the next thing out of his mouth that really took me by surprise: "Will you be eating those in or taking them away?"

Seriously? Twelve doughnuts and twelve enormous cookies, and he really asked if I was about to sit down in the shop and eat until I was sick.

I walked back to the client's offices chuckling to myself, slowly realising that this was just an extreme example of something I've seen many times over the years: standardisation gone wrong.

I can understand that all the servers in these shops are trained to ask whether the food is to be eaten in or taken away, for VAT purposes. But surely common sense and a modicum of intelligence have to step in and override the standard when it is clearly inappropriate. Best case, the customer finds it a little amusing, as I did. Worst case, if I'd been conscious of my weight, that question could have really offended me.

## Intelligent standardisation

Taiichi Ohno is credited with the quote "Without standardisation, there can be no improvement", and I agree 100%.

However, over the last 25 years or so, I've come to realise that "intelligent standardisation" is the key to making this work. The level of standardisation you put in place depends very much on the type and complexity of the work. It also depends on the level of customer interaction (which brings variation from outside) and the skill level of the workers (including the four levels of competence).

Any process or activity can be standardised to a greater or lesser degree. Getting the level of standardisation right is the key to how successful you will be.

## When one size fits nobody

Here's an example. A few years ago, I was working with a client in a major UK bank, and the senior sponsor didn't quite "get" the importance of intelligent standardisation. He had seen, and been really impressed by, the visual management we had introduced, and he'd just been introduced to the idea of standardisation. As a result, he issued an edict across his division: all visual management must be standardised. Every board had to be exactly the same, including the graphs, the metrics and the look and feel.

That may sound great, until you realise that this division held many different departments and teams. Many of them worked on completely different processes, with completely different customers. Needless to say, the "one size fits all" approach didn't fit. Because it wasn't applied intelligently, it almost caused some teams to implode.

## Standard work in a contact centre

Another example that springs to mind is a client who was very forthright in telling me that "everybody knows" standardisation can't be applied to call handling in a contact centre. She was eventually very happy to be proved wrong. We delivered a 30% reduction in cost to serve and a 20% increase in customer satisfaction and quality scores, and she was promoted as a result.

Keep the level of standardisation during any customer conversation at a "structure" or "framework" level. That gives the adviser enough licence to adapt to the variation every customer brings. Only take it to a more detailed level during the transactional parts of the process. Done this way, it can be highly successful. I have done it across many different types of contact centre, from sales to debt collection, to customer service and technical helplines, and many more.

## In summary

Any process can be standardised, but always apply a degree of intelligence and, more importantly, a large dose of common sense.
