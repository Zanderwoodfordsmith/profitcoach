Watch Paul's video: [The Standard Business Roadmap](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zvAVCHkLqpA)

There's no doubt that many companies have tried Lean or Six Sigma, or even both, and not had much success, if any. So from their point of view, the statement above is very true. It is their experience of these methods, and to that degree it is true for them.

So this begs the question: why do Lean and Six Sigma work for some companies and not for others?

The answer is closely linked to the age-old question, "How do we make the improvements stick?" If you're a senior manager asking that question, the answer is very simple: look in the mirror.

Anyway, back to the original question, and why some companies get it right and some just don't.

## The answer in one quote

The answer has been staring everyone in the face for years, but very few ever heard the message as it was delivered:

"Without standardisation, there can be no improvement." (Taiichi Ohno)

But how does this simple quote answer the question?

Think wider than process standardisation. Think about the basics of operations management. The standardisation Ohno spoke about was something the Japanese at Toyota took for granted: their culture, and the discipline of good basic management and sticking to processes (whether the manufacturing processes or the daily management routines). They already had a very solid foundation to build on before they started to look at the many "Lean" tools they later developed over half a century.

Simply put, lay the foundations, then begin to build the house.

## A ferret with a toolbox

It amazes me how many companies set off on their Lean Six Sigma journey with good intentions. Many send their people on courses, and they come back with their shiny new Lean Sigma toolbox. They set about putting in kanban systems for the stationery that no one uses, and value stream mapping a large, unwieldy process that no one cares about anyway. Then they wonder why it didn't work.

You can give a ferret a toolbox, but that doesn't make it a mechanic. A little knowledge is dangerous.

Get your foundations in place first. Get good management practices embedded. Put standard processes in place and make sure people stick to them. Manage your performance and get your quality nailed down. Hold daily production meetings in every team and tell your people exactly what is expected of them. (They will really appreciate it.)

In short, stabilise your operation. Get really, really good at the basics of operations management.

Then, and only then, start thinking about improvement, using whichever method best fits your business. The good news is that if you do the stabilisation phase well, you'll see around a 20% uplift in productivity and quality anyway, just from standardisation.

## Two gardeners

This story shows the importance of patience, and of building the right foundations to support the change.

Two gardeners each plant a seed in some fertile soil. After six months, each seed has sprouted just four inches above the ground.

One gardener is worried because the plant isn't growing fast enough, so he pulls up the sprout, root and all, and plants a new seed. The other gardener is more patient. He understands that the sprout is still building its foundations and has great potential, even though it hasn't grown much yet.

Another six months pass, and the patient gardener now has a plant two feet tall. It grew so quickly because it was nurtured early on and allowed to develop at its own pace.

The impatient gardener's new seed, on the other hand, has only grown four inches again.

Get the foundations in place first, and your Lean or Six Sigma efforts will soar. Who says they don't work?

Take what you find useful, and discard what you don't.
