Problem solving can take many forms. But if you try to solve your problem without any structure, you may end up with a bigger issue than you started with.

## What is a problem?

Something that needs a solution. It can be either positive or negative.

How often it happens:

- **Chronic:** ongoing and accepted problems.
- **Spasmodic:** a sudden change to the normal process.

## Problem levels

- **Level 0:** an abnormality that only affects the people directly involved in the process, and is contained.
- **Level 1:** an abnormality that affects the process's ability to achieve quality, cost and delivery.
- **Level 2:** an abnormality that affects the next process and may have an impact on the final customer.
- **Level 3:** an abnormality that has affected the final customer.

## Tools for analysing problems

**Qualitative tools** are used to gather and structure ideas during the cycle:

- flow charts
- 5 whys
- brainstorming
- cause and effect diagrams (Ishikawa or fishbone).

**Quantitative tools** are used to gather and analyse numerical data during the cycle:

- check sheets
- Pareto charts
- control charts
- histograms
- scatter diagrams.

## The five principles

1. Define the problem.
2. Identify the root cause.
3. Protect the customer and put countermeasures in place.
4. Confirm the countermeasure works.
5. Feed back and feed forward.

## The different types of problem solving

- **Preventive:** putting solutions in place before abnormalities happen. These are best identified at the design stage, before handover to manufacturing, using advanced quality tools.
- **Proactive:** looking at the current standards and, by analysing data with the 7 quality control tools, making kaizen improvements.
- **Reactive:** looking at abnormalities that have happened and, by gathering and analysing data with some of the 7 quality control tools, providing customer protection and a countermeasure.
