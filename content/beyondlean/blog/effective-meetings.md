Here is one of my personal pet hates: the meeting that adds absolutely no value to anyone involved.

A few weeks ago, one of my clients asked if we should set up a meeting. I asked what exactly the purpose of the meeting would be, so that I could decide whether it was worth my time, or anyone else's. She couldn't answer. Needless to say, I didn't attend, and nobody died.

Now here's the useful bit about meetings.

Meetings can be formal or informal, but they should only take place when the purpose can be achieved better by sharing in a room than by any other way of communicating. In other words, would the information be just as effective if it were sent as a memo, or even put up on a noticeboard?

Before any meeting takes place, we should ask ourselves:

**"Why am I having this meeting?"**

If you can't state the purpose of the meeting, perhaps there is no need for it to go ahead.

We should not fall into the trap of holding the weekly departmental meeting just because it has always been held. Many meetings waste a great deal of time, and therefore money, simply because they are held for historical reasons rather than practical ones.

## Setting objectives

Generally speaking, the objectives of a meeting fall into one of four categories:

- To give information
- To make decisions
- To investigate
- To plan

**To give information.** This type of meeting is only concerned with passing information on to the people attending, for example a new product launch, a start of shift meeting or a change to company procedures. Some questions may be asked and some information shared, but in most meetings of this kind, communication flows mostly one way.

**To make decisions.** This type of meeting is held when one person deciding alone might make a biased decision, or when the views of several people would lead to a better decision. A lot of discussion will probably be needed to reach agreement, but the final decision will be easier to accept if everyone has had their say.

**To investigate.** This type of meeting brings together people representing all sides of an issue, for example to investigate a quality concern. You may need people from a number of different areas to make sure every angle of the problem is looked at. It might also help to bring in outside expertise.

**To plan.** This type of meeting makes sure that representatives of everyone affected by a decision, or responsible for action, are involved in planning a project, for example a project planning team. If the plan is to go in smoothly, it is important that all sides agree.

## Types of meeting

Meetings can be grouped in a number of ways. The type of meeting depends on:

- **Frequency**, how often the meeting is held: start of shift, weekly, monthly, quarterly or annually
- **Composition**, who is attending: you and your supervisor, your peers, your customers, your suppliers or your workers
- **Motivation**, the reason behind the meeting: giving information, budget planning, wage negotiation or new product design
- **Decision making**, the scope for making decisions: a working party, an executive committee or a board of directors

Whatever type of meeting you are going to chair, you will have to do some planning and preparation.

## Preparation

A certain amount of preparation before the meeting is essential if the meeting you chair is to be effective. Preparation alone won't guarantee success, but without it the meeting will certainly be less effective.

Think of your preparation in terms of five Ws:

- **Why** should we hold the meeting? The objectives.
- **Who** should attend? The people.
- **What** is to be discussed? The agenda.
- **Where** should the meeting be held? The venue.
- **When** should the meeting be held? The timing.

This also gives you the order in which to prepare: the objective, the people, the agenda, the venue and the timing.

## The objective

We have already looked at the types of objective a meeting may have. We can't overstate how important it is to know **why** we are holding the meeting and **what** we want to achieve: the outcome.

## The people

The value and success of any meeting, except perhaps the information-giving kind, is seriously threatened if too many people are there. Decisions become hard to reach, or people may feel intimidated and hold back contributions that could be valuable. Between four and seven is the ideal number, and twelve is generally considered the outside limit. Any more than this, and the meeting becomes much less effective.

So it is important to put a lot of thought into who should attend. These guidelines may help:

- Invite only people with the authority to get the job done.
- Invite a representative from every group the decision will affect.
- Invite the people needed to reach agreement on the various issues.

If, after following these guidelines, too many people are still attending, there may be a case for splitting the meeting into smaller groups to deal with separate issues.

## The agenda

Of all the documents and papers needed for a meeting, by far the most important is the agenda. Used properly, it can speed up the meeting and make its objective clear. In most cases, agenda items are too brief and vague, so people can't prepare properly for the discussion. It is a good idea to send a circulation list with the agenda, so people can see who else is expected to attend.

The agenda gives discipline to the direction of the meeting, makes sure no important item is overlooked, and gives people time to prepare. For these reasons, the agenda should be written down rather than carried around in someone's head. Even for an emergency meeting called in response to a crisis, a scribbled agenda of the points to cover can give direction and stop the meeting being sidetracked onto other subjects.

These guidelines may help when you write an agenda:

- Show the time and place of the meeting.
- Gather items for discussion from the people attending.
- Convince yourself that an item is worth discussing before accepting it.
- If the person who submits an item can't attend the meeting, don't take the item unless it is absolutely necessary.
- Order items by how easily they can be settled, not by size or difficulty. Items the group can easily agree on bring it together early in the meeting, so deal with those first.
- Put items in a logical sequence.
- Set a time limit for each item. Even if you don't stick to it religiously, it will always be a guide.
- Be realistic about how many items you include.
- Write down the purpose of each item, for example for information, to discuss or to decide. This tells people in more detail what to prepare.
- Use simple language: "take a decision", not "conduct a review of its commercial viability".
- Never leave people guessing. Show exactly what is to be discussed and why.
- Say what preparation people should do, and any other papers they should bring.
- Think about whether to include "any other business". It can be seen as a chance to waste time or slip in a hidden agenda item, but it can also be useful for someone to raise an issue that came up after the agenda was set.

Where possible, set the agenda in advance and send it out, ideally 48 hours before the meeting. That is long enough for people to prepare, but not so far ahead that they forget the meeting.

## The venue

Arrange a suitable venue for the whole length of the meeting. If the meeting is in-house, location isn't an issue, but if some people are coming from other places, try to balance out the travelling times.

You may not have much choice over where you hold your meeting, but it should be somewhere convenient for everyone, free from interruptions and with enough seats for all.

## The timing

There is no hard and fast rule on how long a meeting should take. The points on the agenda will decide how long you need. However, for a meeting to be effective, it should certainly not take longer than two hours. If it would, perhaps some items should move to the next meeting. If that isn't possible, take a break during the meeting, and look at how often you hold these meetings in future. There may be a genuine case for more meetings of shorter length.

There is no "best" time for a meeting, other than when everyone is available. However, it is generally agreed that performance and alertness fall away quickly in the latter part of the day. If you want a meeting to finish promptly, it is always a good idea to hold it just before lunch. Again, think about people's travelling time and how they will arrive. If they come by train, allow them time to get from the station. Breakfast meetings are becoming more common and offer a chance to combine hospitality with information.

Find out when key information will be available if the meeting depends on it. If you need up-to-date financial figures and these are produced quarterly, try to hold your meeting just after each quarter.
