Five years ago, after we had finished a diagnostic, a client asked me when they could start putting a kanban system in place across their organisation. He had heard about kanban systems and thought one would solve all his operational issues.

I told the head of the organisation that they weren't ready for any Lean Six Sigma tools, let alone kanban systems across the board. His jaw almost hit the floor. I backed up my point by asking him some very basic questions:

- How many do you need to complete today?
- How many people will you need to complete today's work?
- How big is your performance spread?
- What does a good day look like?

There were a few more, all simple but essential questions that you would expect any good operations person to have a good handle on.

## Planting a flower in the desert

He couldn't answer a single one, and he quickly understood what I was getting at. We needed to get the foundations in place first, before we could begin a typical Lean Six Sigma project, let alone start putting random kanban systems in place. We needed to get his operation "in control", so that he could at least answer some of those questions.

The phase I was moving him into was the "Stability" phase, which would give us a stable platform to build from. Putting a kanban system in place while the operation was in that state would be like planting a flower in the desert. It would have looked nice for a very short while, but it would quickly die, because the environment couldn't sustain it.

## Steady the ship first

The main purpose of the Stability phase is to lay a solid foundation for the organisation. At the end of this phase you should be certain that the ship has been steadied, firefighting is a thing of the past and forward is the only way to go.

Depending on what the diagnostic uncovers, some actions may be needed immediately to prevent disaster. This is where the Stability phase and the Strategy phase often overlap.

There is no right or wrong order for the actions that follow. It depends entirely on what you uncover in the diagnostic.

For example, say a major cause of your company's problems is poor delivery, because you have no view of productivity on the shop floor, so you never know when your product is coming off the line. You put measures, or KPIs (key performance indicators), in place immediately to give you that view.

This can be done very quickly while other things are being worked on. Put your customer protection in place immediately. That buys you time to work on the long-term fix.

## Put standard operating procedures in place

"Without standardisation there can be no improvement" (Taiichi Ohno). Put standard work in place in the operation, and make sure people stick to it, relentlessly. Once it is in place and followed, if an error occurs there are only two possibilities: either the process wasn't followed, or the process is wrong. It really is that simple (leaving breakdowns and catastrophic failures aside).

Make daily communications, or operations meetings, part of the routine. They tell all your staff that what they do is important, and help them understand what is needed, by when and to what standard.

Getting these seemingly simple things in place, and rigidly sticking to them, will give you benefits beyond your imagination. It also starts to create the culture that paves the way for future changes and improvements to go in much more smoothly.

There is one caveat: involve the people every step of the way. If the people are involved and on board, everything else is easy.
