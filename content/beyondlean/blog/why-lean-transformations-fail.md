Here's the real truth behind failed Lean transformations.

A Lean transformation can let your operations drive the overall business strategy, by creating many real business benefits. Quality can rise through better process yields and lower scrap and rework costs. Costs improve through lower conversion costs, better cash flow and a higher return on assets. Delivery can improve through shorter production lead times and more flexible operations that respond quickly to changes in customer demand.

Despite their efforts, most businesses have not been able to capture or sustain the benefits of a Lean transformation. Lean is a fundamental change, and most businesses have gone about it in a tactical rather than a strategic way. Most failed Lean transformations come down to a lack of true senior management commitment and understanding of the change process. On top of that, many companies rely on a series of quick-hit approaches that deliver short-term benefits but don't last. Add the reluctance of many companies to look at the business as a whole, rather than just operations or manufacturing, and you have a recipe for failure.

## The change champion

The ultimate responsibility for the success of any change programme, whether it's Lean, Six Sigma or anything else, falls squarely on the shoulders of the most senior person on site. Not the change manager, the change team, the consultants or anyone else.

The senior manager is the "change champion", whether they accept the role or not. The speed of success of the change programme is directly proportional to how quickly they accept this responsibility and begin to walk the walk. The change champion must change their own behaviour in line with what they are asking the rest of the organisation to do. If they don't, it breeds mistrust, and it will inevitably cause the programme to falter and possibly fail.

A common reason this doesn't happen is that the change champion doesn't know it is their job. More commonly, they don't know how to do it and are afraid to admit it.

So how do they do it?

## One hour a day at the gemba

First, make sure the basics are in place, as set out in the "Stability" phase of the Standard Business Roadmap. A solid performance measurement system at shop floor level is one of the key building blocks of any change. Its measures must line up with the company's long-term strategy and site measures, and have clear targets for acceptable performance.

A good place to start is for the change champion to set aside a block of time every day (one hour may be enough) to go to the gemba, the shop floor, and carry out "go, look, see", or process confirmation, on these shop floor measures. Check that they are being updated regularly and hitting their targets, and that everyone affected by them understands them. Take the right action if the process for completing them isn't being followed.

This may sound extremely simple, but try it for yourself and watch the change in attitudes, and eventually the change in the whole culture. It is this cultural change that, when it comes, provides the critical mass for the whole change programme.

## An organisation is a shadow of its leader

It's up to you, as the change champion, to show the behaviours you want to see in your organisation. It is not enough to simply talk about them.

The first step is to change the way you behave. Lead by example, and show your people how you expect them to behave.

If, as the most senior person on site, you are not able or willing to change your behaviour to bring about the changes you need in others, and in the systems and processes they work with, then think about stepping aside and letting someone take the reins who will.

As the old saying goes, in any Lean transformation we have only two options: change the people, or change the people.
