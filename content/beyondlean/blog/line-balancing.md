Here is a simple definition of line balancing:

- Everyone is doing the same amount of work.
- They are doing that work to customer requirement.
- Variation is smoothed.
- No one is overburdened.
- No one is waiting.
- Everyone works together in a balanced way.

## A simple example

![Line balance example before: one operator overburdened while others wait](/beyondlean/blog/line-balancing/before.jpg)

Here we see operator number 1 overproducing, and so creating the other six wastes.

We simply rebalance the work content (redistribute some of the work), using what is often called a Yamazumi board.

![Line balance example after the work is redistributed](/beyondlean/blog/line-balancing/after.jpg)

The example above makes line balancing look incredibly simple. So why doesn't everyone do it?

There are two vital prerequisites that must be in place before a Yamazumi exercise can take place: **takt time** and **standard work**.

![The prerequisites to line balancing: calculate takt, and standardise](/beyondlean/blog/line-balancing/prerequisites.jpg)

## Takt time

Takt time is the production "drumbeat", based on customer demand. It matches the pace of each process to customer demand:

Takt = total time available / total customer demand

Only planned breaks are deducted, such as tea breaks, lunch breaks, team meetings and clean-down time. The drumbeat makes no allowance for machine inefficiency, such as breakdowns or changeovers.

## Standard work

Whether you use standard work combination tables, standard work instruction sheets or other standard work documents will depend on the type of work involved.

Standard work instruction sheets describe in detail how to do a particular step of an operation. They give a standard method for carrying out any task, so each task is repeatable and reproducible.

## Benefits of the prerequisites

Takt time maximises productivity because:

- processes are easy to manage
- workflow is matched to customer demand
- the output of each process matches customer demand.

Standard operations give you:

- capable, repeatable processes
- process control at the source
- more accurate planning
- better adherence to plans
- a platform for continuous improvement
- reduced costs
- improved quality
- a basis for training.

Achieve consistency in your operations. It lets you meet your customers' requirements by managing production effectively. Variation in operations demands more human intervention, which increases the risk of human error.

"Without standardisation, there can be no improvement", according to Taiichi Ohno. Contrary to common belief, every process can be standardised. The level of standardisation depends only on how complex the process is, and on how much outside factors can bring variation into it. For example, a telesales process would be standardised at a higher level, because of the variation the customer can introduce. There we would use a "framework" or "call structure" to keep the process on track.
