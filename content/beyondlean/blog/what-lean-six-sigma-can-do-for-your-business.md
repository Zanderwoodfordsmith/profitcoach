## We're not a manufacturer. Does it apply to us?

The Lean Six Sigma principles apply to any business, manufacturing or not. I was part of a six-man team in an airline catering company that produced over $10,000,000 of savings in just six months. I have colleagues in the financial sector applying exactly the same tools and techniques they used in manufacturing, and getting even better results, because the processes are much simpler.

Remember that Lean deals mainly with two things: people and processes (machines count as part of the processes). So any business that has people and processes can be improved using Lean Six Sigma. I haven't yet come across an industry that can't be improved this way. In the UK, the National Health Service, Royal Mail and local government agencies, to name but a few, have all gone down the Lean Six Sigma road.

## My business already makes money. Why bother?

Why leave money on the table?

If you are already making money without Lean Six Sigma, you are more than likely leaving cash on the counter. And how long will it be before your competitors catch up with you, or even overtake you? Imagine if they started to make the same product to a better quality standard at a third of the cost, especially if you're competing with companies in the emerging markets of China and India. How long would it be before your market share took a nasty dip?

You either move forward and improve, or you begin to decline. The laws of the universe don't allow for standing still (not for very long, anyway).

## How will it actually improve my bottom line?

There is something called "the essential business equation", which has three parts: costs, selling price and profit. Your profit is your selling price minus your costs. Of those two, the only one you can really affect is your costs, because the market decides the selling price to a greater or lesser degree. (You can't suddenly put a retail price of $100,000 on a two-door family saloon because you want more profit. The market just won't buy it.) So the one you can really take control of is your costs.

Reduced costs mean increased profits. Lean Six Sigma concentrates on reducing waste in the operation to cut costs, so that the work you do lines up more closely with what the customer is actually willing to pay for.

## We tried improvement fads before and they didn't work

All improvement methods will fail if the management commitment isn't there. Lean Six Sigma needs everyone, from the CEO to the people on the front line, to change the way they work.

If the person at the top isn't willing to walk the walk and lead by example, your road to Lean Six Sigma will be a very slow one, and you may well be out of business before you get there. If the person at the top is on board, actively waving the flag and leading by example, your business will flourish and prosper, and you'll leave your competitors eating your dust.

## My people work flat out and do overtime, but we still can't keep up with orders

Some of the most basic Lean Six Sigma diagnostic tools will very quickly show what your production rate needs to be, along with the ideal number of people. Working to takt time and a simple rebalance of the work could be all you need to get more work out with fewer people and no overtime.

Every situation is different, of course, but a good value stream map will very quickly show you where your bottlenecks and main areas of waste are. (And they probably aren't where you think they are.)

## My people work flat out and do overtime, but we still make no profit at the end of the month

Your people are working flat out and doing overtime. Are they working flat out on the right things? If you spent all morning digging a hole and all afternoon filling it back in, you'd have worked hard all day, but what would you have to show for it?

There are three kinds of work:

- **Value-adding work** changes the form or function of a product so that it is closer to what the customer wants. If you make cakes, mixing the ingredients is value-adding work. In the finance sector, reviewing a loan application and making a decision on it adds value.
- **Incidental work**, or non-value-adding but essential work, is needed with your current processes and resources. In the cake example, inspecting the cakes when they come out of the oven adds no value to the product in itself, but it must be done to make sure the quality is right.
- **Waste** is the third kind, and it is what Lean Six Sigma concentrates on removing.

Waste is the use of any material or resource beyond what the customer needs and is willing to pay for. Finding and removing it improves the performance of the business.

## The seven wastes

Watch Paul's video: [The 7 wastes](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_3hUxpznprQ)

There are seven wastes:

- **Overproduction:** making it for the sake of it
- **Inventory:** high raw material stocks, for example
- **Waiting:** long set-ups and waiting times
- **Motion:** walking, lifting, putting down
- **Transportation:** unnecessary movement and extra handling
- **Rework:** anything that isn't right first time, which hits customer satisfaction
- **Overprocessing:** processing beyond the standard the customer needs

There is also an eighth waste: the under-use of people. People are your greatest asset, so learn to get the best out of them.

## Just get it going

Don't worry about getting it right; just get it going. The worst thing you can do is nothing. If you keep doing what you've always done, you'll keep getting what you've always got. The things that got you into your current situation are not the things that will get you out of it.

The traditional focus in manufacturing is to work harder, faster and for longer. The Lean approach is to improve the process, to remove waste and to work smarter and safer. That is the aim of Lean Six Sigma.
