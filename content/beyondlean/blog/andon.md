Andon is one of the three elements that make up the principle of jidoka.

## Jidoka and andon

Jidoka is the principle of stopping work immediately when a problem occurs. It is often described as "automation with a human element". It is made up of three elements:

1. Andon
2. A full work system
3. Error proofing (poka yoke)

## So what does andon look like?

Andon is an information tool. It gives the operations team an instant, visible and audible warning that there is an abnormality in their area.

![Andon: an abnormality, an indication on the board, and a response](/beyondlean/blog/andon/andon.jpg)

## What an andon system does

1. It allows timely corrective action, by alerting people when abnormal conditions occur.
2. It lets shop floor team leaders spend less time and effort monitoring the situation, and more time solving abnormalities.
3. It lets operations teams monitor equipment and people more effectively.
4. It can act as a two-way communication device. For example, when the indicator returns to green, it tells everybody it's back to normal.

## What it doesn't do

1. Solve abnormalities.
2. Prevent all defects from being passed forward.
3. Replace good verbal communication between work groups.
4. Remove the need for rectification or customer protection.

## Direct benefits

- **Control of production:** operators have the ability to "stop, call, wait".
- **Defects reported and corrected:** operators can report faults straight away, and countermeasures can be put in place at the source.
- **Safety and ergonomics:** it identifies safety and body-stress concerns. Even loading (balanced processes) lets you rebalance a process if overburden occurs.
- **Workable design:** it highlights problems with work density.

## Implementation guidelines

- Put it in after standard operations are in place (stability).
- Team structure, ratios, roles and responsibilities.
- Identify work zones and stations.
- Divide the process into manageable steps.
- Decide what conditions must be measured.
- Design the board.
- Set the escalation procedure.
- Decide the support structure.
- Set the criteria for collecting downtime data.
- Decide the confirmation points and how often to check them.
- Set up an effective communication structure.
- Make the status of problem solving visible.

Andon systems need to be thought through, and designed to suit, to be effective. Put them in when, and only when, a suitable support system and escalation procedure is in place (service level agreements, a problem-solving process and so on).

Signals should be simple and easy to understand.

Avoid spending too much money on a "state of the art" system. Prove the idea with a simple manual system first, to judge its worth.
