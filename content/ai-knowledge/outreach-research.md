# Outreach research: 110,000 connection requests

What BCA learned from 110,769 LinkedIn connection requests sent by 43 coaches between July 2025 and October 2026 (old platform, Sales Robot exports, synced inboxes and bookings). Reviewed in October 2026 and shared with coaches at the "100,000 Connection Requests" workshop on 19 October 2026.

## How to use this

- Treat every number as a guide, not a law. Say "in our data" or "across our coaches", never "studies prove".
- When you quote a number, add the caveat that goes with it in one short line (see "Read the numbers with care"). Don't bury the answer under caveats.
- Meetings are what matter, not accepts or replies. Steer people to count meetings booked.
- Never name a coach or quote another coach's message. Describe the pattern instead ("a coach writing to women founders").
- Close with something they can do: test both, count meetings, pick a tribe, answer within the hour.

## Out of every 100 requests

- About 17 accepted (16.8%). The market average is about 28% (Expandi, 13.3 million invites), so there is room.
- About 4 or 5 replied.
- About 1 was interested.
- Booked calls: roughly 1 or 2 per 1,000 requests.

## Note or no note

| Connection note | Accepted | Meeting requests per 100 |
|---|---|---|
| Blank (no note) | 17.8% | 0.12 |
| Any other note | 14.2% | 0.19 |
| Proof note: "I ask because" plus a real result | 11.9% | 0.31 |
| A link in the note | 11.2% | 0.00 |

- No note gets the most accepts. A short note with a real result gets fewer accepts but 2 to 3 times the meeting requests. In short: "Fewer accept a note. More of them meet."
- On interested replies, proof notes only tie with no note (0.85 vs 0.91 per 100). Don't claim proof beats blank on interest; it wins on meetings.
- A link in a note from a stranger is the worst thing we tried. The BOSS Score link: 0.19 interested per 100, against 1.27 for a normal note. Send the score after a conversation starts.
- Speaking to a tribe gets similar meeting rates to a proof note (0.30 to 0.49 per 100).
- The proof note shape: "I see you run a UK [industry] company. Are you looking to [simple outcome]? I ask because [as role at company, or with a client like them] I used a simple system to [before to after, with numbers] in [time]. Is this of interest?"

## Read the numbers with care

People aren't perfect, and neither is this data. Say the caveat that fits when you quote the note numbers.

- **Not every note was a good note.** The "note" groups mix strong and weak messages. Plenty were vague, all about the coach (ego-led), had no real number, or asked to coach the person or sell a call in the first message. When we scored 38 real notes against our own checklist, the score did not predict results, so we don't yet know how many followed the template well. A well-written proof note could do better than the 11.9% and 0.31 shown.
- **Who they wrote to matters as much as what they wrote.** Some coaches using notes were writing to sectors that barely use LinkedIn (trades, construction, manufacturing, care). That pulls their accept rate down whatever the note says. So part of the gap between 17.8% and 11.9% is targeting, not the note.
- **Market changes everything.** One proof note to law firms abroad got 2.63 meeting requests per 100, the best in all the data. The same note to UK law firms got 1 from 993. That campaign is left out of the table.
- **Meeting requests are coaches' own tags,** not a count of booked calls, and the numbers are small. Some coaches tag heavily, some hardly at all, so "interested" rates are a rough guide. Compare acceptance and reply rates per request where you can.
- **One coach's test can't settle it.** At about 1 interested reply per 100 requests, a test needs thousands of people per side to show a real difference. Of 47 coach A/B tests we found (100 to 700 people per side), only three gave a clear answer. Tell coaches to test for a feel, and that BCA runs the bigger tests across everyone.
- **It's old-platform data.** The rewritten templates (from 19 October 2026) haven't been measured yet.

So the honest answer to "note or no note?" is: both can work. No note gets more connections; a short, specific note with a real result tends to get more meetings. Test both on the same kind of people and count meetings, not connections.

## Who you ask beats what you say

Interested replies per 100 requests, by job title (same campaigns, same coaches):

- Founder 2.46, Co-founder 1.66, CEO or Partner 1.38, Managing Director 0.76, Owner 0.52. Founders answer about 4.7 times more than people titled "Owner".
- Active on LinkedIn (open profile): 26% accept, 2.24 interested per 100. Quiet: 14% accept, 0.93. So active people answer about twice as often.

LinkedIn score by sector (out of 10, from about 58,000 matched requests):

- LinkedIn first: consulting and professional services 9, IT and tech 8, recruitment and HR 7, accountancy and finance 7, property 7, education and training 7, marketing and media 7.
- LinkedIn plus email: retail and e-commerce 6, legal 5, charity and public sector 5, energy 5.
- Other channels first (email, phone, post, events, partners; Facebook and Instagram for trades): construction 4, engineering and manufacturing 4, trades 4, logistics 4, hospitality 4, automotive 4, health and care 3. Trades acceptance was 2 to 7%. That's a channel problem, not a copy problem. The channel suggestions are our recommendation and not yet tested.

Tribes get people talking. A tribe is a group people choose or feel part of: a place, a role, a moment.

- Women founders: 13% replied (average 4.5%).
- Digital founders who may be raising money: 8.2% replied.
- Owners in one region ("Midlands business owners"): 32% accepted.
- Directors ("Being a director is a job in itself. One that nobody prepares you for."): 25.6% accepted.

## Message or list: 38 reviewed notes

Coaches sent Zander 38 connection notes for review (Nov 2024 to Jul 2025); 22 came back with an interest rate the coach reported (4 at 2% or more, 3 at 1.5 to 2%, 6 at 1 to 1.5%, 9 under 1%). Self-reported, on lists of mixed quality, so a guide only.

- **Jargon hooks failed every time.** Every hook in jargon, odd wording or about something other than the owner's main want got under 1%: "improve MRR/ARR", "improve risk management", "supercharging your sales people", "reduce staff turnover". The 2% notes asked for one plain thing: profit, revenue, margins, new listings.
- **A pain hook did no harm.** "Are you struggling to improve your margins?" got over 2%.
- **Proof needs a result they can picture.** The two notes with no result at all got under 1%. The one note without a figure that still got over 2% had a vivid before and after: a home-kitchen recipe to market leader.
- **A good note can't fix a bad list.** Strong notes to electrical contractors and toy distributors got under 1%; the same shape got over 2% from UK construction owners and IT consultancies.
- **A weak note wastes a good list.** Recruitment, publishing, software and financial services owners are on LinkedIn, and the notes to them that failed all had a jargon hook or no number.
- **The industry callout beat {company}.** 11 of 16 notes naming the industry got 1% or more, against 2 of 5 using the company name.
- **The hook must point at what the coach sells.** A hook about getting new listings got over 2%, but most calls thought the coach ran a sales agency.

How to tell which it is: if few accept, look at the list (sector, titles, active profiles). If they accept but don't reply, look at the message.

## Their LinkedIn profile

- A full About section (1,200+ characters) and 1,000+ connections go with about 4 to 6 points more acceptance. Cheap to fix.
- Headline wording showed no clear effect. Treat these as signals; only 37 coaches had enough data.

## After someone replies

- 29% of prospects who replied never got an answer (796 replies in synced inboxes), including 1 in 8 who said they wanted to talk.
- The typical answer came 4 hours 17 minutes later, and that's the good end (only coaches synced to the new app). Interest peaks when they reply; answer within the hour.
- Offer two specific times, with the calendar link as a backup. One slot booked 21.6% of the time, two slots 43.4% (Boomerang). Suggested times got about 3 times the meetings of a bare link (Chili Piper). Example: "Great, Sarah. Would Tuesday at 10:30 or Wednesday at 2pm (UK time) work for a 20-minute call? If neither suits, here's my calendar."
- Keep the first reply short. Answering "I'd love to learn how you can help" with "Thanks", or 28 days later, loses the call.

## Follow-ups

- Replies came 20% before any message, 17% after message 1, 14% after message 2, then 8%, 7% and 4%. Later follow-ups still bring about a fifth of the interest, so keep them and make them better.
- Give, don't chase. Follow-ups that give something useful can run for months; "just checking you saw my message" burns the relationship.
- Calls take months, not days. Matt Lakajev counted his own: a booked call took on average 26 messages over 1 to 4 months. Only about 3% of a market is ready to buy now.
- What hurt in the old follow-up script: 14% pushed a "free coaching discovery call", 9% used fake scarcity, 9% had a merge bug ("HeyThomas,"), and some opened "as I mentioned in my connection message" after a blank invite.

## The rules we now teach

1. Blank invite, or a note with real proof. No links.
2. First message under 300 characters. One question.
3. Answer every reply within the hour.
4. Offer a next step, not "coaching" or a "free call".
5. Give, don't chase. Never "just checking".

Start with people who already know you: messages to existing connections got about twice the replies of cold ones.
