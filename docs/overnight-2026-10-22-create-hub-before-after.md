## Connector campaign

**Question:** Write me a connection note for UK manufacturing owners doing £2-5M. Give me your recommended one plus two variants.

### Before

To write you the strongest possible connection note, I need one piece of information first.

**What proof are we leading with?**

Your brain doesn't have any client results or career wins loaded yet. The connection message lives or dies on the proof line - it's what makes the "I ask because..." land.

Pick whichever fits you:

**A) Client proof** - a business you've helped get a specific result (e.g. "Grew profit from £180K to £420K in 18 months")

**B) Career proof** - something you achieved inside a company or your own business (e.g. "As Operations Director at X, I reduced waste costs by £300K in 2 years")

What have you got? Even a rough number and a timeframe gives me enough to work with.

### After

Before I write these, I need one thing from you: **what proof are you leading with?**

The template lives or dies on a real before-and-after number. A few quick options:

**A) You have a client result** - give me: company/role, before revenue or profit, after, time frame, and what the mechanism was (what actually changed).

**B) You have a career result** - give me: company, your role, before, after, time frame, mechanism.

**C) Neither yet** - tell me and I'll write placeholder versions you can slot proof into once you have it.

Which is it?

## LinkedIn posts

**Question:** Give me three LinkedIn post ideas for owners who are great at the work but weak on pipeline, and write the best one in full.

### Before

Three angles that hit the "great at the work, weak on pipeline" tension:

**1. The skill flip**
The thing that made you exceptional at delivery is the same thing working against you in sales. You solve problems. Pipeline requires you to sit with uncertainty. One skill. Two opposite demands.

**2. The referral trap**
Most great operators build a business entirely on reputation. It works — until it doesn't. The month the referrals dry up is the month you realise you never built a pipeline. You built a waiting game.

**3. The busyness illusion**
Full capacity feels like success. It's actually the moment your pipeline dies. You stop outreach when you're busy. Then you're desperate when you're not. The cycle repeats until you break it deliberately.

---

**The best one, written in full:**

---

Most great operators never crack sales.

Not because they don't know their numbers. Not because they can't close. But because the skill that makes them exceptional at delivery actively works against them in pipeline.

Think about what makes someone brilliant at the work. They solve problems as they appear. They respond to what's in front of them. They prioritise depth over breadth.

Pipeline is the opposite. It's long game. It's contacting people before they need you. It's staying visible when there's nothing urgent to respond to.

The same orientation that makes you reliable in delivery makes you inconsistent in outreach. You wait until it's urgent. Then urgency feels desperate. Then you land clients under pressure and wonder why the fit is always slightly off.

The fix isn't to become a natural salesperson. It's to build a system that doesn't rely on motivation.

Same appointments in the diary each week. Same outreach rhythm. Same follow-up cadence. Not because you feel like it — because the system runs whether you do or not.

The best operators I've seen crack this aren't the ones who got better at sales. They're the ones who stopped relying on themselves to feel like doing it.

What does your outreach look like when you're at full capacity?

---

A couple of notes: your brain is empty so I've written this from the methodology, not personal proof. If you've seen this pattern in a real client, drop me a line or two and I'll rewrite the close with your voice and their result behind it. Want me to do the other two as well?

### After

Here are three angles that hit the gap between delivery skill and pipeline discipline:

---

**1. The "invisible trap" post**
Most owners who are great at the work stop marketing the moment they get busy. Then the project ends and the pipeline is empty. They were never bad at getting clients - they just only did it when they were desperate. The post names this cycle and shows how a simple weekly rhythm breaks it.

**2. The "proof is already there" post**
The results you're delivering for current clients are the best marketing content you have. You're just not using them. The post walks through how to turn one client win into a LinkedIn post, a case study hook, and a conversation starter - in under an hour.

**3. The "best in town, nobody knows" post**
There's a version of your business where you're fully booked, not because you got better at the work, but because more of the right people knew you existed. The post draws the line between craft skill (which the owner has) and pipeline visibility (which they're ignoring).

---

## Full post: The invisible trap

---

The best tradespeople, engineers, and service owners I know have the same problem.

They're brilliant at the work. Clients love them. Referrals come in.

But they only market when they're scared.

Project wraps up. Pipeline looks thin. Panic sets in. They hustle, post a few things, chase a few contacts. Work comes in. They get busy again. Marketing stops.

Three months later, same situation.

It's not a skill problem. It's a rhythm problem.

The owners who stay fully booked don't outwork you. They don't have a bigger team or a fancier funnel. They just never stop the minimum viable version of pipeline activity, even when they're busy.

One post a week. One conversation a week. One check-in with a past client a month.

That's it. It takes about an hour across a full week.

But here's what that hour compounds into over a year: you stop having empty months. You stop chasing work at the worst possible time. You stop discounting because you need a yes.

The feast-or-famine cycle isn't inevitable. It's what happens when pipeline only gets attention during famine.

If your diary is full right now, that's exactly the right time to start.

## Choose ICP

**Question:** I've coached a few construction firms and an accountancy practice. Help me pick my first market to target on LinkedIn.

### Before

Good start - two very different markets, which actually makes this easier to decide.

Let me give you a quick read on both, then a recommendation.

---

**Construction firms**

- High pain, high urgency. Cash flow pressure, site chaos, owner doing everything.
- LinkedIn presence is patchy but growing. Directors and MDs of mid-size firms (£1M-£20M) are findable by title and industry.
- They respond to operational, numbers-led language. "You're losing money on jobs and don't know which ones" lands hard.
- Referral culture is strong - one good client in construction opens doors to three more.
- Objection: "We're not really a LinkedIn business." True of some, less true of the decision-makers you want.

**Accountancy practices**

- Lower urgency pain. They know their numbers better than most, which cuts both ways - harder to shock them with data, but easier to have a peer-level business conversation.
- Very well-represented on LinkedIn. Practice owners, partners, and firm directors are active.
- Competitive space - lots of coaches targeting this niche already.
- Smaller revenue ceiling in many cases (sole practitioner or small firm doesn't hit £1M easily).

---

**My recommendation: construction firms**

Here's why this one wins right now.

1. The pain is more acute. A construction director drowning in site management and cash flow problems feels the problem every day. An accountant's pain is real but lower temperature.
2. Your proof is in that world already. Even a few clients beats zero in a new market, and construction people trust people who've worked with construction people.
3. Less coaching competition. Accountancy is well-trodden territory on LinkedIn.
4. The referral multiplier. One construction client who sees results will talk. They all know each other.

---

Before I lock this in for you - one question: when you worked with the construction firms, what size were they roughly (turnover or headcount), and what was the main thing you helped them fix?

That shapes how we frame the ICP and tells me whether LinkedIn can actually source enough of these people for you.

### After

Good starting point. Two real markets already, which is more signal than most coaches start with.

Before I rank them, a couple of quick questions to sharpen the recommendation:

**On the construction firms:** Were these smaller contractors (under £2M revenue) or more established businesses with a team and real operations? And what did you actually help them with - profit margin, pricing, getting out of the day-to-day?

That will tell me whether construction is a strong fit or just a familiar face.
