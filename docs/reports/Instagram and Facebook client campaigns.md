# Get seen first, then ask one question

The people who really book calls from Instagram and Facebook run a slow, personal campaign by hand, in three steps. First they get seen before they write: a follow, two likes and a real comment, a Facebook friend request, or a helpful answer in an owners' group. Then they send one short message that sells nothing and asks one easy question. Only after the owner replies do they offer something free that owners would normally pay for, then a short call at two set times. **Warm beats cold by a wide margin.** Hormozi's warm benchmark is 100 messages, 20 replies, 4 who take the free offer and 1 paying client. The honest cold Instagram tests get 4 to 7 replies per 100 and 0.5 to 5 booked calls, and one agency messaged about 150 gyms on Facebook for a single reply. The best-proven campaign in Paul Hamnett's sector comes from a gym-industry agency owner. He says all of his **roughly 45 gym clients came from Facebook friend requests and short Messenger chats**, done in 20 to 30 minutes a day. For Bruce Alexander's food and drink founders, nobody has published an Instagram campaign with numbers. Founders gather in named rooms (Bread & Jam's 12,000-member Facebook group, retailer accelerators, The Grocer) and on LinkedIn, so Instagram works there as a warm-up, with LinkedIn and email doing the main work. No source shows a business coach winning £1M+ UK owners this way with numbers. So the three campaigns at the end are my drafts, built from the practitioners' rules, to test for 30 days. The campaign to teach is the same in every niche. Warm contacts and referrals come first, people who engage with the coach second, and a short list of named owners warmed up by hand third. The offer is the free BOSS Score plus the coach's own two-minute read of it. UK rules keep it small and honest. A DM counts as email, so limited companies are fine if you give your name and an easy way out, while sole traders must agree first. On Instagram a stranger can send one text until the owner accepts, and WhatsApp only follows a yes.

*How to read this report. Each source carries a label. **Shows results** means the person shows real chats, posts, analytics or a live test on screen. **Own numbers** means they report their own figures without proof. **Advice** means guidance with no figures, including template sites and tool makers. **Course pitch** means the numbers sit inside a sale of a course, community or tool. Many sources carry two labels. Most sources are YouTube auto-captions, quoted word for word with obvious mishearings fixed. Reddit could not be read. Every script written for Paul, Bruce or BCA coaches is my draft, built from the practitioners' rules and not yet tested with owners. Nothing here is legal advice.*

## Raised hands beat cold messages, so start with people who know you

Nobody in this research sells business coaching to owners of £1M+ firms through Instagram or Facebook and publishes the numbers. The people who publish are agency owners, freelancers, DM "setters" (people paid to start chats and book calls for a coach), coaches who sell to other coaches, and a few firms that sell to gym owners. Almost every channel with big numbers sells a course. So the thing to copy is the shape of what works, and the small honest tests show the likely rates.

Owners are already flooded with pitches. One agency owner could see a local owner's inbox, which "used to get at least 20 to 25 messages a day" in cold DMs ([Adam Consult, Jan 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BZvcNrfWLiY), own numbers and course pitch). The biggest gym-owner mentoring firm says agencies "throw around impressive-sounding metrics" such as "We'll get you 30 leads in 30 days!" It also says it removes marketers from its owners' group "every week for lying about being gym owners" ([Two-Brain Business, Feb 2026](https://twobrainbusiness.com/why-ad-agencies-fail-gym-owners/), advice). Hormozi names the problem in one word: "Cold outreach has one key difference from warm outreach. Trust. Strangers don't trust you" ([$100M Leads audiobook, Part 5](https://podscripts.co/podcasts/the-game-with-alex-hormozi/part-5-cold-outreach-100m-leads-book), own numbers). The owner's first test is "do I know this person?", and every campaign below is built to pass it.

The rates show it. Hormozi's warm benchmark is **100 reach-outs, about 20 replies, about 4 who take the free offer, about 1 paying customer** ([Part 3](https://podscripts.co/podcasts/the-game-with-alex-hormozi/part-3-warm-outreach-100m-leads-book)). Cold is far lower. A new agency owner sent the same DM to 100 home-improvement firms and got 4 replies in a day and no calls ([Ocean Break Media, Feb 2024](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=asVRb34EX5g), shows results). Another sent 1,100 cold Instagram DMs to business owners for 57 positive replies and 6 booked calls ([Laura Cohen on Skool](https://www.skool.com/the-messy-middle-3939/results-after-sending-1100-cold-instagram-dms-to-business-owners), own numbers). About 150 cold Facebook messages to gyms got one reply ([Skool, GoHighLevel community](https://www.skool.com/gohighlevel/dr-client-using-robbs-closing-script), own numbers). Warming up cold people first helps. One live test that followed, liked, commented and replied to a story before writing got 6 replies and 2 calls from 39 DMs ([Charlie Barber, Sep 2025](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IdAfHxufsOU), shows results). People who raise a hand do best of all. A gym firm says chat selling closes 3 to 5% of chats across industries, and "around 12-15 percent" book when the chat's goal is a free consultation, though it doesn't show where those figures come from ([Two-Brain, Jul 2021](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/21538260-sell-by-chat-for-gyms-does-it-work), own numbers).

## Finding owners takes about two minutes a profile

### On Instagram, start from a list or from an account owners follow

Practitioners use six ways to find owners on Instagram. The simplest is to type the trade into Instagram search and open accounts one by one ([Megan Weeks, Jan 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ma9vXpJLRzk), shows results). The second starts outside Instagram. Search Google Maps for the trade and town, drop the national chains, then look each business up on Instagram by hand ([Ocean Break Media](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=asVRb34EX5g)). The third is a Google search limited to instagram.com plus the trade and a place, which gives "around 50" profiles per run by hand ([Patrick Spiegel, Sep 2024](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4e--urNopKk), own numbers and course pitch). The fourth uses a "seed" account that owners follow, such as a supplier, software firm or well-known adviser. Open its followers or following list and search the list for a trade word. Charlie Barber worked a fitness business coach's following list this way and says it lets you "really easily send out 50 DMs in a matter of 30 minutes" ([Charlie Barber](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IdAfHxufsOU)). Patrick Spiegel picks medium-sized seeds, such as a 6,800-follower construction software account, because big accounts' lists can't be browsed fully. The fifth takes the people who liked or commented on a seed account's best posts ([Pedro and Zach, Apr 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ub6hcCXbYzo), course pitch). The sixth is to follow one good prospect and work through the "Suggested for you" accounts Instagram shows next, checking each by hand ([Patrick Spiegel](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4e--urNopKk)).

Whichever way you use, message the owner's personal account, not the brand's. Jordan Platten found owners by Googling "Instagram [first name] [last name]" and checked the brand's Facebook page first for a gap to mention ([Jordan Platten, 2020](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qa6U3soYe2g), older). For a UK coach, Google Maps first fits best, because Maps shows size signs that Instagram hides: review count, number of sites and opening hours (my inference).

### On Facebook, Google the owner, then send a friend request

Facebook switched off search by job or employer in 2019 ([TechCrunch, Jun 2019](https://techcrunch.com/2019/06/10/changes-to-facebook-graph-search-leaves-online-investigators-in-a-lurch)), so practitioners find owners two ways. The first is groups. "Go to the Facebook search bar and type in the type of business you want to work with... Then, click on groups." Skip a group whose latest post is about six months old, join five to ten, and spend "the first week, do nothing". Write down the questions that keep coming up, "because those questions are your content" ([Adam Erhart, May 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2OHrMEvTPOc), advice). Inside a group, the best targets are owners asking for help. Trey Cockrum's rule is "comment right now and give them the advice you would give your clients", because other owners with the same problem read it and message you ([Trey Cockrum](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4iIZAexOR8s)). Friend requests go to the group's "members with things in common" list, because those people share friends with you. Check each one by hovering, since "clicking on a lot of those profiles... is going to look robotic to Facebook" ([Ron Medlin, Aug 2023](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yuvEFxLBCBo), own numbers and course pitch).

The second way finds owners directly, and it is the one that built a 45-client gym agency. "You can't exactly go type in gym owner... Facebook's smart to that." Dave Panchcham listed the gyms near him, then "I would type in XYZ Fitness, and then I put in quotes owner... In Google... take that owner's name, pop him into Facebook search, and then I would add him." Once an owner accepted, "I went through his friends list and I looked for other gym owners", and soon "Facebook started referring me all of these people that were already in my niche." He checked each owner's business Page under "Page transparency" for when it was created: "If that page is brand new... Do not pass go" ([Dave Panchcham](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kJ_RPeodSig)). A cruder local version is a post in a town's buy-and-sell group, "Who else is a small business owner in Carlsbad? Thought I would bring us all together", which got 193 comments ([Dino Gomez, 2020](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MzWPzZAI_oo), shows results, older). Many UK local groups would likely remove that post (my inference). One recent change matters too. Since November 2025, when a private group goes public, only admins can see its member list ([Meta Newsroom](https://about.fb.com/news/2025/11/facebook-update-group-admins-flexibility-protecting-member-privacy/)).

### Qualify by eye, then check the company

Practitioners qualify by eye: recent posts, a real business, the owner on show, and the gap they can fix. A self-published guide adds a prospect only if they meet three of four tests (industry, size, relevant posts, decision-maker), working in batches of about 20 at **about 2 minutes per profile** ([Chrysales](https://www.chrysales.com/post/instagram-dm-strategy-sales-calls), advice). Adam Consult warns against the most pitched targets and very big accounts, which rarely reply ([Adam Consult](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BZvcNrfWLiY)). Nobody showed a way to tell a £1M firm from a sole trader on Instagram. For a coach, the signs to look for are growth and an owner who is stretched: hiring posts, a new site, team photos, several locations, many reviews (my inference). Hormozi adds one: "job postings. Typically people are not hiring when they're shrinking" ([Workshop Session 4](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1CnZZ-WwnhxP3xVJHrAvOadsbayrXfZBK/view), own numbers). A Companies House check then confirms the business is a limited company and names the directors. That settles what you may legally send (see the UK rules section). At two minutes a profile, a coach can find and check about 30 owners in an hour. Dave Panchcham sent 40 to 50 friend requests in "20, 30 minutes".

## The warm-up is a day of small touches and a profile worth clicking

Every practitioner since August 2023 treats the first job as getting out of the "Requests" folder and into the owner's main inbox. Charlie Barber's version: "I would always follow, maybe like one of their posts, we can comment something as well and just go in with a story popup. So the goal is to purely just get a response... because once you get a response, you're then in the actual inbox. So when you present your offer, the person will actually see it" ([Charlie Barber](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IdAfHxufsOU)). Emperor Dino comments first: "Let's say you write two or three comments the day before. So they'll say, okay, I remember Dino from my comments" ([Emperor Dino, Nov 2025](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=txidjZ-yr0U), course pitch). Charlie Morgan, who claims about 5,000 booked calls, connects before anything else: "We need some level of connection to actually you know reach out to the person" ([Charlie Morgan, Feb 2025](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3OR6sMC1dEY), shows results and course pitch). The rule behind this is Meta's. A reply lands in the person's inbox "if the person follows" you, and otherwise in Requests ([Meta for Developers](https://developers.facebook.com/docs/instagram-platform/private-replies/)). One designer's DM with a picture attached showed that the doctor "doesn't accept messages from everyone". The doctor then followed her back, which she felt "pushed that message" through ([Megan Weeks](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ma9vXpJLRzk)). Nobody tested how long the warm-up should be. Same day or the day before is normal. Nobody could confirm whether a story reply from a stranger lands in the inbox or in Requests.

On Facebook the warm-up is the friend request. Aaron Spikes sent requests to everyone who liked or commented on his post, "and then I would wait 24 hours... I don't want to end up in their request box" ([Trey Cockrum and Aaron Spikes](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4iIZAexOR8s)). When someone accepts, say hello at once. Dave Panchcham's reason: "if somebody responds to you... that tells Facebook that oh these two people must know each other... if you make a post on your Facebook profile, it will show up in their feed" ([Dave Panchcham](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kJ_RPeodSig)). Messenger's own help says a message from someone you're not connected with arrives as a "message request" ([Messenger Help](https://www.messenger.com/help/936247526442073)). Start slowly: "start with five a day for the first week, then bump it up to 10, then 15 the week after that" ([Adam Erhart](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2OHrMEvTPOc)).

The owner clicks your profile before replying, so the profile is the landing page. Hormozi: "If you do outreach from a channel that has literally no posts, people be like, 'This is a scam.' And rightfully so... if you post three times a week and you have something that's relevant to your avatar... they will click over to your profile before responding" ([MoreMozi, Jan 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KIs-egpg1bg)). Dave Panchcham's cover photo said: "I help fitness studios charging $100 a month to add an additional 10 to 30 new members per month. Ask me how." Before he had testimonials he made "two-minute videos on my cell phone talking about solutions to those problems", about once a week. He deleted posts that got little engagement and mixed in family photos "so you look like a real person". One owner messaged him first: "Hey there. I'd like to chat about your services. I own two F45 studios and I'm interested in understanding how your product aligns with our strategies." The owner later said he wrote after watching Panchcham's video on the problems with six-week challenges ([Dave Panchcham](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kJ_RPeodSig)). For owner-only groups, the profile must also look like a peer: "if you want to get into a group with the gym owners, you don't want to look like a marketer" (same source).

The warm-up I recommend for a coach (my inference from these sources) has three steps. On day 0, follow the owner's personal account and the business, like two recent posts, leave one specific comment, and send a Facebook friend request. On day 1, reply to a story or send the opener. Send voice or video only after a reply or a follow-back. For five to ten dream accounts, one agency tags the prospect in a custom video on its own story, which skips the DM folder entirely. The agency claims a "90% response rate" from one test, but each video takes about two hours ([Klabunde Media, Apr 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DSY3w-hPWf4), advice).

## Openers ask one easy question and sell nothing

The openers that worked in live tests are short questions about the owner, with no pitch and no link. Alicia Joseph, messaging tradespeople, skipped personal detail. She opened "hi [name], just a quick one", then asked whether they were getting enough work. Her reason: "I get a lot of these personalized DMs... I don't even bother reading the rest because it's just too much paragraph" ([Alicia Joseph, Jul 2024](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9aY92C0HYTo), own numbers). Copy-paste blasts failed: "I was copying the same bland and vague templates to around 50 to 60 people... none of them actually responded." The same freelancer now sends between 10am and 4pm in the prospect's time zone, after "getting cussed out" for 2am messages ([aiDr, Jul 2025](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3-jKacJ1ygY), own numbers). Hormozi found that rewriting his cold template "below a third grade reading level" got "50% more leads" to respond ([Part 5](https://podscripts.co/podcasts/the-game-with-alex-hormozi/part-5-cold-outreach-100m-leads-book)).

### Tactics in the notes to avoid

Several popular tactics get replies by misleading the owner, and BCA should teach coaches to avoid them. The worst are openers that pretend the sender might be a customer. One agency member posted "Hey I'm just checking out gyms nearby. Do you guys do personal training or just open memberships?" and still got under 30% replies ([Skool, Agency World](https://www.skool.com/agencyworld/dm-response-rate-30)). Henry Fortunatow says his qualifier opener works "because people think you're a potential client" ([Henry Fortunatow, Feb 2023](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZoOBHfY7kEE), course pitch). Patrick Spiegel calls his question-then-sell method "a bit sleazy but it works" ([Patrick Spiegel](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4e--urNopKk)). The opener "hey is this a good place for questions to the owner decision maker?" just failed ([Adam Consult](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BZvcNrfWLiY)). The best setter manual found has the setter write and even speak as the coach: "The prospect never knows it is not her. No exceptions" ([SFL setter manual](https://sfl-operations.pages.dev/setter-sop)). One agency owner suggests bluffing past owner-only groups' joining questions, since "Most admins aren't checking profiles closely" ([Adam Erhart](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2OHrMEvTPOc)). Gym groups catch and remove exactly these people ([Two-Brain](https://twobrainbusiness.com/why-ad-agencies-fail-gym-owners/)). Extra profiles per assistant break Facebook's rule to "Only create one account (your own)" ([Facebook Terms](https://www.facebook.com/terms.php)). Hormozi's rule covers the rest: "Make automation obvious and make manual obvious. What I would encourage you not to do is try to make your automation seem manual" ([Workshop Session 7](https://drive.google.com/file/d/12vnRWKUjzNuoLytJuYY9RQcGGTgdty8u/view)). Being open even helps. When one coach added "This is an automated message from Alex" to her welcome message, "that one line changed everything" ([Alex Cattoni, Jul 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=a4QDpAYEh1Q), own numbers, sponsored by ManyChat). If someone else sends messages for a coach, they use their own name, for example "I help Paul with his diary".

## Chats run in three stages, one question per message

### Connect, find the problem, then offer the call

The clearest framework comes from a working setter manual. "1. Connection. Build a real, friendly connection first... 2. Struggles... Ask permission first, then dig into the challenge, what they tried, how long, their goal, and the why behind it. 3. Call. Once you know you can help them and they want help, pitch the quick call." Each message follows a pattern it calls RAQ: Repeat what they said, Acknowledge it, then "Question: ask ONE question". The rules are "Short messages. One or two sentences" and "One question per message. Never stack two questions" ([SFL setter manual](https://sfl-operations.pages.dev/setter-sop)). Hormozi's version is ACA: "acknowledge what they said... compliment them on whatever they just told you... ask another question. Lead the conversation to whatever direction you want" ([Part 3](https://podscripts.co/podcasts/the-game-with-alex-hormozi/part-3-warm-outreach-100m-leads-book)). A UK setter trainer shows the classic mistake: "asking two questions in one message. Not putting the questions as the last thing that the person's going to read" ([Zidan Akel, Aug 2025](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rCQ1WIVRwlU), course pitch).

The manual lists ten things to learn before offering a call: the challenge, what they tried, how long, the goal as a number, why it matters now, their job, location, an income signal, urgency and willingness to invest. For owners these become the constraint, what they tried, how long, a profit or hours goal, why now, whether they own the business, its size, whether it's in the UK, urgency and willingness to pay for help (my mapping). Giulia Guerrieri, who sells to coaches and experts, asks the business versions: "What do you currently sell? Or what's your offer?", "How do you typically sell, or what does your sales process look like?", and "where would you like to be in the next 6 months if you could wave a magic wand. Tell me revenue level, number of clients". Her key question before the invite is "What is holding you back from getting there on your own?" ([Giulia Guerrieri](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7XXib-_biU)). The manual adds one rule that protects the call: "You can tell them WHAT they need... but never explain HOW to do it. When they ask 'how do I do that?' that is your perfect moment to pitch the call."

The best business-owner example is a real thread Dave Panchcham showed on screen with a gym franchise owner. He opened "Hey David, how's business going at your ILKB studios?" After a reply he asked, "I noticed that all of your locations are in the range of two to five years old. Is that about right?" Then: "So I'm guessing each location has at least 2,000 contacts in the non-member database." When the owner said "probably", he made a short offer and asked, "When's best for a 15-minute chat? Happy to show you how it works. Either later today or tomorrow morning." His summary: "number one, get them to respond... Once they've responded, then I went into a little bit of a qualifier... If they have enough contacts, then go make your offer and then ask for a chat." He now makes the promise smaller to make it "a little bit more believable", and he writes the way friends text: "you write three words and then you hit enter" ([Dave Panchcham](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kJ_RPeodSig)). From the scripts, a warm chat takes about 6 to 12 short messages before the invite. A keyword lead needs about 3 to 5 (my count; no source measured it).

### Voice notes and video come after the first reply

On Instagram, voice and video can't be the first touch. Since 3 August 2023, a stranger can send one message request, text only, with no voice, video or images, until it is accepted ([TechCrunch, Aug 2023](https://techcrunch.com/2023/08/03/instagrams-new-feature-protects-users-from-unwanted-images-and-videos-in-dms)). After a reply, practitioners switch to voice or a short selfie video to stand out. Charlie Morgan sends his permission ask "via voice note or via video so no text messages because you need to break the pattern". His pitch is a selfie video under two minutes, made in the app: "We don't send them a Loom, we don't send them a long message, we don't send them a PDF" ([Charlie Morgan](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3OR6sMC1dEY)). On Messenger, Adam Erhart replies "I actually made a short video that covers exactly that. Want me to send it over?" Then he uploads a 60 to 90 second phone video straight into the chat, because with a link "maybe only 20% of people actually click it". He claims 30 to 40% of people who watch reply asking how to start, though that figure is unverified ([Adam Erhart](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2OHrMEvTPOc)). One freelancer found voice raised replies because "people had to actually open the message and hear it" ([aiDr](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3-jKacJ1ygY)). Hormozi gives the only benchmark: personal video or voice to 100 people, "I say their name and add one personal line before delivering my standard message. From there, 20% of people reply" ([Part 5](https://podscripts.co/podcasts/the-game-with-alex-hormozi/part-5-cold-outreach-100m-leads-book)). Timing splits two ways. The setter manual sends a pre-recorded voice note early to followers. A template site says "Voice is for people who already replied twice" ([Coachway](https://coachway.io/downloads/instagram-dm-scripts.txt)). A tool maker puts the best length at 15 to 30 seconds, about 75 words ([BooSend](https://boosend.ai/blog/voice-notes-inside-instagram-automations-where-they-earn-their-keep), advice). For BCA coaches, the evidence points to a 20 to 40 second voice note after the first real reply, then a two-minute video read once the owner has done the BOSS Score.

### Follow-ups: few to strangers, more once they reply

Under Instagram's rule, a stranger who never replied can't receive a second message at all. So every cadence below applies only to people who replied, accepted or follow you. For silent strangers, the only follow-up is another light touch (a like, a story reply) or another channel. Hormozi calls this "more times, more ways": "If someone doesn't respond to one of your outreach methods, use that as a reason to follow up with another method. Hey, I'm calling you to follow up about my email" ([Part 5](https://podscripts.co/podcasts/the-game-with-alex-hormozi/part-5-cold-outreach-100m-leads-book)). On Facebook, repeated nudges to people who never replied now risk a block: "we used to send a ton of follow-up messages to get people to respond to us. That is a thing of the past" ([Ron Medlin](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yuvEFxLBCBo)).

Charlie Morgan's rule is "once someone's given you permission to pitch they've also given you permission to follow up relentlessly". His memes ("Pablo Escobar... captioned 'following up waiting for your reply'") would put off a £1M owner. The same goes for the setter manual's "you never really struck me as the type to ghost lol". For owners, a middle path fits the evidence: a nudge at 2 days, a plain "last message" at day 7, then a check-in every month or two that names their own goal (my inference). An X (Twitter) dataset suggests patience pays. In it, 42% of replies came within 24 hours, but 22.6% came after 7 to 30 days. The summary is second-hand and from X, not Instagram ([Matthew Metros](https://matthewmetros.substack.com/p/what-437-million-dms-reveal-about)).

## The call invite offers more help and names two times

The practitioners who book calls frame the call as more help, never as a pitch for their programme. Mark Coles: "I have a suggestion for you rather than you and I going backwards and forwards here why don't we jump on a call I will take a deeper look at exactly what you're doing right now and I will make some suggestions for you." He warns against "why don't we jump on a call and I can tell you what I do and explain more about my program" ([Mark Coles](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=inVbcg5ahRs)). The setter manual's line is "honestly that's exactly the kind of thing easier to walk through on a quick call than type out here." Aaron Spikes: "Well, I'm sure I could help you reach that goal. Are you open to jump on a call in the next week or so?" ([Aaron Spikes](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4iIZAexOR8s)). Ste McGrath closes the invite with "If not, no problems. At least you'll leave with a plan" ([Ste McGrath, Mar 2025](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Urrq1K9--yo), advice and course pitch).

Good setters book the time themselves rather than sending a link and hoping. "Offer two specific times. Get their best phone number... Do not just send them a link and hope they book themselves." For a hot lead: "Are you free now? I can give you a quick call in about 5 minutes." For a warm lead, book the next day, because "The next-day gap acts as a commitment filter" ([SFL setter manual](https://sfl-operations.pages.dev/setter-sop)). Dave Panchcham offered "Either later today or tomorrow morning" and then got the phone number. For someone unsure: "No need to book anything now. If you want, I will send you two times for a 20-minute call and you pick one, or neither. Want that?" ([Coachway](https://coachway.io/downloads/instagram-dm-scripts.txt)). If a link is used, ask them to say when they've booked: "let me know once you've booked in so I can send you some extra resources" ([Henry Fortunatow](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZoOBHfY7kEE)). Then check on the day: "Everything's still okay for our call in 30 minutes?" ([Adam Consult](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BZvcNrfWLiY)).

On price, practitioners disagree. The setter manual dodges it until the call. A template site states the price range before inviting. The biggest gym-owner firm prints its price ($1,200 a month) so owners "should not have to sit through a sales call to learn it" ([Two-Brain, book a call](https://twobrainbusiness.com/book-a-c), own numbers, per search summary). Hormozi's warning matters most for BCA. His first cold campaign offered "a game planning session as our lead magnet", which he calls "code for sales call", and most people said no. "We swapped from game planning... to actually giving them as much free services we possibly could. Our take rates 3xed" ([Part 5](https://podscripts.co/podcasts/the-game-with-alex-hormozi/part-5-cold-outreach-100m-leads-book)). So the free call needs real free work in front of it. The BOSS Score done beforehand, plus the coach's own read of it, lets BCA's first call (the Constraint Call: find the one thing holding the business back, and the first fixes) start from the owner's own numbers. Hormozi adds: "Expect two or three conversations before a high ticket sale." People who say "not now" go into a slow nurture, for example Giulia Guerrieri's "Keep watching my free stuff and I'm going to send you a message in a couple months and check back in" ([Giulia Guerrieri](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7XXib-_biU)).

## One focused hour a day starts 20 to 40 conversations

Hand-run routines range from minutes to hours. The one closest to BCA's sector is the shortest. Dave Panchcham built his gym agency while working "a full-time job, 50, 60 hours a week". He sent "40, 50 people a day" in friend requests over "20, 30 minutes", in batches of ten, chatted "in the nooks and crannies of the day", and posted one researched video a week ([Dave Panchcham](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kJ_RPeodSig)). At the other end, the setter manual works three blocks a day across the prospects' waking hours, "25 to 35 touches per block", hottest leads first, with a written end-of-day report ([SFL setter manual](https://sfl-operations.pages.dev/setter-sop)). Hormozi asks for the most: "A hundred attempts per day", in the "First four hours of your day", for at least 100 days ([Part 3](https://podscripts.co/podcasts/the-game-with-alex-hormozi/part-3-warm-outreach-100m-leads-book)).

Two rules keep the hour honest. Count the right thing: "How many DMS you send is Vanity how many proposals and book calls you make is sanity" ([Mark Coles](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=inVbcg5ahRs)). And keep the account safe. Meta publishes no limits, and its spam policy covers high-frequency actions "manually or automatically", adding "We may place restrictions on accounts that are acting at lower frequencies" ([Meta Transparency Centre](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/community-standards/spam/)). On Facebook, Ron Medlin starts a new account at about 25 friend requests a day and adds 5 a week. He keeps group invites under about 35 at a time, after "I did I think like 40 one day and my account got shut down". The setter manual's end-of-day report counts conversations started, calls offered, calls booked and closes, which is a simple tracker for a coach too. For a BCA coach on their own personal accounts, the sources support **about an hour a day: replies first, 10 to 20 owners warmed up, 10 to 20 openers, and 10 to 20 warm-network messages**, which is 20 to 40 new conversations (my inference). Start low on Facebook friend requests and build up.

## Hormozi's playbook: list everyone, check in, ask who they know

### Warm outreach, in his words

Hormozi calls warm outreach the start for anyone: "Warm reachouts are when you make one-to-one contact with your warm audience, aka the people who know you. It's the cheapest and easiest way to find people interested in the stuff [you sell]... And most businesses don't do it" ([Part 3](https://podscripts.co/podcasts/the-game-with-alex-hormozi/part-3-warm-outreach-100m-leads-book)). His own first campaign used Facebook: "I called, I texted, and I sent Facebook messages to a bunch of people I knew. Hey, do you know anyone who's trying to get in shape. I'm training people for free for 12 weeks." His ten steps, verbatim: "Step one, get your list. Step two, pick a platform. Step three, personalize your message. Step four, reach out. Step five, warm them up. Step six, invite their friends. Step seven, make them the easiest offer in the world. Step eight, start at the top. Step nine, start charging. Step 10, keep your list warm." The list is every contact on every platform, and "For many of you, this will be your first thousand leads." Start with the platform "you have the most contacts on". The opener asks for nothing: "Use something you know about the contact as your actual reason to reach out... Don't be a weirdo. Pay your social dues. Remember, you haven't asked for anything." Then "Reach out to 100 of them per day... up to three times, once per day for three days or until they respond, whichever comes first."

After "three to four exchanges if on the phone or messaging", he makes the offer as a referral ask. This is his script in full: "By the way, do you know anybody who is [describe their struggle] looking to [dream outcome] in [time delay]? I'm taking on five case studies for free because that's all I can handle. I just want to get some testimonials for my product or service. I help them [dream outcome] without [effort and sacrifice]. It works. I even guarantee people [dream outcome] or I work with them until they do. I just had a girl named [X] work with me, [dream outcome], even though she [described the same struggle that contact has]... Does anyone you like come to mind?" If they say no: "ha ha, well, does anyone you hate come to mind?" Why it works: "We're not asking them to buy anything. We're asking if they know anyone... In all three outcomes, you win." Once referrals start, "start charging", moving from free to "80% off for the next five, then 60% off... keep raising it by 20% every five". To keep the list warm, he borrows "Dean Jackson's timeless nine-word email template. Are you still looking to [four-word desire]?" And: "don't do anything sketchy." After a sale he asks "who else do you know who might also want this", not a yes-or-no "do you know anybody", because "now you're actually asking them to solve a problem with their brain" ([Hormozi, Jun 2024](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qpQvdBFW_yI)).

### Cold outreach, in his words

Cold outreach "sits atop the foundation of warm outreach", and his order is "Start with warm reachouts, get some reps, post some content to grow your warm audience, get even more reps. Then you'll be ready for cold reachouts" ([Part 5](https://podscripts.co/podcasts/the-game-with-alex-hormozi/part-5-cold-outreach-100m-leads-book)). He builds lists three ways, and the hand-built one is freshest: "if you assemble a list of names yourself, it's less likely that person has already seen many cold reachouts." His favourite source: "Interest groups are the warmest cold audiences you can get... They're one of my favorite places to fish." He prefers contacting group members outside the group "so I don't come off as solely trying to milk the group for business". To make cold feel warm, use "one to three pieces of information we can find that a friend might know about the prospect". His workshop formula is "Specific observation plus relevant compliment plus transition to business" ([Workshop Session 4](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1CnZZ-WwnhxP3xVJHrAvOadsbayrXfZBK/view)). Then give "big fast value": "make your life easier by giving away the farm. We're not trying to tickle their interest. We're trying to blow their minds in under 30 seconds." Also: "Give away something for free people would normally pay for and they will want it." His one full cold script is a call to a dog trainer, and it shows the shape: their name, a real compliment on their content, who he works with, a nearby proof case ("We work with someone about a half hour north of you"), "If you got a boatload of high-paying new dog training customers, you wouldn't be upset with me, would you?", then "Will you be around at four?" The rules around it: "If someone asks you not to contact them, don't contact them again", "Expect two or three conversations before a high ticket sale", "wait three to six months, then do it again... Everything may be right except the timing", and "Don't set a daily goal below 100, and don't stop for 100 days minimum."

### What he says about Instagram, Facebook and business owners

He names the platforms only a few times. Instagram is the "front door" for DMs: "the CTA in there was like, hey, if you want the free stuff from the launch, comment... book. And then because of that, there's ManyChat automation that just fires and then sends them the thing... the conversions will happen in the conversations" ([Workshop Session 1](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1RbOspfzghF25i3YLGsWgHce8lpoFvnwr/view)). To a B2B firm with 20,000 Facebook followers, he endorsed "you don't have a lead problem, you have an action problem". He suggested a "drop" of "10 priority... assignments, first come, first serve... Comment EA", then "a DM sprint... do a banger offer, DM everybody" (same source). He uses only official tools: "you never want to risk your profile by doing anything sketchy" ([Session 4](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1CnZZ-WwnhxP3xVJHrAvOadsbayrXfZBK/view)). Content keeps outreach alive: "one to three posts a week on the channels that you get the most leads from. All we want someone to say is like, okay, they're legit." Over time, "a third of our sales are coming from these tiny posts." A profile with no followers fails: "this honestly looks to me like a bot account... invite people who actually know you like in the real world to just follow you" ([Hormozi live Q&A, Apr 2025](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6cWahKKGkR4)).

For selling to owners he adds four moves. Research for two minutes: "people will think that you are so intelligent and worked so hard if you take like two minutes to find out something about their business... I noticed you just expanded... I saw you're hiring for." Use outreach to find partners who already have your buyers, for example "cold outreach to accounting practices who have many high net worth individuals". Qualify on budget, authority, need and timing, since "if you know their revenue, you don't necessarily have to ask a specific budget question" ([Session 4](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1CnZZ-WwnhxP3xVJHrAvOadsbayrXfZBK/view)). And cold outreach suits big tickets: "I like 10 grand or more as a rule of thumb... you want to be hunting whales, not hunting minnows with a harpoon" ([MoreMozi, Jan 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KIs-egpg1bg)). His favourite free offer is one that reveals a problem, like his Scaling Roadmap assessment, which tells owners "what stage of scaling you're currently at". He warns against giving free services "to somebody who's broke, who can't make a decision" ([Hormozi, Nov 2025](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Mst4hreQYl0)). The BOSS Score has the same shape. By his own test, though, a free quiz alone is not "something people normally pay for". The coach's personal read of the score is the part owners would pay for.

## Gym and food sellers win inside the owners' own rooms

### Gym and sports-venue owners buy from people who have run a venue

The firms that built big businesses selling to gym owners did not do it with cold Instagram DMs. Two-Brain Business has published "free blog posts every day for a decade". It runs Gym Owners United, a free Facebook group of 7,000 gym owners in 2023, with a free call before paid mentoring ([Chris Cooper, May 2023](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/23730894-two-brain-mentorship-from-free-help-to-earning-more-than-100-000-per-y), own numbers). The group page now says "10,000+ members" and "We vet every profile so only real gym owners join" ([Two-Brain](https://twobrainbusiness.com/gym-owners-united/)). Its core message suits a business coach: "about 80 percent of the owners we talk to already have enough leads. They lose them with a weak sales process" ([Two-Brain](https://twobrainbusiness.com/book-a-c), own numbers, per search summary). Hormozi's Gym Launch began with proof in person: "I would fly out, set up a table, and then I would like sell 200 memberships in, you know, 20 days" ([Hormozi, Feb 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JlY8cvqlXoI), own numbers). Its first big sales came from owners already in the pipeline. Then there is Dave Panchcham: insider words ("your ILKB studios"), a peer-looking profile, small near-term promises and Facebook friend requests to franchise owners one by one ([Dave Panchcham](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kJ_RPeodSig)). The one documented cold gym campaign shows what to avoid. Its message read "We have launched a new tool for gyms... where we send you 10+ new members in 7 days... No catch, we just want to prove ourselves". It got one reply from about 150, "I chased him for about a week lol", and then closed ([Skool](https://www.skool.com/gohighlevel/dr-client-using-robbs-closing-script), own numbers).

Padel and multi-sport venues have a small, public list and a money hook suppliers already use. A UK court builder sells on empty off-peak hours. Adding ten points of use off-peak is "about 370 more hours a year per court. At £27 an hour, about £8,300 before VAT. For a four court club, £33,000 a year." It ends "Planning a paddle club? Tell us about your site" ([Padel Blue, 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7LC0H-MEkvM), advice). Britain had **2,445 padel courts at 682 venues** in October 2026 ([LTA Padel](https://ltapadel.org.uk/news/2026/october/lta-marks-world-padel-day-with-record-1.5-million-people-playing-padel-in-britain)). One data firm counted 872 padel planning applications in January to July 2026, which name the applicant and give a dated "about to open" signal ([Barbour ABI](https://barbour-abi.com/padel-court-construction-why-its-booming-what-it-costs-whats-next/), per search summary). One agency owner starting a padel agency planned to visit the biggest clubs and offer "I want to come and shoot for you for a couple of hours for free" to build proof first, though he reported no clients ([Joel Blackman](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Gu93AJFvkoA), course pitch). A UK gym-software firm lists the Facebook groups UK owners use, including UK PTs, Gym Owners Business Network and CIMSPA ([TeamUp, Mar 2024](https://goteamup.com/resources/fitness-owner-facebook-groups), advice). A UK gym-owner coach, Justin Devonshire, runs a podcast, a gym-owner Facebook group and a free strategy call, and invites Instagram DMs ([Fitness Business Blueprint](https://podcasts.apple.com/gb/podcast/fitness-business-blueprint/id1450722381?mt=2), per search summary). No campaign aimed at climbing walls was found.

At this size the brand's Instagram is often run by a marketer, and DMs to brands often land in "the 'requests' section" ([Sked Social](https://skedsocial.com/blog/how-to-dm-brands-instagram-collaboration), advice). Agencies that sell to brands open on a gap the founder can check, or a plan for a coming peak. For a business coach, the matching moments are a new listing (cash gets squeezed), a raise (hiring and reporting), or a seasonal peak (my inference). Each one gives a real, dated reason to write.

## UK rules keep the campaigns small, named and honest

Four rules limit which of these campaigns BCA can teach. First, the ICO counts a private social media message as "electronic mail" under PECR, the UK law on marketing by email, text, phone and private message ([ICO, key concepts](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/guidance-on-direct-marketing-using-electronic-mail/key-concepts-for-direct-marketing-using-electronic-mail/)). Limited companies and LLPs may get cold marketing if you say who you are and give an easy way to opt out. Sole traders and most partnerships must agree first, and if you're unsure, treat them as individuals ([ICO, business-to-business marketing](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/business-to-business-marketing/)). So check Companies House before a cold message, and add a plain way out such as "If this isn't for you, just say and I won't message again." Stop the moment anyone says no. Second, WhatsApp allows business messages only once someone has given you their number and their permission ([WhatsApp Business Messaging Policy](https://whatsappbusiness.com/en-gb/policy/)), so WhatsApp comes after a yes. Third, on Instagram a stranger can send one text-only message request until it is accepted ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2023/08/03/instagrams-new-feature-protects-users-from-unwanted-images-and-videos-in-dms)). Fourth, Facebook allows one account in your own name and bans collecting data "using automated means" without permission ([Facebook Terms](https://www.facebook.com/terms.php)). Two grey areas remain. Nobody knows whether a DM to an owner's personal profile counts as messaging the company or the person, and the ICO's B2B guidance is under review. A UK-qualified adviser should check the scripts before BCA trains coaches on them.

## Three campaigns to run by hand for 30 days

All three campaigns share one spine taken from the evidence. Warm contacts and referral asks come first (Hormozi). People who engage come second (the setter manual's order). A short list of named owners, warmed up by hand, comes third (Panchcham, Charlie Barber). The offer is the free BOSS Score (10 quick questions) plus the coach's own two-minute read of it, which is the part owners would pay for (Hormozi's "big fast value"). The call is 20 minutes to find the one thing holding the business back (BCA's Constraint Call). The campaigns differ in where the list comes from, the hook and the coach's own proof. **Every script below is my draft in plain words, built from the practitioners' rules, and none has been tested with owners.** Square brackets are for the coach to fill from real events and real numbers only. Each runs on the coach's own personal Instagram and Facebook, by hand, in about an hour a day after a short setup week.

### Any BCA coach: the seen, ask, help campaign

This version works for any coach and any owner niche. It puts Hormozi's warm outreach first, because a coach's own network gives the fastest first clients and referrals.

**Days 1 to 7: profile and warm list.** On day 1 the coach fixes both personal profiles: a face photo, a bio that names who they help and one result, one link to the BOSS Score, and three posts about owner problems. On day 2 they list every contact on every platform and start with the biggest list. Facebook friends are often the biggest for coaches with long careers (my inference). On days 3 to 7 they send personal check-ins, using Hormozi's three tries, once a day. That means 100 a day with a three- to four-hour morning, or 30 with one hour. After three or four messages they make the referral ask.

**Days 8 to 30: add the people who engage and a short named list.** Each day the coach answers replies first. Then they message everyone who commented, liked several posts, followed and engaged, or accepted a friend request. Then they warm up 10 named owners and send openers to yesterday's 10, and give one full answer in an owner group. Facebook friend requests start at 5 a day and build to 15 to 25. The coach posts three times a week: a useful tip on Monday, a two-step offer on Wednesday and a client story on Friday. At a coach's volume, reply to each SCORE comment by hand. If a post gets more comments than they can answer, a tool such as ManyChat can send the link, with a line saying it's automatic.

**From month two: an owners' group, only if posts are drawing owner comments.** Use a narrow name and three joining questions: the owner's biggest growth problem, an email for their BOSS Score, and "Would you like a free review of your score?" This copies the joining question "We help [blank] and [blank] do [blank]. ### How to judge the first 30 days

Count seven numbers every Friday: conversations started, replies, offers made, scores done, calls booked, calls held and clients. Then compare them with the evidence (my inference from it). Warm-network messages should get about 20 replies per 100 (Hormozi). Named owners warmed up by hand should get about 5 to 10 replies and 1 to 3 calls per 100 (Charlie Barber, Alicia Joseph, Laura Cohen). For Paul or Bruce with 150 named owners plus their warm networks, **3 to 6 calls in the first month is a fair target**. Each number points to its own fix. Fewer than 8 replies from 150 owners means change the opener or the list. Replies but no scores means change the offer. Scores but no calls means change the invite. Hormozi's warning applies: "Get your first hundred conversations... out of the way before tweaking it."

## Conclusion

The research changes where BCA should look for the weak link. The method itself is well proven. It transfers from consumer coaching almost word for word: get seen, ask one question, give something worth paying for, invite to a call at two set times. What doesn't transfer is the volume. An owner niche throws off a handful of signals a week where consumer coaches see dozens a day, so each conversation carries far more weight. Two things will decide whether a BCA coach wins: whether the owner already half-knows them when the message lands, and whether the free thing is the coach's own read of the owner's numbers or just a quiz link. Both favour BCA's coaches over the agencies flooding owners' inboxes. Paul has built and filled a sports facility, and Bruce has run a food brand through a crisis. That is the "peer in the room" story that won the gym market for Two-Brain and Gym Launch, and that no "30 leads in 30 days" agency can claim.

The second change is about evidence. Nobody has published how a coach wins UK owner-led businesses on Instagram and Facebook, so BCA's first pilots, tracked with the seven weekly numbers, would be the best data anywhere. That suggests teaching one campaign shape to every coach, running it by hand for 30 days with Paul and Bruce first, and only then deciding which parts deserve tools.
