# Find owners where they already talk

The best way for BCA coaches to win clients outside LinkedIn is to build the list somewhere else first, then reach each owner slowly and personally across several channels. For Paul Hamnett's sports and fitness venues, that means a Google Maps list, a light Instagram warm-up and one short message, then a phone call, letter or email. For Bruce Alexander's food and drink brands, it means a Companies House list, his existing LinkedIn campaign, and post and email to limited companies, with Instagram as a warm touch. Instagram is the strongest second channel after LinkedIn, but it opens doors rather than producing volume: a stranger can send an owner only one text message until the owner accepts, and the safe limit is about 100 actions a day per account. Facebook works better for being seen in owners' groups than for cold messages, because Unipile (the service the app uses to send from coaches' own accounts) cannot search Facebook, a business Page cannot start a chat, and safe sending starts at 15 messages a day. WhatsApp is the best follow-up channel in almost every niche, but only after the owner gives their number and says yes. Plan for about 5 positive replies and half a booked call per 100 cold Instagram messages; warm messages, and messages carrying a personal voice note or video, reach about 20 replies per 100. UK law treats a social media DM like an email: it is fine to send one to a limited company if you say who you are and offer an opt-out, but sole traders and most partnerships need to agree first. The Profit Coach app already has most of the parts, including Instagram, Messenger, WhatsApp and email campaign steps and a Google Maps import. What it lacks are the joins between them, a Companies House import, a safe cold email setup and a Facebook connection on the Unipile version that still supports it.

*How to read this report. Each important claim carries a label. **Evidence** means a regulator, a national survey, official platform documents, Unipile's own developer docs, or BCA's own data and code. **Vendor claim** means a company selling a tool or course said it, usually with no method shown. **Inference** means our own reasoning from the facts. Unipile's docs show what Unipile says it supports; nobody has tested these features inside the app yet. Nothing here is legal advice.*

## Only LinkedIn has a search box, so lists start somewhere else

Sales Navigator lets a coach filter by job title, company size and place. No other channel does. Unipile offers people and company search on LinkedIn only; its Instagram and Facebook features have no search at all (evidence, [Unipile provider features](https://developer.unipile.com/v2.0/docs/list-provider-features)). So outside LinkedIn, a coach finds a business first, then checks by eye whether it is owner-led and big enough: team photos, number of sites, review count, the website (inference). That is more work per lead, so the list source matters more than the sending tool.

### Instagram: owners show off the business and often answer their own DMs

Owners of small and mid-sized firms often run their own Instagram, so a DM can reach the person who decides (practitioner opinion, [marketing consultant's blog](https://www.lilachbullock.com/how-to-send-a-direct-message-on-instagram-professionally/)). Marketing on Instagram runs two ways. The inbound way: the coach posts, asks people to comment a keyword, and a tool sends each commenter an automatic DM with a link. Meta only lets its official tools message people who messaged first ([Meta Instagram Messaging API](https://developers.facebook.com/documentation/instagram-platform/instagram-api-with-instagram-login/messaging-api)), so these tools only work on people who engage. The outbound way: the coach follows the business, likes or comments on a post, replies to a story, then sends one short message.

Finding people on Instagram works in "buckets". A DM course run by a tool vendor lists five: followers of niche or competitor accounts with 5,000 to 50,000 followers, recent posts under niche hashtags (sort by Recent to skip dead accounts), posts tagged at a place, followers and commenters of people the audience follows, and accounts that feature businesses in a niche (vendor claim, [Autogram](https://autogram.gitbook.io/autogram-dm-masterclass/guides/4.-where-to-find-leads)). Scraping tools such as Apify (paid tools that copy public profile details into a spreadsheet) then export name, bio, website and sometimes a business email or phone (vendor claim, [Apify](https://apify.com/scrapio/instagram-b2b-lead-scraper)). Unipile can do part of this from a coach's own account. It can list the followers of any public account, list who commented on or liked a post, and read a profile's public business email, phone and city (evidence, [Unipile Instagram users](https://developer.unipile.com/v2.0/docs/instagram-users); [Unipile profile reference](https://developer.unipile.com/reference/userscontroller_getprofilebyidentifier)). It cannot search by hashtag, keyword or place. It also pages followers 25 at a time under a guide of 100 actions a day, so mining one 5,000-follower account would take about 200 calls on the coach's account (inference). A scraper is the better list tool. Unipile is the better sending tool.

### Facebook: owners talk in groups, and groups are hard to mine

On Facebook, owners gather in groups. The usual way in is to join groups of a sensible size (one older guide says 500 to 10,000 members) and watch for owners asking for help ([Fast Company, about 2016](https://www.fastcompany.com/3065985/how-i-use-facebook-groups-to-grow-my-business)). One 2026 post says to reply within 48 hours of a public "can anyone recommend" post, and claims this closes at three to five times the rate of cold outreach (unverified, [BotsGoneWild](https://botsgonewildd.substack.com/p/stop-cold-dming-start-reading-signals)). Unipile can't help much here. It can't search Facebook, can't list group members, and lists only the coach's own friends (evidence, [Unipile Facebook users](https://developer.unipile.com/v2.0/docs/facebook-users)). A separate tool, PhantomBuster, exports group members, but Facebook shows only about 4,000 to 5,000 per group (vendor claim, [PhantomBuster](https://support.phantombuster.com/hc/en-us/articles/26971138918802-How-to-Use-the-Facebook-Group-Members-Export)). Most owner-run groups ban selling, so the coaches who win there either run the group or are a helpful regular in it (inference from niche coaching case studies).

### Google Maps and Companies House are the two best list sources

Google Maps is the best list source for physical venues, and the app already imports it through Apify. Each record carries business name, category, address, phone, website, star rating, review count and opening hours, and paid add-ons pull emails and social links from the business's own website (vendor docs, [Apify Google Maps Scraper](https://apify.com/compass/crawler-google-places)). It costs from **$1.50 per 1,000 places**, but one search stops at about 120 places, so a national list needs many town-by-town searches (same source). Many listings link to the venue's Instagram and Facebook pages. The app already collects those links but hides them (evidence, app code review).

Companies House is the best free list source for limited companies above £1M. Its free API (an API is a plug that lets one piece of software ask another for data) filters by industry code (the SIC code each company picks when it registers), status, company type, start date and place, and returns up to **5,000 companies per request** (evidence, [Companies House API](https://developer-specs.company-information.service.gov.uk/companies-house-public-data-api/reference/search/advanced-company-search)). It has no turnover or staff filter, and turnover stays mostly hidden: in November 2026 the government paused the plan to make small firms file a profit and loss account (evidence, [ICAEW, Nov 2026](https://www.icaew.com/insights/viewpoints-on-the-news/2026/nov-2026/uk-government-pauses-profit-and-loss-filing-but-sticks-with-software-only-filing)). There is still a good shortcut. Since April 2025, a company can file the smallest "micro-entity" accounts only if it meets two of three tests: turnover up to £1M, balance sheet up to £500k, and 10 or fewer staff (evidence, [ICAEW](https://www.icaew.com/insights/viewpoints-on-the-news/2024/dec-2024/uk-company-size-thresholds-to-increase-from-april-2025)). A firm with more than £1M turnover and more than 10 staff fails two tests, so it can't file as micro. **Dropping micro-entity and dormant filers removes firms below Paul's and Bruce's targets without losing real prospects** (inference). Companies House also gives the directors' names for free, and it shows whether a business is a limited company at all, which decides what you may legally send (see the law section).

### Email, WhatsApp, phone, post and "rooms" fill the gaps

Owner emails come from enrichment tools, which find and check an address from a name and a website. One claims about $0.01 per verified email at 98% accuracy (vendor claim, [Prospeo](https://prospeo.io/s/clay-enrichment)), while BCA's own cold email research found bought lists are only 70 to 80% valid (evidence, BCA research, job 84). WhatsApp has no search at all; Unipile can only check whether a known number uses WhatsApp (evidence, [Unipile WhatsApp](https://developer.unipile.com/v2.0/docs/whatsapp-send-messages)). The last source is the "room" where owners talk to each other: a Facebook group, podcast, trade magazine, trade body or supplier. A North American gym mentoring firm runs a vetted Facebook group that claims 10,000+ gym owners ([Two-Brain Business](https://twobrainbusiness.com/gym-owners-united/)). A UK and Ireland gym billing company runs a free vetted owners' group and a podcast ([Ashbourne Memberships](https://ashbourne-memberships.com/en-IE/gym-owners-forum/)). The Federation of Master Builders sells its own coaching course to members ([FMB](https://www.fmb.org.uk/events-calendar/elevate-your-building-business-a-12-week-coaching-programme.html)). These rooms make good partners because they already hold owners' attention (inference).

## Unipile can message almost anywhere, but only slowly

Unipile is the plug the app uses to act as the coach inside their own accounts. It does not use Meta's official tools. It logs in to the coach's own Instagram with a password, a browser cookie or a browser add-on called UniLogin; to Facebook the same way; and to WhatsApp through WhatsApp's "Link a device" QR code (evidence, [Unipile Instagram](https://developer.unipile.com/v2.0/docs/link-instagram-accounts); [Unipile Facebook](https://developer.unipile.com/v2.0/docs/facebook-link-accounts); [Unipile WhatsApp](https://developer.unipile.com/v2.0/docs/link-whatsapp-accounts)). Unipile says it is "not a Meta Partner" and tells customers to avoid spam, mass automation and scraping (vendor, [Unipile](https://www.unipile.com/instagram-api-endpoints-available-with-unipile/)). That is why it can send a first message to a stranger, which the official tools can't, and it is also why it carries account risk and can break when Meta changes its login. Its November 2026 change log lists fixes for Facebook message history that broke after Facebook updated its encryption (evidence, [Unipile change log, 13 Nov 2026](https://developer.unipile.com/changelog/v2202611131-better-onboarding-disappearing-messages-controls-additional-auth-checkpoints)).

Unipile now runs two versions of its plug. Version 1 (v1) is the older one; version 2 (v2) is the new one, and it gets all new features. **Facebook Messenger works only on v2**: Unipile's v1 guide says "Facebook Messenger is deprecated in v1 and available in v2" (evidence, [Unipile v1 Messenger guide](https://developer.unipile.com/docs/messenger)). The app calls only v1 (evidence, app code review). So the app's Messenger connection and Messenger campaign step probably don't work today, or will stop (inference; needs a test). Instagram, WhatsApp, email and LinkedIn all work on v1.

Three rules in that table shape every campaign. First, Instagram's one-message rule means a cold Instagram "sequence" is really a warm-up plus one text, then other channels; voice notes and video can only follow once the owner replies or accepts. Unipile doesn't document what happens if the app tries a second message too early, so that needs a test (inference). Second, a coach's business Page can never start a Facebook conversation, so any Facebook outreach comes from the coach's personal profile at low volume. Third, the daily limits are tight. One prospect on Instagram costs two or three actions (follow, like, message), so one account can start roughly **30 to 60 new conversations a day at most** (inference), against LinkedIn's 80 to 100 invites a day. Unipile also claims coaches can ignore Instagram's "we suspect automated behaviour" warning (vendor claim, [Unipile limits](https://developer.unipile.com/v2.0/docs/provider-limits-and-restrictions)). Treat that with care: Meta acted against about 500,000 accounts for spammy behaviour in July 2025 ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/07/14/following-youtube-meta-announces-crackdown-on-unoriginal-facebook-content)), and some genuine users said they were wrongly removed ([Yahoo News](https://www.yahoo.com/news/facebook-erases-10m-fake-accounts-155047003.html)).

Money is not the limit. Unipile charges the same for every channel: **€49 a month covers the first 10 accounts**, then €5 per account, falling to €4.50 at 51 to 200 accounts, with no usage fees (vendor, [Unipile pricing](https://www.unipile.com/pricing/)). A coach with LinkedIn, Instagram and email connected costs about £11 to £13 a month (inference). The real costs are account risk and the work of moving to v2.

## Site count and owner age pick the channel better than sector does

UK small firms use Meta's apps more than LinkedIn. One lender's survey found **Facebook used by 69% of SMEs, Instagram 67%, WhatsApp 58% and LinkedIn 52%**, though it published no sample size (vendor claim, [iwoca](https://www.iwoca.co.uk/news/meta-channels-lead-97-smes-use-social-media)). The national figures point the same way. Facebook and Messenger reach 93% of UK online adults and WhatsApp 90% (evidence, [Ofcom, 2025](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/media-use-and-attitudes/online-habits/from-apps-to-ai-search-how-the-uk-goes-online-in-2025)). Only 25% of Britons are on LinkedIn at all; half of them work in business-to-business firms, and 40% never post (evidence, [YouGov](https://yougov.com/en-gb/articles/47039-who-are-the-brits-who-use-linkedin)). BCA's own data from 110,769 LinkedIn requests by 43 coaches scores consulting 9 out of 10 and IT 8, but construction, engineering, trades, hospitality and food and drink only 4, and health, care and wellbeing (where gyms fall) only 3 (evidence, BCA data). By those scores, Paul's market sits at 3 or 4 and Bruce's at 4. Bruce's score comes from a rule built for hospitality, so the real LinkedIn response for packaged food brands is unknown (inference).

Two things predict the right channel better than the sector label. The first is owner age. Among UK tradespeople, Facebook use stays high in the over-55s (75%) while Instagram drops to 40% (evidence, insurer-commissioned survey of 501, [Markel](https://www.markeluk.com/knowledge-centre/trades-and-social-media-uk)). Many £1M+ owners are over 45, so Facebook is often the safer social channel for them than Instagram (inference). The second is site count. A single-site owner who works on the floor lives on Instagram, Facebook and WhatsApp even at £1M. Once a business has several sites or a management team, often around £3M to £5M, its leaders tend to appear on LinkedIn (inference; no UK data measures this). The most useful rule is to **find the room where owners talk to other owners**: a Facebook group for trades, gyms, salons and nurseries; trade press and podcasts for dental, salons and estate agents; LinkedIn for professional services; trade bodies and events for care, manufacturing and multi-site hospitality (inference from the sector research).

### Paul's venues fit Google Maps and Instagram

Paul targets owners of commercially run sports, fitness and activity venues at £1M to £5M with 11 to 50 staff: independent gyms, specialist fitness, climbing, padel and racket venues, commercial pitches and multi-sport operators, with golf and leisure clubs later. BCA's targeting notes flag the market as tricky because few such venues reach £1M (evidence, BCA targeting notes, 19 Nov). These are physical places with Google listings, so the built Maps import fits well, and many listings link to an Instagram account (inference). Gym owners live in their DMs: one gym mentoring firm tells owners to sell memberships by chat on Instagram and Facebook ([Two-Brain, via Goodreads blog](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/21538260-sell-by-chat-for-gyms-does-it-work)). Cold Facebook messages alone look weak in this sector, though: one agency owner messaged about 150 gyms on Facebook and got a single reply, which became a sale only after a week of follow-up (anecdote, [Skool](https://www.skool.com/gohighlevel/dr-client-using-robbs-closing-script)). Because the market is small, Paul should treat every venue as a named account and touch it several ways, rather than chase volume (inference).

### Bruce's brands fit Companies House, LinkedIn, post and email

Bruce targets founders and managing directors of UK packaged food, drinks, snacks, chilled and frozen, and premium brands at £1M to £10M with 11 to 50 staff, and BCA's notes call this list "clean" and easy to build (evidence, BCA targeting notes). Consumer brands show off their products on Instagram, but their founders gather in Slack groups, meetups with WhatsApp groups, and on LinkedIn (low to medium confidence, sector research; e.g. [Meetup, London e-commerce](https://www.meetup.com/lp/career-and-business/ecommerce-meetups-london)). Brands of this size are almost all limited companies, so cold email to them is legal with an opt-out, and Companies House gives their directors by name (inference). A brand's Instagram account is often run by a marketer, so a DM there may not reach the founder (inference). Instagram is best used here as a warm touch: follow, comment on a launch, and message the founder's own account where one is linked.

## Warm up, send one short text, and save the voice note for the reply

Three ideas recur across every source. Warm up before you message. Send one short message that asks one question. Give something useful that people would normally pay for.

The book $100M Leads gives two scripts, as summarised in third-party chapter notes. For people the coach already knows, its warm script is Acknowledge, Compliment, Ask, plus a referral question: "Do you know anybody who is [struggle] looking to [dream outcome] in [time]?" It suggests 100 reach-outs a day and up to three tries per person, and its sums are **100 warm reach-outs, about 20 replies, about 4 who take a free offer, about 1 paying customer** ([Margin Notes, ch. 5](https://margin-notes-600.pages.dev/books/100m-leads/ch-5)). For strangers, it says personalise with one to three real details and offer "big fast value", meaning "something people normally pay for". When the author swapped disguised sales calls for genuinely free work, take-up tripled ([Margin Notes, ch. 9](https://margin-notes-600.pages.dev/books/100m-leads/ch-9)). For a BCA coach, the natural big fast value is a BOSS Score result or a two-minute recorded review of the owner's business (inference).

The shape of the message is simple. One vendor course calls it ICE: icebreaker, context, easy ask, and claims one line of context can double replies (vendor claim, unverified, [Autogram](https://autogram.gitbook.io/autogram-dm-masterclass/guides/8.-dm-script-templates)). Setter guides say two or three lines, one question per message, and never several messages without a reply (vendor claim, [SetSmart](https://setsmart.io/blog/instagram-dm-scripts)). A gym mentoring firm puts it as "three bubbles: their name, what they did and a question that gets a response" ([Two-Brain Business](https://twobrainbusiness.com/exact-dm-scripts/)). Generic praise such as "love what you're doing" reads as fake (opinion, [Gumroad guide](https://herwhybylaurafama.gumroad.com/l/colddm)).

Voice notes help most after the owner replies. Vendors put the sweet spot at **15 to 30 seconds, about 75 words** (vendor claim, [BooSend](https://boosend.ai/blog/voice-notes-inside-instagram-automations-where-they-earn-their-keep)), sent after a text line such as "I recorded a quick voice note for you" (vendor template, [Inrō](https://www.inro.social/templates/voice-note-campaign)). On Instagram a voice note can't be the first touch to a non-follower anyway. The popular claim that voice lifts replies by 30 to 40% comes from LinkedIn and unnamed reports (vendor claim, [Reply.io](https://reply.io/blog/linkedin-voice-messages/)). Treat it as unproven. A coach can test it cheaply by sending text follow-ups to half the people who reply and voice to the other half (inference).

### Sequences that fit the rules

**Instagram, owner doesn't follow the coach.** Day 0: follow the business and like a recent post. Day 1 or 2: reply to a story or leave a real comment. Day 3: send one text, two or three lines, one question. If they reply, send a 20 to 30 second voice note or a short screen video with one specific observation and an offer to run their BOSS Score, then invite them to a call. If there's no reply after three or four days, switch channel: email if it's a limited company, a screened phone call, or a letter that mentions the message. The book's advice is to follow up across channels over five to seven days, with each touch referring back to the last ([Margin Notes, ch. 9](https://margin-notes-600.pages.dev/books/100m-leads/ch-9)). Stop after three tries and come back in three to six months, because "everything may be right except the timing" (same source).

**Facebook.** Be useful in a group thread first. Then message the person who posted, from the coach's personal profile, within a day or two. Send voice or video only after they reply (inference).

**Email to limited companies.** Two or three plain-text emails, no links in the first one, sent from a separate domain, with a clear opt-out line in every email (evidence, BCA cold email research, job 84). One vendor found 58% of replies come from the first email ([Instantly](https://instantly.ai/cold-email-benchmark-report-2026)), so the first email matters most.

**WhatsApp.** Only after the owner says yes on a call or in a reply ("Can I WhatsApp you the summary?"). Then send the summary and a short voice note.

**Inbound on Instagram and Facebook.** A post asking owners to comment a keyword such as "SCORE" gets an automatic DM with the BOSS Score link. Vendors claim high rates (one says 12 to 20% of qualified leads buy from DMs), but none of the figures is independent (vendor claim, [ManyChat](https://manychat.com/blog/how-course-creators-use-instagram-dm-automation/)). It is still the only route Meta allows at scale, because the owner makes the first move (inference).

## UK law treats a DM like an email, so limited companies are the safe target

PECR (the Privacy and Electronic Communications Regulations) is the UK law on marketing by email, text, phone and private message. The ICO, the UK data regulator, says "electronic mail" includes "direct messaging on social media (ie private messages)", as well as texts and voicemail (evidence, [ICO](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/guidance-on-direct-marketing-using-electronic-mail/key-concepts-for-direct-marketing-using-electronic-mail/)). What you may send depends on who receives it. Limited companies, LLPs and Scottish partnerships are "corporate subscribers": you may send them cold marketing if you say who you are and give an easy way to opt out. Sole traders and other partnerships count as individuals: you need their consent, or the "soft opt-in" (they bought something similar from you before and didn't opt out). If you are unsure, the ICO says treat them as individuals (evidence, [ICO B2B guidance](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/business-to-business-marketing/)). That guidance is "under review" after a 2025 law change, so check it again before training coaches (same source).

Two further laws apply. UK GDPR, the data protection law, covers any named person's details: you need a fair reason (usually "legitimate interests"), you must tell people where you got their details within a month, and you must stop the moment they object (evidence, [ICO B2B guidance](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/business-to-business-marketing/)). The Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 now names direct marketing as an example of a legitimate interest (evidence, [legislation.gov.uk, s.70](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2025/18/section/70)). The same Act raised the top PECR fine from £500,000 to **£17.5M or 4% of turnover from 5 February 2026** (evidence, [DLA Piper](https://privacymatters.dlapiper.com/2026/02/uk-commencement-of-the-data-protection-provisions-in-the-data-use-and-access-act/)), and messages now count as breaches even if they never arrive ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/data-use-and-access-act-2025-factsheets/data-use-and-access-act-factsheet-pec-regulations)). Every recent fine targets high-volume calls and texts to consumers, such as £300,000 for unwanted texts ([ICO](https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/media-centre/news-and-blogs/2026/06/manchester-firm-fined-300-000-for-bombarding-people-in-debt-with-over-55-million-unlawful-texts/)) and £160,000 for a firm that bought phone data without checking do-not-call lists ([ICO](https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/media-centre/news-and-blogs/2026/05/glasgow-based-energy-company-fined-160-000-for-making-unsolicited-marketing-calls/)). For a coach writing to limited companies by hand, the realistic risks are complaints and spam folders, not fines (inference). Buying unscreened phone lists is exactly what gets fined.

| Channel | To a limited company | To a sole trader or partnership | Platform rule on top |
|---|---|---|---|
| Email | Allowed with your name and an opt-out | Needs consent | Gmail and Outlook expect email security settings (SPF, DKIM, DMARC) |
| Instagram or Facebook DM | Allowed with your name and an opt-out (but see below) | Needs consent | Instagram: one text until accepted. Facebook: a Page can't start chats |
| WhatsApp | Allowed by PECR with your name and an opt-out | Needs consent | **WhatsApp requires opt-in from everyone** |
| Text message | Allowed with your name and an opt-out | Needs consent | None found |
| Phone call | Allowed if the number isn't on the CTPS list | Allowed if not on the TPS list | Show your number |
| Letter | Outside PECR | Outside PECR | UK GDPR still applies |

Calls need screening against both do-not-call lists: TPS (the Telephone Preference Service, for individuals and sole traders) and CTPS (the corporate version), and the caller must show their number (evidence, [ICO live calls](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/guidance-on-direct-marketing-using-live-calls/how-do-we-comply-with-the-rules-on-live-marketing-calls/)). Post sits outside PECR, though UK GDPR still applies to letters to named people ([ICO B2B guidance, Welsh mirror](https://cy.ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/business-to-business-marketing/)).

Two grey areas matter for Paul in particular. First, it is unclear who the "subscriber" is for a social media account. A DM to a gym company's own business account looks like emailing the company, but a DM to the owner's personal profile looks like messaging an individual (inference; no ICO example found). Second, many small gyms and studios are sole traders or partnerships, so a cold DM, email or WhatsApp to them needs consent. Matching each venue to Companies House settles this, and at Paul's £1M and 11-staff floor most venues will be companies (inference). A director's mobile is risky too: the ICO's "if unsure, treat as individual" rule makes texting or WhatsApping one without consent a bad idea (inference from ICO guidance).

The platforms add their own rules. WhatsApp's business policy says you may contact people only if "they have given you their mobile phone number" and you have "opt-in permission", and it warns that "messaging people at scale in an unauthorized manner" can mean a permanent ban (evidence, [WhatsApp Business Messaging Policy](https://whatsappbusiness.com/en-gb/policy/)). Meta publishes no daily DM limits; blocks follow behaviour such as evenly timed actions, identical text, and messaging straight after following (vendor claim, [ChatPlace](https://blog.chatplace.io/en/instagram-limits/)). Gmail has rejected unauthenticated bulk mail more firmly since November 2025, and Outlook.com since May 2025 (evidence, [Google](https://support.google.com/a/answer/14229414?hl=en-GB); [Microsoft](https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/blog/microsoftdefenderforoffice365blog/strengthening-email-ecosystem-outlook%E2%80%99s-new-requirements-for-high%E2%80%90volume-senders/4399730)). Coaches send far below the 5,000-a-day "bulk" line, but those email security settings are now basic hygiene (inference). Finally, scraping tools warn that they return personal data and you need a legal basis to use it (evidence, [Apify](https://apify.com/compass/crawler-google-places)). Meta's own terms on scraping were not checked. A UK-qualified adviser should review the playbook before BCA trains coaches on it.

## The app has most of the parts, but the joins are missing

The Profit Coach app is much further along than "LinkedIn only" (evidence, app code review, 21 Nov). Coaches can connect six account types through Unipile: LinkedIn, WhatsApp, Instagram, Messenger, Gmail and Outlook. The Conversations inbox reads and replies on all of them, and coaches can record and send voice notes and video from the inbox on any of these channels. Campaigns already have steps for Instagram DMs, likes, comments and follows, Messenger DMs, WhatsApp messages and emails. Google Maps and Google Search imports are built, open to coaches, and available to Ava. BOSS Score report emails already go out from the coach's own mailbox.

The gaps sit in the joins:

| Gap | What it means |
|---|---|
| Replies probably don't stop the sequence on Instagram, Messenger or WhatsApp | The campaign senders save each chat's ID in a different place from where the reply checker looks. So an owner who replies "no thanks" may still get the next step. This is untested but likely, and it is the most urgent fix: it harms trust and breaks the absolute right to object. |
| Facebook runs on the old Unipile version, where Unipile no longer maintains Messenger | Fixing it means using Unipile v2 for Facebook. v2 also changes how LinkedIn works (Unipile stops storing LinkedIn chats, so reading them counts against limits), WhatsApp accounts must reconnect, and webhooks (instant "something happened" alerts between systems) must be set up again ([Unipile limits](https://developer.unipile.com/v2.0/docs/provider-limits-and-restrictions); [Unipile account transfer](https://developer.unipile.com/v2.0/docs/transfer-accounts-subscription)). Running v2 for Facebook only, beside v1 for everything else, is the safe path; Unipile bills only the higher of the two account counts (same source). |
| A campaign can't start from an Instagram handle | Campaigns must be LinkedIn, email or WhatsApp. Anyone without a LinkedIn profile, email or phone gets skipped. |
| Instagram and Facebook links from Google Maps are hidden | The app stores them but doesn't show them, can't remove duplicates by them, and nothing ever fills a contact's Instagram link. So even the inbox's "start an Instagram chat" can't reach Maps prospects. |
| Daily limits are LinkedIn-shaped | Instagram follows count as LinkedIn invites, Instagram and Messenger DMs count as LinkedIn messages, and WhatsApp and email have no daily cap at all. |
| No guard for Instagram's one-message rule | A second Instagram step to a non-follower will probably fail. |
| No opt-out handling | The do-not-contact list covers only emails and LinkedIn links, and no campaign email carries an opt-out. |
| WhatsApp lacks safety checks | No check that a number uses WhatsApp before sending (Maps numbers are often landlines), no record that the owner said yes, and no STOP handling. |
| No Companies House import | Lists of limited companies can't come from Companies House, and the app can't tell a company from a sole trader, which decides what may legally be sent. |
| No cold email setup | Email goes from the coach's main mailbox, with no separate domains, warm-up, address checks, daily cap or threading. BCA's own research says never send cold email from the main domain. |
| Ava only plans LinkedIn | Her step writer can't write WhatsApp, Instagram or Messenger steps, and her automatic launch planner only builds Sales Navigator lists and LinkedIn connection campaigns, even for Paul's gyms. |
| "Ready to launch" checks look for LinkedIn | A coach running on Instagram or email looks unready. |

Voice notes inside Instagram and WhatsApp campaign steps are missing too, but this matters less than it seems. On Instagram a voice note can only follow a reply, and a reply stops the campaign, so the coach (or Ava) sends it from the inbox, which already works (inference).

## Conclusion and recommended approach

The weak point for coaches outside LinkedIn is the list and the law, and the app's sending side is ahead of its finding side. Instagram and Facebook can't replace Sales Navigator's volume: one coach account safely starts a few dozen conversations a day, and a stranger gets one text. So the winning pattern is fewer, better-chosen owners, each touched three or four ways, with the warmest touches (a reply, a group post, a referral) getting the most time. That also shapes any BCA-run lead generation: each coach needs their own aged accounts connected, because pooling volume through a few BCA accounts would pile the ban risk in one place (inference). BCA can still run the list-building centrally, since Google Maps, Companies House and scrapers need no coach login.

### Lead channels for each niche

| Niche | Lead with | Follow up with | Use with care | Leave for now |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Paul: sports, fitness and activity venues | Google Maps list checked against Companies House; Instagram warm-up and one opener; being visible in UK gym-owner groups and podcasts | Screened phone call, letter, email to limited companies, voice note after a reply, WhatsApp after a yes | Facebook DMs from his personal profile, 15 a day at most | Cold WhatsApp, cold texts |
| Bruce: food, drink and consumer brands | Companies House list; his LinkedIn campaign; letter to the named director, then email to the company | Instagram warm touch on the brand, founder's own account where linked; phone | Cold Instagram DMs at volume | Facebook outreach, cold WhatsApp |
| Other weak-LinkedIn niches (trades, salons, single-site hospitality) | The room where owners talk (Facebook groups, trade press) plus a Google Maps list | Phone, letter, WhatsApp after a yes | Cold DMs to sole traders | Bulk cold anything |

### A four-week pilot for Paul's venues

Before the pilot, ship items 1 to 3 of the build order below; until then, Paul runs Instagram by hand from his phone. In week 1, build the list: Google Maps searches for his venue types town by town, keeping venues with many reviews or more than one site, then match each to Companies House to keep limited companies that don't file micro-entity accounts and to get the directors' names (sports facility and fitness SIC codes such as 93110, 93120, 93130 and 93290; developer to confirm). Aim for about 200 named venues. In weeks 2 to 4, run the Instagram sequence from Paul's own established account at 15 to 20 openers a day, with phone and a letter for venues without an active Instagram, and email for limited companies once opt-out handling (item 2) is live. The offer is a BOSS Score or a two-minute review of the venue. Alongside, Paul joins two or three UK gym-owner groups as a helpful regular and pitches one podcast guest spot. Track which touch produced each reply. Benchmarks suggest about one booked call per 100 to 200 cold touches, so **beating one booked call per 100 venues means the approach is worth scaling** (inference).

### A four-week pilot for Bruce's brands

In week 1, pull about 200 brands from Companies House by hand or with a short script: active limited companies in food and drink making codes (SIC divisions 10 and 11) plus the wholesale and online retail codes many brands use when an outside factory makes their products, a few years old, not micro-entity, with directors named (inference; developer to confirm the code list). Find each website and Instagram account with the built Google Search import or by hand. In weeks 2 to 4, run three lanes on the same brands. Lane one is the LinkedIn connection campaign Ava already drafted for founders with profiles. Lane two is a short letter to the named director with a BOSS Score link, followed three days later by an email to the company, sent from a separate domain set up for Bruce (job 85); if that domain isn't ready, send letters only and hold email. Lane three is an Instagram warm touch on the brand, with an opener only where the founder runs or appears on the account. Measure replies and calls by lane, and judge with the same one-call-per-100 bar.
