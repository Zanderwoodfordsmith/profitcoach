### Issue 1: The 6 Cs of a listing that lasts

**SEO title:** The 6 Cs of a listing that lasts: a plan for UK food and drink founders
**SEO description:** Six questions to answer before you chase the next retailer, from a former MD of Montezuma's Chocolates.

---

In the summer of 2020, Tesco rang.

They wanted three of our chocolate products in their Christmas range. For a small business, that's the dream call. Of course we said yes.

We spent two months on the products, the packs, the price, the promotion and the volume. By 24 December we had sold about 75% of what Tesco had bought. Christmas lines don't stay on shelf into January, because the Easter eggs are coming in. So we paid for a 50% sale to clear the rest.

We made no money from the whole thing. It pulled us away from other buyers. And we weren't asked back the next Christmas.

That listing taught me more than any of the good ones. A listing is only worth what it leads to. Before you chase the next one, answer six questions. I call them the 6 Cs.

**1. Channels.** Supermarkets are one channel. So are discounters, convenience stores, wholesalers, farm shops and food service, from hotels to zoos. Most founders picture the shops they use themselves. Pick the channels that suit your product, then say no to the rest for now.

**2. Customers.** Inside each channel, who goes first? Tesco or the Co-op? Know why one comes before the other.

**3. Consumers.** The real work starts once you're on the shelf. You have about 12 weeks to show it sells. If it doesn't, it goes, and it's at least three years before that buyer looks at you again.

**4. Capacity.** Say you plan a promotion for a 25% lift and it does 100%. Have you got the ingredients and the packaging? Retailers hate empty shelves more than anything.

**5. Cash.** Add up the whole deal: the price, the promotion, the clearance at the end. Some listings cost you money.

**6. Case studies.** Every listing is proof for the next one. Start small, build a loyal base, and use the numbers to win the bigger buyer.

Over the next six issues I'll take one C at a time, with what I've seen work and what I've seen go wrong.

### Issue 2: Channels. Your shopping trip is a poor channel plan

**SEO title:** Which sales channels should a food or drink brand start with?
**SEO description:** Supermarkets are one channel of many. How UK food and drink founders choose the two or three that suit them, and say no to the rest for now.

---

Ask a founder where they want their product, and they'll usually name the shops they use. Tesco. Sainsbury's. Aldi. Waitrose. Maybe the local convenience store.

That makes sense. Your own shopping trip is your picture of the trade. But it's a small part of the map, and the map is where a sales plan starts.

**The channels, in plain words**

When I say channel, I mean a type of customer. Here are the main ones:

- **Superstores and supermarkets.** The big names. Huge reach, tough terms, and a buyer who wants proof.
- **Discounters.** Sharp prices and tight ranges. A good fit for some products, a margin trap for others.
- **Convenience.** Two very different worlds: symbol groups, where a group buys for many stores, and independents, who often buy store by store.
- **Wholesale.** Cash and carry, where shop owners come to buy, and delivered wholesale, which takes stock out to them.
- **Farm shops.** Small, loyal, and often the first place a premium product finds its fans.
- **Food service, or out of home.** Hotels, gyms, leisure attractions, even zoos. A big world with its own layers.

**Choose, then say no**

Most founders try to be in all of them. It feels like more chances. In practice it spreads a small team across buyers who all want something different: different packs, different prices, different service.

So pick two or three channels that suit your product, your price and your stage. Then say no to the rest, for now. No for now keeps the door open. It just means you won't start there.

A few questions help:

- Where does a shopper expect to find a product like yours?
- Which channel pays a price that leaves you a margin?
- Which one can you supply well with the team and the stock you have today?

**When the channels close**

At Montezuma's Chocolates, the shops closed for more than a year during Covid. The channels we relied on changed almost overnight. We grew revenue from £10m to £12.5m in 18 months because we moved the business to where shoppers still were. **[ask Bruce: which channels grew most for Montezuma's during Covid, and which shrank]**

That taught me that a channel plan is a choice you keep making, as the market moves.

### Issue 3: Customers. Tesco or the Co-op first?

**SEO title:** Which retailer should you pitch first? Choosing your first customer
**SEO description:** Inside every channel there's an order. Why the first buyer a food or drink brand wins matters more than the biggest.

---

Say you've decided supermarkets are right for your product. Good. Now the harder question: which supermarket first?

Do you start with Tesco, or with the Co-op? Waitrose, or Sainsbury's?

Most founders answer with whoever will take the meeting. That's understandable. A buyer saying yes feels like the hard part. But the first customer in a channel shapes everything after it. It sets your price. It sets your volumes. And it becomes the story you tell every buyer after that.

**Why the first customer matters most**

Your first listing in a channel is a test, in public. If it sells well, you have a case to take to the next buyer. If it struggles, that buyer hears about it too.

So the first customer should be the one where you have the best chance of selling through. That's often a smaller customer, with shoppers who already buy products like yours.

**What to weigh up**

When I look at the customers inside a channel, I want to know:

- **Their shoppers.** Do they already buy products at your price? A premium product in a store where shoppers hunt for value will struggle.
- **The size of the order.** Can you supply it well, every week, from day one?
- **The terms.** What does the listing cost you in promotions, fees and margin?
- **What it proves.** Will the numbers from this customer persuade the next one?

I've met founders who did brilliant work to win an early listing in Waitrose or the Co-op. There's a big celebration, and then the focus moves straight to the next one. "We've got Waitrose, let's go into Sainsbury's."

The listing you've just won needs your attention first. Which brings us to the next issue: what happens on the shelf.

### Issue 4: Consumers. The 12-week clock

**SEO title:** The 12-week clock: what happens after you win a retail listing
**SEO description:** A new listing has about 12 weeks to sell. Miss it and it can be three years before you're back. How food and drink founders get shoppers to try something new.

---

The day you win a listing feels like the finish line. It's the start.

Once your product is on the shelf, the clock starts. You have about 12 weeks to show the retailer it sells. If it doesn't, it goes. And once it's gone, it's at least three years before you have a real chance of getting back on that shelf, even with good numbers from elsewhere. Retailers won't get burnt twice.

**The problem nobody plans for**

Your product is on the shelf next to brands shoppers have bought for years. Nobody walking down that aisle knows who you are. They have no reason to pick yours up.

So the work after the listing is giving them one. Awareness. Interest. A reason to try it this week.

**What gets a new product tried**

Every product is different, but the plan behind a new listing usually covers:

- **Where it sits.** Is it at eye level, or on the bottom shelf? Is it with the products it should be compared to?
- **A reason to try.** A sharp price or a promotion for the first few weeks, planned and paid for before launch.
- **Your own customers.** If you have fans from farm shops, your own shops or online, tell them exactly where to find you now.
- **Your story near the shelf.** Why this product, why you, why now.

**[ask Bruce: one thing he put behind a new listing that got shoppers trying it, at Montezuma's or elsewhere]**

**Watch rate of sale from week one**

Rate of sale is how many units sell, per store, per week. It's the number the buyer watches. Get it every week and look at it every week. Don't wait for the buyer to tell you it's low.

If it's slow, you still have time to act. At week ten, you don't.

**Every listing is the next pitch**

When a listing works, its numbers become the case for the next one. When it fails, that story travels too. That's why the 12 weeks matter so much.

### Issue 5: Capacity. When the promotion works too well

**SEO title:** Can you supply the uplift? Promotions, capacity and empty shelves
**SEO description:** Plan a promotion for a 25% lift and it does 100%. What food and drink founders must check before they agree a promotion with a retailer.

---

Here's a problem most founders would love to have. It can still cost you the listing.

You've won a listing. Sales are steady, and you want more shoppers to notice you. So you agree a price promotion with the retailer. You plan for a 25% lift in volume and make enough stock to cover it.

Then it flies. 50% up. 100% up.

**Empty shelves**

If you can't make that much, the shelves go empty. Retailers hate empty shelves more than almost anything, because an empty shelf is lost sales for them.

So your promotion, the thing meant to build your brand, becomes a red flag on your account. And if they delist you, you're back to the three-year wait before they'll look at you again.

**What to check before you agree a promotion**

Making enough means having everything you need to get product onto shelves, not just the factory time. Before you sign up to a promotion, ask:

- **Ingredients.** If volume doubles, can you buy enough, in time, at a price that still works?
- **Packaging.** Packaging often has the longest lead times. Have you got enough cases, boxes and labels?
- **Making it.** Who makes it, you or a co-packer? How much more can they make, and how fast?
- **Getting it there.** Can your warehouse and delivery keep up with more orders, more often?
- **The people.** Who's watching the sales every day, so you can react while there's still time?

**Plan for the good outcome too**

Most plans are built for the expected case. Build one for the best case too. Know what you would do if the promotion did twice what you planned, and agree it with your suppliers before the promotion starts.

In my commercial roles at Nestlé, Lucozade Ribena Suntory and Kerry Foods, and then as an MD, I've seen how fast the sales plan can run ahead of the operation. The sales ambition only works if the operation behind it can deliver.

### Issue 6: Cash. The listing that cost us money

**SEO title:** The Tesco Christmas listing that made us nothing
**SEO description:** A dream call from Tesco, three Christmas products, and no profit at the end. How to add up a listing before you say yes.

---

In the summer of 2020, during Covid, Tesco rang Montezuma's. They wanted three of our products in their Christmas range.

For a small business, that's the dream call. Of course we said yes.

**What happened**

We spent the next couple of months on the products and the packs. We agreed the price, the promotional support and all the commercials. We agreed the volume Tesco would buy. We had a premium product, near the top of the prices in that part of the store.

Everyone was excited. Huge visibility, and a Tesco listing to take to every other retailer.

By 24 December, we'd sold about 75% of what Tesco had bought.

Christmas lines don't stay on shelf into January, because the Easter eggs are coming in. So the rest had to go. That meant agreeing a promotion to clear it. And clearing Christmas stock takes far more than 20% off. It was 50% off, and as the maker, we paid for it.

**What it cost**

When we added it all up, we made no money from the whole thing.

It cost us more than money. It pulled our attention away from other retailers. And because the performance was only okay, we weren't asked back the next Christmas.

**Add up the whole deal before you say yes**

A listing has more costs than the price you sell at. Before you agree one, add up:

- **The price** you sell to the retailer at, and the margin left after your costs.
- **The promotions** you're agreeing to fund, and how often.
- **What happens to what doesn't sell.** Seasonal lines especially. Who pays to clear it?
- **Your time.** What other buyers and channels will you stop working on while you deliver this one?

If the deal only works when everything sells, it's a risky deal.

**Grow profit with revenue**

Revenue growth feels good. But growth that doesn't show up in the bank balance just makes you busier. Every account should earn its place.

### Issue 7: Case studies. Every listing is a pitch for the next one

**SEO title:** Every listing is a case study: how small listings win big ones
**SEO description:** Start small, build loyal customers, and use the numbers to win the next buyer. How to turn each listing into proof, plus the full 6 Cs checklist.

---

When I joined Montezuma's Chocolates, we had a few farm shop listings and our own shops. Then we won a couple of products in Waitrose and Sainsbury's. Over the next 18 months, while the shops were closed, we grew revenue from £10m to £12.5m.

Each step made the next one easier, because each one gave us proof.

**Think of every listing as a case study**

Buyers take risks for a living, and they hate the ones that go wrong. The best way to make yes easy for them is evidence. Shoppers who buy it, then buy it again, with numbers that prove it.

So start where you can win and keep a loyal base. Then use what happened there to persuade the next, bigger buyer. It's a cycle. Bigger case studies justify bigger listings.

**Collect the proof from day one**

Don't wait until you need a case study to build one. From the first week of every listing, keep:

- **Rate of sale**, per store, per week, and how it compares with the products around you.
- **Repeat buying**, if you can see it.
- **What worked**: the promotion, the position, the launch plan.
- **Supply**: proof you delivered on time and in full, every week.

That last one matters. A buyer who sees you can keep the shelf full stops worrying about the order size.

**The 6 Cs, as a checklist**

That's the series. Before your next listing, run through all six:

1. **Channels.** Which kinds of shop suit you, and which will you say no to for now?
2. **Customers.** Inside that channel, which buyer first, and why?
3. **Consumers.** Why will a shopper pick it up in the first 12 weeks?
4. **Capacity.** Can you make enough if it does twice what you planned?
5. **Cash.** Does the whole deal make money, including what doesn't sell?
6. **Case studies.** What will this listing prove to the next buyer?

Get them right and each listing wins you the next one.
