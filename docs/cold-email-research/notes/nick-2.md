# Nick Saraev, Cold Email Copywriting & Outreach Full Course 2026 (part 2 of 2, 01:56 to 03:59)

Part 2 is mostly live teardowns of real cold emails, then: what you can tune on each platform, subject lines, follow-ups, testing, using AI, and "grey hat" tactics. The "four-step framework" comes from part 1. Here it's only mentioned by name, plus the idea that the reader's first question is "is this spam?" Every teardown scores emails against 7 levers: **give first, micro-commitments, social proof, authority, rapport, scarcity, shared identity**.

## 1. Core principles
- Every teardown scores the email on those 7 levers. Most bad emails score 0 or 1 out of 7. A rewrite aims to hit all 7 [02:05:01, 02:23:02, 02:28:01].
- Sound like one human writing to one human. Write "I", not "we": "I want to talk to you, Peter. I don't want to talk to your company" [02:25:02, 02:31:01]. Sign with a person's name, not "Team X" [02:28:01].
- Never present yourself as a bot or AI. It wipes out authority and shared identity [02:05:01].
- A vague offer kills the email. Make it concrete: a number, a time frame, a guarantee [02:35:00]. A complicated offer needs more lead-up, so a longer email is fine [02:36:00].
- Emails don't need to be long. His rewrites were longer only because the originals had no concrete offer in them [02:35:00].
- Specific beats generic. "Most creators lose viewers in the first 60 seconds" "reads like a TV commercial". "You're losing a tremendous number of viewers" talks to the reader directly [02:39:00].

## 2. Campaign structure
- Start with **2 emails only**: an opener plus one simple follow-up. One follow-up already puts you "ahead of 99.9%" [03:22:00].
- Add email 3, then 4, only once a campaign is clearly beating your normal results. Example: 4.8% replies with 2 emails, ~6.1% with 3, ~6.8% with 4. He would not add a step to a 2% campaign [03:25:00].
- Why start short: an untested long sequence gets more spam/block clicks and damages your mailboxes, phone numbers and profiles [03:23:00].
- Follow-ups should read like a real person's quick nudge, not a newsletter or case study. "Hey X, checking in on Y. TL;DR if this is the first time you're seeing this: [short restated body]. Thanks, Nick" / "Hey Pete, let me know if this got lost in your mailbox" [03:22:00, 03:26:00].
- In each follow-up, rewrite the opening of the first email and use a **different subject line**, so every follow-up also tests a subject [03:27:00].

## 3. Copy
- **Sender name:** use your full first and last name. It's the first thing the reader sees [02:53:02].
- **Subject + preview text:** about 148 to 150 characters show in the inbox before anyone opens it. Subject roughly 30 to 50 characters, preview roughly 50 to 100. Any space you leave empty fills with date junk, so the email body should be at least ~150 characters [02:55:00 to 02:56:00].
- **Subject lines don't sell.** Their only job is "plausible deniability": the reader can't tell if you're a seller, a friend or a buyer, so they open to find out [03:16:00 to 03:18:01].
  - Good: "Nick", "Nick, are you taking coaching clients?", "Nick, are you hiring?", "Nick's blind spot", "nick, you're wasting 2,300 per month" (lowercase, framed as a loss), "been with you from the start", "2026 capital", "your claude code course and a formatting thought" [03:15:00, 03:20:00 to 03:21:00].
  - Bad: "Interested in AI driven performance optimization", "Video editor", "Quick collab?" (the word "quick" is the problem), a long pitch like "Nick, you scaled to 72K a month with automation. Here's the next level.", or no subject at all (looks like spam) [03:14:00, 03:18:01 to 03:19:01].
  - Fear of loss beats promised upside [03:20:00]. Put a name or personal detail in the subject or the preview [03:21:00].
  - Small slips on purpose (two exclamation marks, all lowercase) make it look human [02:13:00, 03:19:01].
- **Opener:** a short personal line, e.g. "Love your design btw" [02:32:00]. Guessing well works too: "Watched your last [topic] video" applies to most people in a niche [01:59:00].
- **Offer:** use a guarantee or results-based terms. "I'd be willing to guarantee you 10K in the next 60 days or you don't pay" [02:34:02]. Size the promise to their revenue: "if they make 100K, I say I can make you 20" (~20% lift) [02:13:00]. "Mostly on commission" shows your incentives match theirs [02:11:00]. **Never put a price in a cold email** [02:05:01].
- **Name the doubt:** "I know this is the first you're hearing of me and I'm cognizant you may not be entirely comfortable with the prospect of working with a stranger…" [02:34:02, 02:36:00].
- **Call to action:** a short call with set times. "We'd only need to talk once for around 15 to 30 minutes… I'll give you a ring whenever is convenient today or tomorrow 3:30pm?" [02:34:02 to 02:36:00]. "Could show you in around 15 minutes, can call today around noon or tomorrow 3:30pm, or I'll send a Google Meet link" [02:11:00]. "Quick chat" alone is too vague and reads as a request for their time [02:44:00].
- **Free sample:** say exactly what they get and how little they have to do. "Free sample edit in around 48 hours. Just send me the raw footage in a Google Drive link" [02:41:00]. A 15 to 20 minute survey is a "scary commitment", not a small one [02:16:00].
- **Scarcity:** "Happy to do this for you anytime this week", "credits expire end of week", "in talks with a few other podcasts" [02:41:00, 02:21:00, 02:49:00].
- In a long email, put the offer near the top [02:46:00].

## 4. Targeting and lists
- **Pick a big enough market.** "Holistic nutritionists in Texas" gives a few hundred leads, not even one good test. "Digital agencies in the US" (~100k) gives 100 to 200 tests [03:32:00].
- **Clean scraped fields.** Use only the first word of a scraped name ("Nick Automates" becomes "Nick") [01:59:00]. Never put quotes or bold around a merged-in field, because it shows it was scraped [01:58:01].
- **Casual company names:** "Leftclick Incorporated" becomes "Leftclick". "The Pacific Creative Group LLC" becomes "PCG" [03:43:00 to 03:45:02]. Do the same for neighbourhoods ("Vancouver, BC" becomes "East Van") and schools [03:46:01].
- Lead sources mentioned: Airscale, LinkedIn Sales Navigator, Vayne (spelled "Vein.io" in the captions), custom directories, conference lists [03:47:01].

## 5. Deliverability and infrastructure
- **No links in cold emails.** Gmail and Outlook treat links as a phishing sign, and they "tank deliverability over time" [02:05:01 to 02:06:00, 02:22:00]. Drop links from the signature too, and leave out the email address [02:06:00].
- Spam and block clicks are what you're managing. Keep sequences short until the copy works [03:23:00].
- Pre-warmed mailboxes (Instantly and Smartlead sell them) skip the "21-day wait" for warm-up. People buy 50 to 100 at a time, often under made-up sender names. He calls this grey hat [03:50:01 to 03:51:00].
- Don't send on Sundays. He uses Sunday for making changes [03:29:01].

## 6. Numbers and benchmarks
- Test at least **500 to 1,000 sends per version** before picking a winner. 50 to 100 sends is "laughable" as evidence [03:31:00 to 03:32:00].
- A typical week of changes moves replies from 2.5% to 3.2%, to 3%, to 4%, then it levels off [03:27:00].
- Reply rates by sequence length: 4.8% with 2 emails, ~6.1% with 3, ~6.8% with 4. He wouldn't add steps to a 2% campaign [03:25:00].
- LinkedIn: 100 to 200 connection requests a week, more with Premium [03:49:01].
- Cold calling without a power dialer: only ~20% of your time is spent talking to decision makers. A dialer gives about 2.5x [03:53:00 to 03:54:00].

## 7. Mistakes to avoid
- Scraped names used as-is ("Hi Nick Automates", "Hi Stacked Podcast") [01:56:02, 02:42:01].
- Playing down your proof ("small AI team"). Make it as big as is true [02:16:00].
- Too many people in one email (the founder, a co-founder, "on behalf of"). It confuses the reader [02:44:00].
- Text crammed into a block, odd capitals, "We support companies refine their value proposition" (PhD words for a grade-3 reader) [02:34:00, 02:39:00].
- Picking a winner on too little data, or changing things daily for a week and then stopping [03:30:01, 03:31:00].

## 8. Small tricks that make a big difference
- Make changes once a week on the same day (his is Sunday, 20 to 30 minutes) and log each change like an experiment. That's 45 to 50 rounds a year [03:29:01 to 03:30:01].
- **Big changes early, small changes late.** Start with two very different emails (very short vs. long and personal, formal vs. casual). Each round, make the difference smaller ("3x, 2x, 1x, 0.5x") until you're only changing a word or the subject [03:33:00 to 03:35:00].
- Use AI only for **one small slot inside a template you know works**: e.g. "Hey {name}, love your {channel/LinkedIn posts}, man. Big fan, has made me think a lot about {topic}", or the casual company name [03:40:00 to 03:43:00].
- Give something first that feels made for them, even if it's a template (e.g. "a couple of thumbnail examples for you") [02:01:01].

## 9. Tools mentioned
- **Instantly**: built-in A/B testing, pre-warmed mailboxes [03:29:01, 03:50:01]. **Smartlead** ("Smartle"/"Smartly" in the captions): the same [03:29:01, 03:50:01]. **HeyReach**: LinkedIn outreach with testing [03:29:01].
- **Airscale, LinkedIn Sales Navigator, Vayne**: building lead lists [03:47:01].
- **Claude Code**: skills that tidy company names, an "auto research" loop for testing, counting characters [03:30:01, 03:45:02, 03:11:00].
- **Loom**: a personal video in the email (better as a link, not embedded) [02:42:01]. **Google Meet**: suggested in the call to action [02:11:00].
- **Power dialers**: cold calling. Automatic voicemail drops are regulated [03:53:00, 03:56:01].
- LinkedIn Premium badge and certificates on your profile help messages get read and keep you out of the "Other" inbox [03:03:00, 03:13:02].

## 10. UK/GDPR/PECR, free calls and audits, service businesses
- **Nothing UK, GDPR or PECR-specific.** He only says that grey hat tactics break laws and platform rules, that people have had accounts banned and received "warning letters", and that cold SMS and WhatsApp are "extremely regulated". He would avoid them [03:47:01 to 03:57:00].
- **For a free call or audit:** the free sample ("free sample edit in ~48 hours") counts as giving first. Make the call length exact (15 to 30 min) and "only once". Offer set times. Name the "you don't know me" worry. Size the promised gain to their revenue (~20%). Back it with a guarantee or results-based terms [02:11:00, 02:34:02 to 02:41:00]. "Quick chat" with nothing given scores near zero [02:44:00].
- **For selling a service:** the advisory-firm example ("Morningside Advisors") fits Profit Coaches closely. His fix: one plain question, a short personal line, one similar client with a number ("XYZ Marketing, 5M/yr… generated around $50,000 in 3 weeks"), the method in plain words ("figure out your CAC, LTV, conversion drop-offs, then fix the leaks"), then a guarantee and a 15 to 30 minute call [02:29:02 to 02:37:01].
- A case study is easier to put together than people think. Past experience can be shaped into one "with a couple of hours of work" [01:56:02]. **Caution:** this, the made-up sender names and the "pretend it's your secretary" advice [03:51:00] are risky for honest UK B2B selling.
